# SMB Beginner's Guide: The 4-Step Swing Trading Process

## Overview

A structured 4-step framework for traders transitioning into swing trading, designed to help beginners build a swing trading practice using their existing day trading skills. This process teaches you to systematically increase position size, identify trade setups with defined criteria, and manage trades with documented rules.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are a day trader looking to expand into swing trading
- When you want to capture multi-day to multi-week moves from trades you already take intraday
- When you need a systematic approach to position sizing and trade management
- When learning to identify trade setups with defined criteria (e.g., specific moving average alignments, ATR thresholds, volume levels relative to average daily volume) in markets meeting measurable environment metrics: breadth (percentage of stocks advancing versus declining), risk appetite (rotation between offensive/risk-on sectors and defensive sectors), and market behavior (tracking whether breakouts above resistance succeed or fail, and how stocks react to positive or negative news)
- When you want to avoid beginner mistakes like over-leveraging or exiting winners before capturing the full daily chart move

## Steps

### Step 1: Review Your Biggest Winners

Go back through your trading history and identify your trades with the highest gain relative to initial risk, not just swing trades but all winners. Examine the charts on a daily timeframe and ask: could any of these day trades have been swing trades? Look for trades with contextual factors including alignment across weekly, daily, and hourly timeframes (e.g., the stock's 10-day and 20-day moving averages trending higher on the daily chart, and price holding above the 200-day moving average on the weekly chart), catalysts (such as an earnings release or sector-wide narrative), and day-one breakout patterns (opening above the prior day's high with volume above the stock's average daily volume). Document the characteristics that made these trades work so you can build a documented set of rules.

### Step 2: Build Experience With Small Position Sizes

Do not jump into your standard day trade position size for swing trades immediately. Start by holding 5% of your existing day trade position overnight to gain experience with overnight risk. Use the MCR (Momentum-to-Core) ratio to predetermine how much of each position you will sell into strength (the "momentum" portion) versus hold for the swing (the "core" portion). For example, with a 100-share position at a 60/40 MCR ratio, you would sell 60 shares into strength on day one and hold 40 as a core swing position. Scale up by 5-share increments (for example, 5, then 10, then 20 shares per 100-share position) as you gain experience with overnight holding.

### Step 3: Learn Your Setups

Swing trading is a trading style, not a specific strategy. Focus on mastering one setup at a time rather than overwhelming yourself with multiple strategies. Key setups discussed include:

- Consolidation breakout: A stock in a sector that is outperforming the broader market (showing relative strength versus the S&P 500 ETF (SPY) or the relevant sector ETF), with a multi-week to multi-month consolidation base, converging 10-day and 20-day moving averages within a tight price range, and energy compression building from the consolidation. Enter when price closes above the consolidation high with volume exceeding the stock's average daily volume, and confirm alignment across the weekly, daily, and hourly timeframes (e.g., the 10-day SMA above the 20-day SMA on the daily, and price above the rising 200-day SMA on the weekly chart).
- Mean reversion: A stock in a rising trend (its 200-day simple moving average is trending upward, with each period's 200-day SMA value rising) that disconnects 7 to 12 ATRs from its 50-day moving average (the exact threshold varies by trader), with a greater distance from its 20-day, 10-day, and 5-day simple moving averages. Look for volume expansion and range expansion over 2 to 3 trading days signaling an extreme, accelerated move disconnected from the moving averages, then sell into strength toward the 5-day SMA.
- Continuation trade: A stock that has already broken out of a consolidation and is pulling back to its rising 10-day or 20-day moving average or to its prior breakout price level. Enter when it closes above the prior day's high or prior week's high in the same trading session, confirming a higher low within a developing uptrend (the most recent pullback low is above the prior pullback low).

For each setup, write down every variable that influences the setup, back test them, and practice identifying them in real time before trading with real capital.

### Step 4: Have a System to Manage the Trades

Each setup requires a different trade management system. Determine your stop loss and profit targets before entering. For mid to large-cap stocks, trail your stop loss against the prior trading day's low, the 10-day SMA, or the 20-day SMA, or to the low of the consolidation, depending on where you entered, the setup type, and where the stock is within its individual trend cycle (early-stage uptrend versus extended-stage uptrend). For small-cap or low-float stocks, use multi-day VWAP (volume-weighted average price calculated from day one of the trade) or key horizontal support and resistance levels instead of the prior day's low, because prior day lows for small-cap long positions can fall 50% to 70% below the entry price, making them too wide to use as stops. For swing trades triggered on an earnings day-one move, place your initial stop loss at the anchored VWAP from the earnings release. Adjust your management based on market trend stage: use wider stops and longer time horizons when the market is in an early-stage uptrend (a market that has recently reclaimed key moving averages after a brief pullback), but use tighter stop losses and exit by the second trading day after entry when the market is extended (has trended higher for several weeks with narrowing breadth and failing breakouts) and you are buying breakouts in the later stage of a move (after the market has trended higher for several weeks and breakouts are failing).

## Examples

### Example 1: AMD Day Trade That Became a Swing Trade

A trader bought AMD intraday on a first-day breakout above resistance and exited the same day. Upon reviewing the chart later, they realized the stock never revisited their entry price and went on to run for two additional weeks. This illustrates how a day trade with contextual factors including higher time frame technicals and a catalyst could have been held as a swing trade for larger gains.

### Example 2: ARM Consolidation Breakout

ARM formed a 2-year base on the weekly chart, broke out above 190 in April, then consolidated for close to 3 weeks with a tightening price range (each week's range smaller than the prior week) and converging 10-day, 20-day, and 100-day moving averages. The 200-day remained in a rising trend (each period's moving average value rising). The breakout on May 20 featured above-average volume, extension above the stock's all-time high, and no pullback. Entry was staged: an initial partial position above 230, then the full planned position size added on the actual breakout above 240 with a stop at the day's low price. The setup was graded A- (on a scale from C to A+) due to market environment (the first pullback in QQQ / Invesco QQQ Trust reclaiming), leading sector (semis), and impending NVIDIA earnings catalyst.

### Example 3: ONDS Breakout With Mixed Grade

ONDS gapped up near a resistance level established over a multi-month consolidation with a catalyst that was not yet proven groundbreaking. However, the sector lacked broad bullish sentiment and had not seen recent explosive price moves (stocks in the sector had not made sustained moves of 50% or more over recent weeks, indicating limited institutional participation). Graded B+ (on a scale from C to A+), the trader entered long but trimmed when the stock extended by one 14-day ATR (the ATR value was exactly 1 point at the time), holding a core position with a stop at the day's low price and planning to trail the stop against the prior day's low the following day. The plan was to lock in profits and hold the remainder for two to three additional trading days of upside or stop out.

### Example 4: Enphase Continuation Trade

After Enphase ranked as the top-performing stock in the market on day two of a multi-day move (the day after a day-one catalyst breakout, highest relative strength score among large caps), it pulled in quickly to the 170 level intraday and reclaimed the 170 level in the same trading session. The trader bought the dip with a tactical 1-2 day hold (exit by the second trading day) rather than a multi-week swing entry, selling everything the following day, May 5, on a green candle. This demonstrates that not every continuation trade is meant to be held for weeks; the time frame depends on the market conditions and the entry criteria.

## Best Practices

- Use the MCR (Momentum-to-Core) ratio to predetermine position allocation before day one ends, which eliminates FOMO
- Start with one backtested setup and master it before adding new strategies
- Size to at least 5% of your normal day trade size (start at 5% and scale up to 20%) when transitioning to swing trading to build confidence without risking capital you cannot afford to lose
- Assess the market environment across three categories: breadth (the percentage of stocks advancing versus declining on a given day — surges in breadth at the start of a new uptrend signal a genuine move; deterioration at market tops can foreshadow a reversal), risk appetite (rotation between risk-on sectors such as technology and risk-off sectors such as health care or consumer staples — a useful barometer is whether the S&P 500 ETF (SPY) and Nasdaq-100 ETF (QQQ) are outperforming or lagging defensive sector ETFs), and market behavior (tracking whether breakouts above resistance succeed or fail, and how individual stocks respond to positive or negative news — in some markets good news is sold, in others good news drives prices higher).
- Let the market condition metrics dictate your activity: when market breadth is narrowing, risk assets are underperforming defensive sectors, and breakouts are failing (stocks breaking above resistance then falling back below), reduce trading activity
- Place stops at the current trading day's low, consolidation low, 10-day SMA, or 20-day SMA and resist the urge to tighten stops based on intraday price movement
- In markets that have trended higher for several weeks with narrowing breadth, use tighter stop losses on new entries and cut losses within one to two trading days (exit by the second trading day after entry)

## Keep In Mind

- Swing trading is a style, not a specific strategy. Do not confuse the two.
- Not all swing setups are equal. A consolidation breakout in a sector outperforming the broader market, with institutional buying interest (evidenced by volume on the breakout day exceeding the stock's average daily volume and price holding above the consolidation high, as seen in the ARM trade), carries different expected value and management rules than a mean reversion trade in a sector with highly speculative trading behavior and elevated short interest.
- The market condition metrics matter more than exact entry prices. K-Fits philosophy: when market breadth is expanding, risk assets are leading defensive sectors, and breakouts are working, you do not need exact entry prices; when market breadth is narrowing, risk assets are lagging defensive sectors, and breakouts are failing, no entry is good enough.
- K-Fits' "last in, first out" rule: manage recent positions by cutting them faster and using tighter stops. Positions bought later in a trend should be cut within one to two trading days, while positions established at the start of a move can be held longer. Market stage dictates this: in an early-stage uptrend, hold positions established at the start of a move for the full trend; in an extended market stage, cut new entries within one to two trading days.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Avoid trading on margin if you are new to holding positions overnight. A 2% single-day decline in the S&P 500 can trigger margin calls that erase a month of gains.
- Never risk more than you are willing to lose on any single trade.
- Back test each setup over at least 200 trades and practice identifying them in real time using a demo account before trading with real capital. Only risk real capital once your demo results show a positive expectancy and consistently profitable outcomes.
- Be aware of overnight risk: earnings reports, gaps, and news can create opening moves against your position.
- If adverse news occurs overnight, honor your stop loss and do not hope for a recovery.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Believing swing trading is a specific strategy rather than a style that encompasses multiple setups
  **Solution:** Understand that swing trading refers to the time horizon (holding multiple days to multiple weeks), and you must define your specific setups and rules for each.
- **Problem:** Reviewing winners but failing to act on the insight that they could have been swing trades
  **Solution:** When you identify a winner that ran longer than your day trade, intentionally design a swing trading version of that setup with clear hold rules.
- **Problem:** Starting with full position size and experiencing large overnight drawdowns
  **Solution:** Begin with 5% of your normal day trade size and scale up to 20% in 5% increments as you build experience with overnight holding.
- **Problem:** Using the same stop and management rules for every setup regardless of context
  **Solution:** Differentiate between intraday momentum scalps and true swing trades. For small caps, use multi-day VWAP or key horizontal support and resistance levels; for mid to large caps, trail against the prior trading day's low or against a moving average that is trending upward (each period's moving average value rising over time).
- **Problem:** Getting stubborn and refusing to exit when the market becomes extended (markets that have trended higher for several weeks with narrowing breadth) and new entries reverse back below the entry price
  **Solution:** Adopt a tight stop loss mentality in extended markets. Cut losses within one to two trading days (exit by the second trading day after entry) on new breakouts that fail (stocks breaking above resistance then falling back below), even if earlier positions in the trend are still working.
- **Problem:** Overwhelming yourself with too many setups at once
  **Solution:** Master one setup completely before expanding your documented set of trade rules. Add new strategies in 5% increments, just as you would scale position size.
