# Do Not Buy A House! Do THIS Instead - Morgan Housel

## Overview

Morgan Housel, author of The Psychology of Money, explains that wealth is not about spending money on status symbols—it is about the money you do not spend. True financial freedom comes from independence, not from owning a big house or a fancy car. This teaching distills his core advice: save at least 20% of gross income, invest in low-cost index funds, understand how returns on prior returns accelerate wealth over decades, keep housing and lifestyle expectations at or below 150% of your current income, and treat a house as a lifestyle decision rather than a get-rich-quick investment.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are deciding whether to buy or rent a home
- When you want to build sustainable, long-term wealth
- When your spending habits are driven by trying to impress others
- When you feel stuck in a low-income job and need a path forward
- When you are choosing how to allocate your investments for the next 10+ years
- When you are tempted to pick individual stocks instead of using index funds

## Steps

### Step 1: Separate "Rich" from "Wealthy"

Define rich as having enough income to buy what you want. Define wealth as the money you did not spend, saved, and invested. Wealth is hidden. It buys independence and autonomy. Focus on building unspent money, not visible status.

### Step 2: Keep Your Expectations Below Your Income

If your lifestyle spending increases faster than your annual income growth, your savings rate will remain near zero and financial independence will be delayed indefinitely. The first rule of financial happiness is to keep essential and discretionary spending below income growth by at least 10 percentage points. Stop comparing your possessions to others; behavioral research shows that observers evaluate your choices far less critically than you expect. Replace external validation with measurable progress toward your own net-worth targets.

### Step 3: Treat Savings as Buying Your Future Time

Every $1,000 in invested savings represents approximately $40 in annual passive income at a 4% safe withdrawal rate. This accumulated capital buys optionality: the ability to choose where you live, what job you accept, and when you retire. The path to that optionality is earned income minus spending converted into invested assets, not upgraded possessions. Lack of financial control correlates with elevated cortisol, higher anxiety, and increased risk of stress-related illness per multiple peer-reviewed studies.

### Step 4: Invest in Low-Cost Index Funds Through Dollar-Cost Averaging

Buy the same dollar amount of investments every month, regardless of market conditions. Use low-cost, diversified index funds. Endurance matters more than annual returns. Warren Buffett’s secret was not beating the market—it was staying invested for 80 years. 99% of his wealth was accumulated after age 60.

### Step 5: Prepare for Risk You Cannot See

Risk is what you have not thought of. Keep 3 to 6 months of essential expenses in a high-yield savings account and up to 12 months if your income is variable or your industry is cyclical. Invest in preparedness, not prediction. When the opportunity cost of holding cash visibly exceeds your desired return on safety, adjust to a standard target rather than an emotional threshold.

### Step 6: Do Not Buy a House Purely as an Investment

Buying a house to get rich is a bad financial decision. Over the last 150 years, U.S. home prices adjusted for inflation have been flat. If you are buying because you need stability, kids, or lifestyle reasons, that is fine—but do not mark it as a wealth-building strategy. If you are buying purely to make money, run for your life.

### Step 7: Accept That Making Money and Keeping Money Require Opposite Skills

Income growth requires calculated risk and optimism in career and business opportunities; wealth preservation requires risk aversion and humility in portfolio management. These are distinct skill sets that most people do not master simultaneously. Apply optimism to career income and business risk-taking; apply conservatism to asset allocation, spending, and debt.

## Examples

### Example 1: The Janitor Who Left $8 Million

Ronald Reed was a janitor who mopped floors at a gas station. He dollar-cost averaged into stocks for 70 years, never sold, and died with an $8 million net worth. He had no pedigree, no financial background, and no special skills—only endurance. Most people cannot replicate his lifestyle, but the strategy is available to everyone.

### Example 2: Warren Buffett’s Endurance

Warren Buffett is worth over $100 billion. His secret was not stock-picking genius; it was starting at age 11 and never stopping. 99% of his wealth came after his 60th birthday. If you earn average market returns and stay invested for 40–50 years, you will end up in the top 3% of professional investors.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Save at least 20% of gross income before discretionary spending; each dollar invested compounds into future optionality
- ✅ Dollar-cost average into low-cost index funds every month without exception
- ✅ Keep lifestyle inflation below income growth by at least 10 percentage points and decouple happiness from income growth by measuring progress against personal goals rather than social comparisons
- ✅ Treat your house as a lifestyle purchase, not an investment
- ✅ Maintain 6 to 12 months of essential expenses in liquid savings
- ✅ Establish quarterly accountability with one trusted person who reviews your major financial decisions and challenges your assumptions
- ✅ Acknowledge that market forecasts have consistently failed; rely on historical averages and a margin of safety instead of predictions

## Keep In Mind

- Wealth equals income minus spending; it is the unconverted portion of earnings that remains invested
- Happiness correlates with the gap between desires and consumption, not absolute income level
- The hardest financial skill is maintaining a constant savings rate as income rises rather than upgrading lifestyle spending to match higher earnings
- Wealth accumulation is gradual and compounding; losses from leverage, fraud, or panic selling can erase years of gains in a single day
- You need a minimum level of productive challenge to stay engaged in life, but chronic financial anxiety reduces cognitive performance and decision quality
- After reaching a financial milestone, your original motivation may fade; plan for post-goal direction by setting new non-monetary objectives
- The cost of holding wealth is enduring drawdowns; expect at least three 20% to 40% market declines per decade and avoid selling during them

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never allocate capital based on predictions, forecasts, or someone else's story; use historical averages and a personal investment policy instead
- Avoid mortgage leverage on a primary residence if your only justification is expected price appreciation; treat the purchase as consumption
- Do not let peers, family, or media pressure you into lifestyle inflation that outpaces your income growth
- Keep an emergency fund equal to at least 6 months of necessary expenses, and 12 months if your income is variable

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Buying a house to get rich
  **Solution:** Only buy a house for documented lifestyle reasons such as school district quality, job stability, or family size needs; treat the purchase as consumption with an expected annual cost, not an investment with projected returns
- **Problem:** Raising expectations faster than income
  **Solution:** Practice gratitude and intentionally keep wants below means; behavioral research on social comparison shows that observers evaluate your possessions far less critically than you expect
- **Problem:** Trying to beat the market with stock picks
  **Solution:** Use low-cost index funds and dollar-cost average fixed dollar amounts monthly; endurance in holding through drawdowns beats stock selection skill in generating long-term returns
- **Problem:** Not saving because the amounts seem too small
  **Solution:** Small amounts compound over decades; $50 per month invested at a 7% annual return grows to approximately $40,000 over 30 years. Start now.
- **Problem:** Assuming past success guarantees future success
  **Solution:** Recognize the role of luck in outcomes; maintain humility by tracking your decisions and their results; use a trusted partner or fee-only advisor to challenge your major financial decisions quarterly
