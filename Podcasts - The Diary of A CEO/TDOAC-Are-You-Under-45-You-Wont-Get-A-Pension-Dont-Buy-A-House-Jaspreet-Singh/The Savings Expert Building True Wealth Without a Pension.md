# The Savings Expert: Building True Wealth Without a Pension

## Overview

Jaspreet Singh explains why traditional wealth-building advice—get a good job, buy a house, rely on a pension—is failing most people. With 78% of Americans living paycheck to paycheck, social security drying up, and pensions disappearing for anyone under 45, the old playbook no longer works. This teaching distills his core framework: understand how money actually works, buy income-generating assets instead of liabilities dressed up as assets, adopt the 75-15-10 money rule, and invest in yourself above all else. The goal is to reach a state where your investment cash flow exceeds your expenses—true financial freedom on your own terms.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are spending 90% or more of your monthly income with little or no savings
- When you are considering buying a house but cannot verify the purchase against the 75-15-10 rule or do not have the 20% down payment plus closing costs
- When you want to understand the difference between an asset and a liability
- When you are under 45 and worried about retiring without a pension or social security
- When you want to build passive income through real estate, stocks, or business ownership
- When you are ready to shift from "working for a salary" to "owning assets that pay you"

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand the Core Difference Between Wealthy People and Everyone Else

Wealthy people understand how money works. Everyone else does not. The educational system teaches you to study hard, get good grades, get a good job, and climb the corporate ladder. But wealthy people aren't climbing ladders—they're owning them. The three things that have built more wealth than anything else over the last century are: starting a business, investing in real estate, and investing in stocks. None of these are taught in school. You need to educate yourself outside the classroom through books, podcasts, and real-world experience. Jaspreet's recommended starter reading list includes: *Rich Dad Poor Dad* by Robert Kiyosaki, *The Total Money Makeover* by Dave Ramsey, and *The Creature from Jekyll Island* (about the Federal Reserve). Over 12 months, read 5 books on money management, 5 on personal development, 5 on starting a business, 5 on leadership, and 5 on scaling—you'll have an MBA-level education for a fraction of the cost.

### Step 2: Adopt the 75-15-10 Money Rule Immediately

For every dollar you earn, allocate it as follows: 75 cents maximum for spending (mortgage, food, lifestyle, everything), 15 cents minimum for investing, and 10 cents minimum for saving. No exceptions. If you make $100,000 a year, that means a maximum of $75,000 for all your expenses combined. If your mortgage and lifestyle costs exceed that, you cannot afford that house. The key behavior shift: always invest and save first, then spend whatever is left—not the other way around. This rule applies whether you make $10,000 a year or $10 million a year. If you don't know your exact monthly spending over the last 6 months, you are not ready to buy a house or invest seriously. Track every dollar.

### Step 3: Escape the Financial Danger Zone with Drastic Action

If you do not have $2,000 saved for an emergency and you have credit card debt, you are in the financial danger zone and must make drastic changes today. Reduce discretionary spending to under 5% of your monthly income. Eliminate restaurant meals, vacations, and luxury purchases until your emergency fund exceeds $2,000 and all credit card debt is paid off. Sell things you own but don't need—a TV you're not using, a car you can't afford, a house that's too expensive. Downgrade your living situation. Redirect at least 2 hours per day currently spent on passive entertainment toward learning, side work, or business building. The goal is to stop the bleeding first—seal the holes in the boat before you try to row it anywhere. Banks, corporations, and the government all profit when you are financially illiterate. You must make yourself rich before you make them rich.

### Step 4: Break the Invisible Barriers Holding You Back

Your mindset is the first and most critical barrier to wealth. You must genuinely believe you can become wealthy—not "might," not "can," but "will." If you don't believe it, you will never achieve it. Jaspreet uses the "nine dots exercise" with every new employee: you must connect nine dots with four straight lines without lifting your pen. Most people fail because they create an invisible box around the dots. The solution is to extend your thinking beyond the perceived boundaries. The same applies to your financial life—growing up in poverty, being told you can't succeed, or lacking role models are all invisible barriers, not real ones. Find your "why"—a picture or reminder of who you're doing this for—and place it next to your desk. If your fuel is proving people wrong, use it. Toxic fuel still burns. The moment you break through one invisible barrier, you'll never be trapped by that same illusion again.

### Step 5: Buy Assets, Not Liabilities Disguised as Assets

Your primary home is not an asset—it is a liability. An asset puts money in your pocket. A liability takes money out. Your house costs you money every month via mortgage, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and upgrades. Even after you pay off the mortgage, your house doesn't generate cash flow. The myth that buying a home builds generational wealth is one of the biggest lies in personal finance. In a best-case scenario, you buy a $300,000 home, it grows to $1,000,000, and you leave it to your kids—but unless they have the income to cover property taxes, insurance, and maintenance on a $1,000,000 home, they will be forced to sell it. If they have no financial education, the million dollars will be spent within 20 years. Real generational wealth comes from assets that produce cash flow—rental properties where the rent covers all expenses and pays you, dividend-paying stocks, and businesses. When Jaspreet bought his first investment property at age 19, a foreclosure condo for $8,000, he rented it for $600/month. That condo paid him without him having to work for it.

### Step 6: Treat Your House as a Liability and Understand Opportunity Cost

If you do want to buy a house to live in, you must meet three criteria: afford the 20% down payment, afford the monthly payment within the 75-15-10 framework, and afford all moving and setup costs (movers, furniture, upgrades). A 20% down payment is non-negotiable—anything less means you have no real equity and no skin in the game. Understand opportunity cost: if you put $20,000 into a home's down payment, you lose the opportunity to invest that $20,000 in a rental property, the stock market, or a business. Before buying, ask yourself: am I ready, or should I build more wealth first? Also understand how banks front-load mortgage interest: on a 30-year mortgage paying $3,000/month, for the first ~15 years, roughly $2,900 of that $3,000 goes to interest and only ~$100 goes to equity. If you refinance before year 15, the clock resets. Your mortgage payment is a payment to your bank, not an investment in your future.

### Step 7: Build Your Five-Pocket Investment Portfolio

Jaspreet allocates his money across five buckets: (1) your own business (~40-50% of total net worth, cash-flow generating), (2) real estate (~50% of investment portfolio—single-family homes or multi-family apartments with a minimum 7% cash-on-cash return), (3) stocks (~30%, split evenly between individual company stocks and ETFs/index funds), (4) speculative assets (~18%, including cryptocurrency and startups—treat as speculative only, not as core wealth), and (5) physical gold (~2%, held as insurance against currency collapse, not as an investment, since gold produces no cash flow). Keep multiple cash buckets: an emergency savings fund for personal life, a separate business emergency fund, cash waiting to deploy into real estate, cash waiting for stocks, and cash for speculative opportunities. When investing in real estate, hire a property manager so your time is spent acquiring deals, not managing tenants.

### Step 8: Cultivate the Three Pillars of the Money Mindset

**Pillar 1: "I will become wealthy."** Say it as a statement of fact, not a hope. You have to believe you are worthy of more before you can achieve more. **Pillar 2: Money is a tool.** It does not make you good or bad—it amplifies who you already are. You need four types of fitness for a fulfilling life: physical, mental, spiritual, and financial. If you lack financial fitness, it will erode your physical fitness (can't afford healthy food or gym), mental fitness (financial stress causes anxiety and depression), and spiritual fitness (can't pay bills, you lose your sense of purpose). **Pillar 3: Money is abundant.** Scarcity thinking costs the average person thousands of dollars per year in higher prices, missed investment returns, and avoidable fees. There is a limitless amount of money in the world—the U.S. government alone has $35+ trillion in debt, meaning there is massive capital flowing everywhere. Don't think in terms of squeezing an extra $100 from your current salary; think in terms of building an additional $1,000-per-month income stream through a side business, rental income, or investments. Being cheap on things like hiring, accounting, or contractors costs far more in the long run than paying for quality upfront.

### Step 9: Take Full Responsibility for Your Financial Situation

The economic system is designed to profit from financially illiterate people—banks profit from your debt, corporations profit from your spending, and the government profits from your W-2 income taxed at the highest rates. Acknowledge this reality, then flip the script. It is your responsibility to become financially educated. You profit when you are educated. Taking personal responsibility means not blaming your employer, the government, your upbringing, or bad luck for where you are today. You may not be at fault for your starting position, but you are fully responsible for where you go from here. Developing an internal locus of control—believing your decisions shape your outcomes—is the single biggest predictor of future success. When your accountant makes a $118,000 tax mistake, the fault is yours for hiring cheap. When you get a bad tenant, the fault is yours for rushing the vetting process. Own every outcome.

### Step 10: Use the Tax Code and Wealth Protection Tools to Amplify and Protect What You Build

The tax code rewards investors, not employees. A CEO paying $8 million in cash compensation faces a 37% top federal tax rate. An investor like Warren Buffett making $700 million from dividends pays only 20%. Advanced investors use strategies like borrowing against appreciated assets (not selling them, thus avoiding taxable income) to access liquidity tax-free. The wealthy also invest heavily in wealth protection: a skilled accountant who does tax strategizing (not just tax filing), proper legal entity structures to shield assets, comprehensive insurance, and estate planning to control what happens to wealth after death. Generational wealth isn't just the money you leave behind—it's what that money does after you're gone, and you control that while you're alive. If you have under $250,000–$500,000 in assets, most financial advisors won't work with you—so self-education is not optional, it is essential.

### Step 11: Invest Consistently and Patiently for the Long Term

The stock market has returned an average of 10% per year over the last century. Household incomes grew ~18% from 2019–2024 while the S&P 500 grew nearly 100%. Over five decades (1971–2021), median household income grew ~600% while the S&P 500 grew ~4,000%. You cannot earn or save your way to wealth—you must invest your way there. Wealth is defined as: your investment cash flow exceeds your expenses. If your expenses are $4,000/month and your investments pay you $4,001/month, you are wealthy. This is not about retiring at 67 and doing nothing—it's about having options. If you invest $100/month from age 21 to 65 at a 10% return, you will retire a millionaire. Don't try to beat the market, pick hot stocks, or chase crypto moonshots. Invest boringly. Invest consistently. Stay in the market when it's up, down, or sideways. Like the tortoise, patience and boring consistency beats excitement and speed every time.

## Examples

### Example 1: The Foreclosure Condo—Starting with $8,000

At age 19, while studying for the MCAT in 2011, Jaspreet bought a foreclosed condo for $8,000 (it had sold for $150,000+ a few years prior). He put in a few thousand dollars for repairs and rented it out for $600/month. The rent paid for maintenance, taxes, insurance, and produced surplus cash flow. This single deal taught him the fundamental principle that assets pay you without you having to work for them—while liabilities like his car rims, sound system, and watches were making other people rich. This is accessible to anyone: you don't need millions. You need education, initiative, and the willingness to start small and be inconsistent.

### Example 2: The Friend Who Made a Million by Doing Nothing Special

One of Jaspreet's friends—who runs the smallest business, earns the least income, and lives in a studio apartment with no fancy car or clothes—has over $200,000 in liquid cash, more than all five of Jaspreet's other best friends combined. He achieved this in 4–5 years simply by not spending money. He didn't invest. He didn't make risky bets. He just saved. Meanwhile, Jaspreet's other friends earning five times as much per month have five times less cash. This illustrates that controlling your spending is the prerequisite for building wealth—without it, no investment strategy matters.

### Example 3: The Doctor Who Looks Rich but Is Broke

Jaspreet regularly hears from Indian-American professionals—doctors earning $200,000 to $400,000 per year—who haveRange Rovers, nice houses, and fancy vacations but zero savings and zero investments. They're living in a credit-based economy where higher income just means more credit and more spending. Following the 75-15-10 rule would fix this immediately: cap total spending at 75% of income, force 15% to investments, 10% to savings. The moment you start, wealth accumulation becomes automatic.

### Example 4: Hiring Cheap and Paying $118,000 for the Lesson

Jaspreet hired a cheap accountant who only filed taxes. In January, he got a call: wire $118,000 to the state and IRS by end of day, plus penalties and interest. He hired a premium accountant who costs 3 to 5 times more—but the tax strategizing they do now saves him more money than the cheap accountant ever cost. The lesson: one of the most expensive things you can do is be cheap. Recruit and hire exceptionally. A players hire A players. Quality compounds.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never invest money you cannot afford to lose into speculative assets like cryptocurrency—these should be a small portion of your portfolio and treated as lottery tickets, not wealth-building tools
- Always have proper insurance on rental properties and formal written contracts with every contractor, property manager, and tenant
- Do not lend against speculative assets or use leverage that exceeds 70% loan-to-value on any single investment or that requires monthly debt payments exceeding 30% of your monthly rental income
- Keep your emergency savings in liquid, accessible cash—do not lock it into investments or real estate you can't quickly sell
- When hiring professionals (accountants, lawyers, contractors), prioritize competence and references over cost—cheap mistakes cost more than quality fees
- Protect your wealth through proper legal entity structures, estate planning, and insurance before you accumulate significant assets

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Buying a house you can't afford and calling it an asset.
  **Solution:** Run the numbers through the 75-15-10 rule first. If your mortgage, taxes, insurance, maintenance, and lifestyle costs exceed 75% of your income, you can't afford it. Treat your home as a liability.

- **Problem:** Using credit cards and lifestyle inflation to look rich while being broke.
  **Solution:** Stop spending money on status symbols—such as luxury cars, designer clothing, or expensive vacations—to impress people who do not care about your financial situation.

- **Problem:** Trying to earn your way to wealth through salary alone.
  **Solution:** You cannot save or earn your way to wealth—you must invest your way there. Investment values grow ~5x faster than incomes. Start investing now, even with small amounts.

- **Problem:** Getting caught up in get-rich-quick schemes when emotionally desperate.
  **Solution:** Until you master your mindset and stop seeking dopamine hits from spending or quick wins, you will be a target for scams. Fix your financial foundation before chasing speculative investments.

- **Problem:** Hiring the cheapest option and hoping for the best.
  **Solution:** A cheap accountant cost Jaspreet $118,000. A bad contractor and property manager led to a lawsuit. One exceptional hire is worth many cheap ones. Always prioritize quality and do proper due diligence.

- **Problem:** Relying on social security and a pension for retirement.
  **Solution:** Social security is projected to be insolvent by 2034. Pensions are disappearing. If you're under 45, you almost certainly won't get one. Your own savings and investments are your only reliable retirement vehicle.

- **Problem:** Trying to time the market or pick individual winners.
  **Solution:** Most people who try to beat the market lose money. Invest consistently in low-cost index funds or ETFs regardless of market conditions. Boring, patient, long-term investing wins.

- **Problem:** Believing you can't build wealth because you don't come from money.
  **Solution:** Jaspreet started with $8,000. His friend built a million-dollar cash position in 4–5 years running a small business. You don't need rich parents, a million-dollar idea, or a six-figure salary. You need financial education and the discipline to start and stay consistent.

## Notes for Implementation

- The 75-15-10 rule is a framework, not a law. Adjust the percentages based on your income level, cost of living, and financial goals, but never drop below 10% saved and 15% invested.
- The five-pocket portfolio percentages are starting benchmarks. As your net worth grows, shift toward lower-risk assets.
- The $2,000 emergency fund threshold is a minimum. Aim for 3–6 months of expenses as your long-term target.
- The 7% cash-on-cash return is a baseline. In high-cost markets, accept 5–6% if the property has strong appreciation potential.
- The 20% down payment guideline reduces private mortgage insurance (PMI) and improves loan terms, but 15% may be acceptable if you have strong credit and low interest rates.
- Jaspreet's personal story and the examples in this teaching are for illustration. Your path may differ based on your location, income, and risk tolerance.
