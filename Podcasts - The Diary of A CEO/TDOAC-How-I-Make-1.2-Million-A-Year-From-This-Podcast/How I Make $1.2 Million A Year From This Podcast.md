# How I Make $1.2 Million A Year From This Podcast

## Overview

Stephen Bartlett turned The Diary of a CEO podcast from a bedroom experiment into a multi-million dollar business in under four years. This teaching distills the exact monetization and growth principles he used, including cutting out podcast ad middlemen, securing direct brand deals, and maintaining a consistent weekly release schedule while upholding a strict quality threshold for every episode.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When starting a podcast or content-based business and need to monetize it sustainably
- When building a personal brand or audience that you want to turn into revenue
- When you want to move beyond low-paying ad networks and secure high-value brand partnerships
- When choosing between broad reach and deeper audience connection

## Steps

### Step 1: Publish on a Reliable Cadence

Consistency creates compounding audience growth. Bartlett published episodes on an irregular schedule for three years and saw minimal growth, then committed to releasing every single Monday at 6 AM. That one decision drove a 10x increase in listeners over the next 12 months. Pick one day and time and show up without fail. For any content or business model, a fixed publication schedule builds listener habit and signals reliability to both audiences and sponsors.

### Step 2: Leverage Guests to Reach New Audiences

When you have no existing audience, your own voice alone will not scale. Bartlett invited guests onto the show, and each guest promoted the episode to their own social-media followers. This created exponential organic growth without relying on Spotify or Apple Podcasts' discovery algorithms. Guests also cut his episode preparation time from 7–8 hours per episode to roughly 2 hours, reducing production costs per episode by more than 70%.

### Step 3: Protect Quality Over Quantity

Bartlett has deleted full episodes—including one with a guest who had 30 million YouTube subscribers—because the conversation lacked actionable insight for his audience. Set a concrete value rubric before every recording, protect it aggressively, and only publish episodes you and your team would recommend to a close friend. That standard builds audience trust and reduces churn.

### Step 4: Cut Out the Middleman and Pitch Brands Directly

Traditional podcast ad networks pay roughly $25–$50 per 1,000 downloads and take a 20–40% cut. Bartlett bypassed them entirely. He made a list of five products he genuinely used every day, found the CEOs' email addresses through company websites and LinkedIn, sent a concise 2.5-slide pitch deck showing audience growth data, and asked them to sponsor the show. All five replied within two weeks. One of those sponsors (Huel) turned into a direct equity investment and a board seat for Bartlett.

### Step 5: Ask for Longer-Term Contracts

Every sponsor deal Bartlett secured is on a 12-month contract. This gives him predictable monthly revenue, stronger brand relationships, and the creative freedom to speak without weekly renegotiation. Long-term contracts also turn sponsors into repeat buyers rather than transactional advertisers.

### Step 6: Prioritize Depth Over Reach

Bartlett compared his viral Facebook videos (10M+ views each, generating brand awareness but few conversions) to his podcast episodes (smaller audience, but generating 100x more revenue per listener). He chooses podcasting because long-form conversations build lasting trust, behavioral change, and word-of-mouth referrals. Focus on revenue-per-listener and retention, not total download counts.

### Step 7: Build a Small, High-Trust Team Behind You

Bartlett runs an eight-person production team: one producer, one booker, one PR manager, two video editors, one social media manager, one business manager, and one executive assistant. The team handles all logistics, editing, and sponsor communication so he can focus on content creation and guest relationships. Delegating operational tasks preserves his autonomy and keeps the work feeling like a strategic asset rather than daily labor.

## Examples

### Example 1: Huel Direct Pitch

Bartlett had been a Huel customer for three years. He found CEO Julian Hearn's contact through Huel's website, sent a concise pitch showing podcast growth, and promised to make the show the #1 business podcast in Europe within 12 months if Huel backed him. Julian called the next day. Six months after the sponsorship began, Bartlett invested an undisclosed amount in Huel and joined its board—all because he went direct instead of through an ad network.

### Example 2: Fiverr Partnership

Bartlett had used Fiverr across three portfolio companies for design, video, and audio production. He messaged the global marketing director on LinkedIn—someone he had never spoken to before—shared podcast stats and content plans, and secured a six-figure annual sponsorship. No middleman, no scripted ad reads—just an authentic alignment between brand and audience.

### Example 3: My Energy Renewable Energy Sponsorship

Bartlett reached out to Jordan Brampton at My Energy because their sustainability mission matched his values. After a 30-minute Zoom call, they signed a 12-month sponsorship contract. Over the following 18 months, the relationship expanded into a strategic partnership where My Energy also sponsors live events. This deeper involvement was driven by authentic alignment and repeated performance rather than a one-off ad buy.

## Best Practices

- Commit to one publish day and time and never break it
- Pitch brands you genuinely use and love—authenticity produces higher conversion rates than cold outreach to irrelevant products
- Get 12-month contracts so you can plan and forecast revenue ahead
- Delete episodes that do not meet your quality bar, even if it costs you a big-name guest
- Build guest relationships that can turn into genuine friendships and business partnerships
- Choose revenue-per-listener and retention over total download counts
- Keep creative autonomy—never let a brand script your content

## Keep In Mind

- Podcasting generated the lowest monthly revenue of any of Bartlett's revenue streams during the first 18 months, but it became a multi-million dollar business because he treated it as a scalable asset rather than a hobby.
- 95% of podcast discovery happens outside of Spotify and Apple Podcasts—on social media, WhatsApp, and word of mouth—so your distribution strategy matters as much as your content.
- The "labor of love" mindset is fine, but if you want to scale revenue, you need a written monetization plan from the first month.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never let sponsors dictate what you say. Scripted ad reads destroy audience trust and reduce long-term sponsorship renewal rates.
- Always disclose sponsorships and brand relationships to your audience to maintain legal compliance and trust.
- Avoid over-promising future metrics (e.g., rankings, listener numbers) that you cannot verify or control.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Relying on podcast ad networks that pay per download and take a 20–40% cut.
  **Solution:** Build a small list of brands you genuinely use, find decision-maker contact details through LinkedIn and company websites, and pitch them directly with audience data and a clear 12-month vision.

- **Problem:** Inconsistent publishing that causes audience attrition and stalled growth.
  **Solution:** Pick one cadence (e.g., every Monday at 7 AM) and protect it like a non-negotiable appointment. Build audience habit around that slot and signal reliability to sponsors.

- **Problem:** Sacrificing quality for volume or guest fame.
  **Solution:** Set a concrete value rubric before recording. If the conversation lacks actionable insight or a clear takeaway for your audience, do not publish it—even if the guest has a massive following.

- **Problem:** Treating the show as a hobby and under-investing in production or team support.
  **Solution:** If you want to scale, commit to proper equipment and a dedicated team early. Bartlett invested £40,000 in equipment during his first year and built an 8-person crew, which made the show look and sound professional enough to attract premium sponsors paying £5,000–£15,000 per episode.
