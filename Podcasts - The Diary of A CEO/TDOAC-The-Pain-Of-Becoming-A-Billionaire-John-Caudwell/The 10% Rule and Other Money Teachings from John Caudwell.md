# The 10% Rule and Other Money Teachings from John Caudwell

## Overview

John Caudwell founded Phones 4u with minimal capital and no existing retail network, growing it into a business with 12,000 employees and £2.4 billion in annual turnover, reaching billionaire status through his majority shareholding. This Money Teaching distills his hard-won lessons on surviving extreme business pressure, diversifying risk, motivating people, and turning setbacks into comebacks. The focus is on practical principles you can apply whether you are running a startup or managing a growing company with more than 50 employees.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When a single supplier, customer, or key employee represents more than 20% of your annual revenue or operational capacity
- When you are facing a threat that could force the business to close within 12 months
- When you need to motivate a team of more than 10 people without making individual contributors so confident that they leave for competitors
- When you are deciding whether to implement a change requiring more than 10% of annual revenue or 20% of management time
- When you are analyzing how you responded to past crises and whether your behavior helped or hurt the team

## Steps

### Step 1: Apply the 10% Rule to Diversify Risk

Never allow more than 10% of your supplies to come from any one supplier, never allow more than 10% of your sales to come from any one customer, and never allow more than 10% of critical responsibility to rest with any one employee. This creates insulation against sudden catastrophes such as a supplier terminating your agreement, a customer leaving, or a key person departing. Use 10% as your target threshold even if you cannot achieve it perfectly when you first start.

### Step 2: Build Unshakeable Resilience

Resilience is partly innate, but it is also strengthened by repeated exposure to high-stakes problems. Expect to face situations that threaten your business's survival and train yourself to take action rather than freeze. John Caudwell worked extended hours during Phones 4u's most critical growth periods and survived because he refused to quit when competitors failed or suppliers terminated contracts. Accept that extreme pressure comes with rapid growth, and build specific coping systems—such as a weekly check-in with a mentor, a fixed sleep schedule, and a clear protocol for delegating decisions to trusted deputies—to keep functioning when the stakes are highest.

### Step 3: Let Fairness Drive Every Decision

Fairness is the foundational quality for long-term trust with employees, partners, and customers. After experiencing an unfair childhood during which his family faced financial instability caused by another party's breach of contract, Caudwell resolved never to treat another human being unfairly if he could avoid it. Before making a tough call, run a concrete test: ask whether you would be willing to receive the same treatment if your roles were reversed. Fairness reduces hidden resentment and protects your reputation when times get tough.

### Step 4: Make Quantum-Leap Changes Only

Do not change anything unless the change produces at least a 25% improvement in a core metric such as revenue per employee, gross margin, or customer retention. Small changes for the sake of change waste energy, force employees to learn systems they will abandon within six months, and create operational disruption without measurable reward. Before pushing a new initiative, write down the specific end-state numbers you expect and verify that the projected upside exceeds the total cost of execution, including training time and temporary lost productivity.

### Step 5: Harness Self-Criticism to Improve

Self-criticism is one of the most powerful forces in business. Within 24 hours of a high-pressure situation, write down three specific things that went wrong and three concrete actions you will take differently next time. The goal is not to dwell on failure, but to extract the lesson fast so you do not repeat it. At the same time, celebrate specific milestones—such as a product launch on schedule, a key hire starting within budget, or a quarterly revenue target met—so your team does not feel perpetually inadequate.

### Step 6: Hire for Your Weaknesses

Identify the two or three disciplines where you are merely competent but not exceptional, and bring in people who are demonstrably better than you in those specific areas. Caudwell described himself as competent across all business functions but exceptional in none, and his success depended on hiring specialists he could respect in their fields. If you are weak at follow-up, hire a project manager who delivers every milestone on schedule. If you are weak at detail, hire an operations manager who catches errors before they reach customers.

### Step 7: Use Compensation to Balance Ambition and Loyalty

When managing high performers, tie basic pay rises to demonstrated ambition—such as completing a leadership program, launching a new product line, or expanding into a new market—rather than guaranteeing bonuses larger than 20% of base salary for hitting easily achievable targets. This prevents entitlement while still rewarding results. Complement financial incentives with long-term wealth-creation schemes such as share-option plans that vest over four years, so people feel valued without becoming poachable by competitors offering one-year bonuses.

## Examples

### Example 1: Surviving the Motorola Termination

When Motorola terminated Caudwell's distribution agreement representing 90% of his business, he faced instant bankruptcy. Instead of giving up, he found alternative suppliers secretly, struck a deal with Nokia to build their UK market share, and ultimately helped Nokia become the UK market leader while Motorola declined. This shows how obeying the 10% rule, combined with relentless problem-solving, can turn terminal risk into opportunity.

### Example 2: Motivating Employees Without Ego Inflation

In a volatile mobile phone market where Nokia, Motorola, and Ericsson competed for the same UK retail shelf space, Caudwell had to drive his team hard without making them so confident that they would leave for competitors. He used a mix of fear (no pay rise without demonstrated ambition), incentive (substantial bonuses for genuine stretch targets such as doubling market share in 18 months), and pride (being part of a winning organization with four-year share-option schemes) to keep people engaged and loyal.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Diversify suppliers, customers, and key responsibilities toward a 10% maximum threshold
- ✅ Respond to threats by mapping at least three alternative courses of action before concluding a problem is unsolvable
- ✅ Be fair, even when the other party has been unethical
- ✅ Make change only when it delivers at least a 25% improvement in a core business metric
- ✅ Criticize yourself within 24 hours, then move on
- ✅ Hire people who are demonstrably better than you in your weak areas
- ✅ Tie pay rises to demonstrated ambition, not just outcomes

## Keep In Mind

- Resilience is partly innate and partly built through repeated exposure to high-stakes problems; not everyone will survive the same extended hours and existential threats that you can endure
- Your childhood wounds—such as growing up in poverty, experiencing betrayal, or feeling powerless—can become your greatest leadership strengths if you consciously invert them into specific behaviors such as paying staff early, refusing to break contracts, or granting autonomy to employees
- Success in a competitive market with thin margins often requires making hard decisions—such as closing unprofitable stores, switching suppliers, or restructuring teams—that others will criticize
- Wealth without purpose is hollow; the deepest satisfaction comes from measurable social impact such as funding a children's charity that has saved 1,000 lives or mentoring 50 young entrepreneurs to launch profitable businesses

## Security & Safety Notes

- When negotiating secret supply arrangements, require all parties to sign non-disclosure agreements and limit knowledge of the alternative supplier to a maximum of three trusted executives so primary suppliers cannot cut you off
- Protect employees working more than 55 hours per week from burnout by offering paid sabbaticals after 12 months of sustained overtime, mandatory rest days, and role adjustments when they show signs of collapse such as missed deadlines, illness, or emotional outbursts
- When handling a family health crisis alongside business pressure, delegate operational control to at least two senior leaders who have authority to approve expenditures up to £500,000 and hire or fire direct reports, so you do not have to carry both loads alone

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Allowing one supplier, customer, or employee to represent more than 10% of your business
  **Solution:** Audit exposure every quarter and sign letters of intent with alternative suppliers or customers before a crisis forces your hand

- **Problem:** Changing processes for the sake of change
  **Solution:** Require a written business case showing specific end-state metrics—such as revenue per employee increasing from £200,000 to £260,000 within 12 months—before approving any significant change

- **Problem:** Praising people so much that their ego makes them leave
  **Solution:** Balance public recognition with written feedback on specific areas for improvement, and tie long-term rewards such as share options to both tenure of at least four years and measurable results such as revenue growth or cost reduction

- **Problem:** Refusing to delegate follow-up because you are the only one who cares
  **Solution:** Hire or promote an operations director who thrives on weekly accountability meetings and detailed financial reporting; your job is setting the three-year vision and approving the top 10 capital projects each year
