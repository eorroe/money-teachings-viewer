# Codie Sanchez: They're Lying To You About How To Get Rich! How To Turn $0 Into $1M!

## Overview

This money teaching distills Codie Sanchez's core philosophy that financial freedom and decision-making autonomy comes through ownership, not reliance on a salary or permission from others. It provides a practical roadmap for building wealth from scratch by leveraging relationships, developing deal-making skills, and acquiring simple cash-flowing businesses. The guidance is designed for anyone willing to put in consistent effort and learn from failures, frontload effort early, and reject the myth that wealth is reserved for the lucky or well-connected.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are in your 20s or early career and want to maximize learning and compounding advantage
- When you feel stuck in a job with limited upward mobility or tired of other people directing your life and finances
- When the user asks how to build wealth from scratch with limited capital, no contacts, or from a salary
- When you want to transition from employee to owner without risking capital you cannot afford to lose
- When you are looking for business models that require minimal upfront capital and do not require prior entrepreneurship experience

## Steps

### Step 1: Get Around Successful People and Provide Value

Identify the richest or most successful person you can reach within a 1-2 degree connection within your concentric circles—starting with your block, community, school, or local network. Do not chase fame or billionaires; instead target someone 1-3 years ahead in their career or business journey who is someone you can meet through a warm introduction or mutual contact and already living the life you want. Offer to offer specific help with their business or projects without immediately asking for anything in return. Ask thoughtful, specific questions about their decisions, then follow up by showing exactly how you implemented their advice. Consistency and follow-through are rare; if you play this relationship-building process for one to two years, you will build trust, access, and opportunities that money cannot buy.

### Step 2: Frontload Pain in Your 20s and Maximize Learning Over Earning

Accept that your 20s will be marked by long hours, low pay, and limited autonomy. You will have little time, limited money, and jobs that often do not align with your long-term goals. Do not prioritize salary above all other factors during this decade. Instead, treat your paycheck as an investment in your skills and network: work for people from whom you can learn the most, even if it means earning less. Switch to a new role or company every one to two years once you stop learning new skills or increasing your income by at least 10-15% annually. Avoid entrepreneurship too early; learn on someone else's dime for five to seven years before risking your own capital. If you frontload effort now, you build stamina, skills, and relationships that continue to benefit you throughout your career.

### Step 3: Make as Much Money as Possible from Your Salary and Invest in Side Deals

Earn the highest salary available for your role and experience level while you are an employee, but do not keep all of it sitting in a low-interest savings account. Direct income beyond your monthly living expenses and emergency fund into investments that generate cash flow before you ever consider leaving your job. Avoid startup investing until you are a millionaire; seed-stage deals have roughly a 90 percent failure rate. Instead, focus on profitable, cash-flowing opportunities such as bond deals, equity in small businesses, or employer-sponsored investment programs. If your company allows it, ask to invest alongside leadership in additional ventures. Use your salary to fund actual equity or profit participation in a business while you still have the safety net of a W-2.

### Step 4: Buy a Simple "Gateway Drug" Business Using Sweat Equity

If you do not have $50,000 or more in liquid savings, buy a business with sweat equity, expertise, or time instead of cash. Target boring, simple businesses that anyone can understand: laundromats, car washes, window cleaning, pressure washing, or painting. These businesses have few employees, few moving parts, and low barriers to entry. Use seller financing whenever possible—this is how roughly 60 percent of small businesses change hands. Your goal is not to get rich on your first deal; it is to learn how to run a P&L, market, and generate profit so you can sell and move to a larger, more profitable business later.

### Step 5: Use the Unfair Advantage Venn Diagram to Choose Your Business

Before buying or starting a business, map three things: your unique skills, your network, and what genuinely excites you. The intersection of these three is your unique position where you have skills, connections, and motivation that competitors lack. For example, if you are good at marketing, know a plumber, and love sales, you might partner on a plumbing business instead of chasing a generic franchise. The most reliable path to meaningful wealth is ownership aligned with something you have the skills and drive to operate successfully. Do not skip this step; most aspiring entrepreneurs struggle because they do not know themselves well enough to choose the opportunity that matches their specific skills and network.

### Step 6: Negotiate Like Your Life Depends on It

Spend more time learning how to do a deal than actually doing the deal. Study valuations, due diligence, and deal structure before you look at businesses. Treat deal sourcing like dating: review at least one hundred opportunities, filter them based on your criteria, and then commit to one. Never do a deal where you overpay or have no clear exit strategy, because it will often be your last. Use seller financing, performance-based equity splits, and creative terms to lower your risk. If you can negotiate well, you will have a skill that continues to increase in value throughout your career regardless of market conditions.

## Examples

### Example 1: The 19-Year-Old Marketing Employee

Adam was a 19-year-old marketing employee with no capital and no business experience. He identified a vendor his company used for graphic design work and noticed the owner was tired of running the business. Adam proposed buying the company with seller financing—paying a percentage of future profits instead of a lump sum. He negotiated a minority ownership stake by bringing the deal to his boss and agreeing to run the operations. Within a short time, Adam owned a profitable business he later sold, all without risking his own savings.

### Example 2: The Writer Who Moved to a Better Market

A writer was earning roughly $35,000 per year at a local newspaper. Instead of staying in that low-value market, she recognized that medical writers—who can translate complex clinical data into clear copy—earn $150,000 or more with minimal additional training. She repositioned her existing writing skill into biotech, a high-margin, high-revenue industry. She also structured her compensation with upside: a lower base salary plus a percentage of revenue driven above goal. Within months, her income and equity potential increased tenfold.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Frontload pain in your 20s by learning obsessively rather than chasing the highest immediate salary
- ✅ Provide massive value before asking for anything in return; follow through consistently
- ✅ Target simple, understandable businesses with low overhead such as laundromats or car washes
- ✅ Use seller financing and sweat equity instead of waiting until you are rich to buy a business
- ✅ Ask your boss for equity or investment opportunities if you become a marquee performer
- ✅ Make decisions in 30 seconds or less; iterate quickly rather than overthinking
- ✅ Surround yourself with people richer and faster than you; economic interconnectedness is contagious
- ❌ Do not badmouth your last boss or take a victim mentality in interviews
- ❌ Do not ask about work-life balance before proving your obsession and value
- ❌ Do not invest in startups until you are already a millionaire
- ❌ Do not confuse thinking with work; action and iteration beat perfect planning

## Keep In Mind

- The 20s are supposed to be marked by long hours, low pay, and limited autonomy; seeing clear evidence that your skills and network are growing makes the temporary financial constraint and extra work hours bearable
- Your first deal is never your last deal; treat early businesses as training grounds, not permanent identities
- If your business has low referrals and reviews, you have a product problem—not a marketing problem
- Speed of communication and speed of action are among the strongest predictors of promotion to senior management or executive roles
- Obsession cannot be faked; if you cannot stop thinking about something at 3:00 a.m., that is your true advantage
- Baby boomers own more than 50 percent of small businesses and many have no transition plan—this is a generational transfer of wealth opportunity
- Being different is an asset in a world of blue suits; reframe perceived weaknesses as memorable strengths

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never invest in startups or speculative deals until you have at least one million dollars in net worth
- Do not put all savings into a single business or asset; maintain diversification and a cash buffer
- Verify seller financing terms and business profitability through proper due diligence before signing anything
- Avoid putting yourself in a position where a bad first deal can cause bankruptcy
- When approaching a business owner, never offer unpaid labor that could create legal or PR liability
- Seller financing relies on trust; ensure the seller believes you can grow or at least not ruin the business

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Getting stuck in a low-value job or industry because you do not realize your skill set is transferable to a higher-margin market
  **Solution:** Map your skills on one side of a whiteboard and high-margin industries on the other; move to where your skill is scarce and the stakes are high
- **Problem:** Waiting until you are "rich" to buy a business, missing out on decades of compounding ownership
  **Solution:** Use sweat equity, seller financing, and creative deal structures to gain ownership exposure now
- **Problem:** Spending too much time planning and not enough time acting, causing opportunities to pass
  **Solution:** Apply the 30-second decision rule and the 24-hour execution rule to compress the gap between thinking and doing
- **Problem:** Hiring people who waste time and lack obsession, poisoning company culture from the start
  **Solution:** In interviews, watch for victim mentality, badmouthing former bosses, or premature work-life-balance questions—these are immediate red flags
- **Problem:** Pouring marketing dollars into a product with no referrals or reviews
  **Solution:** Fix the product and retention first; the three Rs—referrals, reviews, and retention—are the only sustainable growth engines
