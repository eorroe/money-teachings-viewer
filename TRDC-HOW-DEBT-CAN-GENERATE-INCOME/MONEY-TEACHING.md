# How Debt Can Generate Income

## Overview

This Money Teaching teaches you how to classify every form of debt as either "good debt" or "bad debt" by applying the test of cash flow direction, and how to use good debt to build a portfolio of income-generating assets. The critical insight is that the type of asset itself (house, car, boat, student loan) is irrelevant — what determines whether debt is good or bad is whether the borrowed money ends up putting cash into your pocket each month or taking cash out of your pocket each month. Robert Kiyosaki and his wife currently own 6,500 rental properties, all 100 percent financed with debt.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When evaluating whether a loan, mortgage, credit card balance, or any form of debt is worth taking on
- When you are deciding whether to use debt to finance a real estate purchase, business investment, or asset acquisition
- When someone tells you "get out of all debt" and you want to understand why that advice is incomplete for financially educated people
- When you want to distinguish between your primary residence (a liability) and a rental property (an asset) for financial statement purposes
- When you are a millennial or young adult evaluating student loan debt and want to understand when it is worth taking on
- When you want to use a credit card strategically to fund an investment rather than a consumer purchase

## Steps

### Step 1: Learn the Six Words of Financial Intelligence

Memorize and internalize the six foundational words of financial education: **income, expense, asset, liability, cash flow, and the financial statement**. These six words are the lens through which every financial decision must be evaluated. As Alexandra notes, this was never mentioned in her college courses, and Kiyosaki observes that accounting classes rarely cover cash flow analysis, so you must seek out real-world financial education independently — Robert Kiyosaki recommends taking real estate classes and learning from investors, accountants, and financial advisors who have personally used these principles in actual investment transactions, not from instructors who lack real-world experience.

### Step 2: Define Asset vs. Liability Using the Cash Flow Test

Apply the cash flow test to every item you own or are considering buying: an **asset** is anything that puts money in your pocket each month. A **liability** is anything that takes money out of your pocket each month. A house you live in is a liability — even if you have no mortgage on it — because property taxes, depreciation, repairs, upkeep, and insurance all drain cash from your pocket every month. A car you drive personally is also a liability for the same reason. Student loan debt is bad debt because it does not generate any income while you are obligated to make monthly payments. A rental property that produces positive monthly rent after all expenses is an asset. A boat that is chartered out to paying customers and generates monthly income is an asset. The physical item itself does not determine whether it is an asset or liability — only the direction of monthly cash flow does.

### Step 3: Classify Every Form of Debt as Good or Bad

Use the cash flow test to classify each debt obligation you hold or are considering:

- **Good debt**: Debt used to borrow money for an asset where the monthly income generated by that asset covers the monthly debt payment, covers all monthly expenses, and still puts additional money in your pocket each month. Examples include a mortgage on a rental property where monthly rent exceeds the monthly mortgage payment plus monthly expenses.
- **Bad debt**: Debt used to borrow money for a liability where no income is generated, meaning you are personally responsible for making the debt payment from your own earned income. Examples include student loans, a mortgage on your primary residence, car loans for personal use, and credit card debt spent on consumer goods like clothing, electronics, or vacations.

A credit card can be good debt or bad debt depending entirely on what you purchase with it. If you use a credit card to buy an asset that generates income (for example, a rental property), it is good debt. If you use a credit card to buy a luxury jacket, it is bad debt.

### Step 4: Commit to Ongoing Financial Education

Recognize that using debt strategically to build wealth requires a high level of financial intelligence, not just a willingness to borrow. Robert Kiyosaki began taking real estate classes at age 25 and never stopped. The reason is simple: in real estate and any income-generating investment, a single mistake can turn what was supposed to be good debt into bad debt. If you buy a rental property and the tenant stops paying, the property can flip from an asset to a liability, with cash flowing out of your pocket instead of in. You must learn how to evaluate deals, structure financing, and manage the asset so that it consistently produces positive monthly cash flow. Find investors, real estate advisors, and qualified accountants who have personally used these principles to acquire income-producing assets — not from instructors who lack real-world experience.

### Step 5: Control the Direction of Your Cash Flow

Whether you are an employee or an entrepreneur, you must control the direction of your monthly cash flow. Most employees earn income that immediately flows out through taxes, mortgage payments, student loan payments, car payments, and living expenses — leaving little to nothing for investment. Entrepreneurs legally structure their affairs so that cash first flows into assets (which then generate more cash) before flowing out to expenses or taxes. This does not happen accidentally. It requires deliberate deal structuring and treating debt as a tool to acquire income-producing assets rather than as a burden to be eliminated.

### Step 6: Act on the Insight — Don't Be Afraid of Debt, Use It Correctly

The final step is to take action based on the framework you have learned. Do not avoid debt out of fear. Avoid bad debt — debt that takes money from your pocket. Seek out good debt — debt that puts money in your pocket. Robert Kiyosaki bought his first investment property in Maui, Hawaii in the 1970s for $18,000 using a credit card. After taking real estate classes and learning how to evaluate deals, he found the right property, charged the purchase to his credit card, and the property immediately began generating $25 per month in positive cash flow. That was good debt. He later sold that property and considers it a mistake because it would probably be worth $400,000 to $500,000 today — and it would still be generating monthly income. He now advises never to flip (quickly sell) income-producing properties, because holding them preserves the monthly cash flow stream.

## Examples

### Example 1: Maui Condo Purchased with a Credit Card

Robert Kiyosaki's first real estate investment was a one-bedroom condo in Maui, Hawaii purchased in the 1970s for $18,000. He charged the purchase to his credit card, making the credit card debt the source of financing for the acquisition. After taking several real estate classes, he knew how to evaluate the deal and structure it so that the property immediately generated $25 in positive monthly cash flow after all expenses. The credit card debt was therefore good debt: it financed an asset that put money in his pocket every month, even after covering the debt payment. He later sold this property and calls it a mistake, because a property worth probably $400,000 to $500,000 today that generates monthly income would still be producing cash flow. This example demonstrates that the form of debt (credit card) does not determine whether it is good or bad — the cash flow direction does.

### Example 2: 6,500 Fully-Financed Rental Properties

Robert Kiyosaki and his wife currently own 6,500 rental properties, and every single one is 100 percent financed with debt. All 6,500 properties generate positive monthly cash flow for their owners. Every month, all 6,500 properties collectively put money in their pockets. This is possible because each property was acquired using good debt: borrowed money was used to buy an asset where the rental income covers the mortgage payment, property management, maintenance, and all other expenses, with money remaining. This is the practical result of applying the cash flow test and continuously educating oneself on real estate investment.

## Best Practices

- Define every item you own as an asset or liability using only the cash flow test: does it put money in your pocket each month, or take money out?
- Classify every debt obligation as good or bad using the same cash flow test before you take it on
- Invest in ongoing financial education — take real estate classes, work with qualified accountants, and learn from investors and financial advisors who have personally used these principles in actual investment transactions
- Use debt to finance income-producing assets, not consumer purchases or lifestyle spending
- Buy rental properties that generate positive cash flow from day one, not properties that require ongoing cash infusions
- Never flip (quickly sell) an income-producing property — holding it preserves the monthly cash flow stream
- Control the direction of your cash flow so that money first flows into assets before it flows out to taxes and expenses
- Do not classify your primary residence as an asset — it is a liability regardless of whether you have a mortgage on it
- Do not take on student loan debt unless you are 100 percent committed to graduating
- Do not use a credit card to finance consumer purchases such as clothing, electronics, or vacations — that is bad debt
- Do not assume that all debt is bad or that all debt is good — the classification depends entirely on the cash flow direction
- Do not avoid studying financial principles just because traditional schools teach them poorly or irrelevantly
- Do not buy a real estate investment without thoroughly learning how to evaluate deals and manage the property — one mistake turns good debt into bad debt

## Keep In Mind

- The word "debt" itself is neutral — it is neither inherently good nor inherently bad. What makes debt good or bad is the purpose for which it is used and the direction of cash flow it produces.
- A house is a liability when you live in it, even if it is completely paid off with no mortgage. Taxes, insurance, repairs, and upkeep all require cash outflow every month.
- A car, boat, or any other physical item is only an asset if it generates monthly income that exceeds its monthly ownership and operating costs every month.
- Student loan debt is classified as bad debt by Robert Kiyosaki because it does not generate income while you are obligated to make payments from your own labor.
- A credit card is a tool, not a type of debt. Whether credit card debt is good or bad depends entirely on what you buy with it.
- Traditional financial education does not teach the practical application of cash flow analysis. You must seek out investors, accountants, and financial advisors who have personally used these principles in actual investment transactions, not from instructors who lack real-world experience.
- Entrepreneurs legally structure their affairs so that cash flows into assets first, in contrast to employees whose cash immediately flows out to taxes.
- If you are not willing to invest the time to learn how to properly evaluate and manage income-generating assets, then focusing on eliminating bad debt is the safer strategy for you.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Using debt to acquire assets carries real financial risk: if the asset does not produce the expected cash flow, you are personally responsible for the debt payments regardless of the asset's performance.
- A rental property that sits vacant or requires expensive repairs can shift from being good debt to bad debt, draining your personal finances.
- Using a credit card to fund an investment exposes you to high-interest charges if the investment does not generate income fast enough to cover the minimum payments.
- Robert Kiyosaki emphasizes that using debt to acquire assets requires a high level of financial intelligence and continuous education — a single mistake can turn good debt into bad debt.
- Student loan debt cannot be discharged in most bankruptcy cases filed in United States federal courts, making it one of the most dangerous forms of bad debt if the investment (your education) does not produce the expected return.
- Real estate investments are subject to market conditions, tenant risk, property damage, and regulatory changes — all of which can disrupt the expected cash flow.
- Always consult with qualified legal, tax, and financial professionals before using debt to acquire assets, as individual circumstances and local laws vary.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Believing your primary residence is an asset because it has market value or because the mortgage is paid off.
  **Solution:** Apply the cash flow test. If the house takes money from your pocket each month for taxes, insurance, repairs, and upkeep, it is a liability — regardless of its market value or mortgage status.

- **Problem:** Taking on student loan debt without being absolutely 100% guaranteed that you will commit to graduating.
  **Solution:** Only take on student loan debt if you are 100 percent committed to graduating.

- **Problem:** Using a credit card to buy consumer goods (clothing, electronics, vacations) and treating the resulting debt as normal or acceptable.
  **Solution:** Only use a credit card for purchases that will generate income — such as funding an investment. If the purchase does not put money in your pocket, it is bad debt and should be avoided.

- **Problem:** Buying a rental property without sufficient education and losing money because the property generates negative cash flow.
  **Solution:** Take real estate classes, learn how to evaluate properties for cash flow before purchasing, and never buy an investment property that does not produce positive monthly income from day one after all expenses.

- **Problem:** Flipping (quickly selling) income-generating properties instead of holding them for long-term cash flow.
  **Solution:** Never flip properties that generate positive monthly cash flow. Holding such properties preserves the ongoing monthly cash flow stream, and the property continues to compound in value while producing income.

- **Problem:** Believing all debt is bad and should be eliminated, missing the opportunity to use good debt to build wealth.
  **Solution:** Reframe your thinking: do not ask "how do I get out of debt?" — ask "is this debt good or bad?" If it is good debt (generating positive cash flow), keep it and use it to acquire more assets. If it is bad debt, eliminate it.

- **Problem:** Making a mistake on a real estate deal and having the property flip from an asset to a liability because a tenant stops paying.
  **Solution:** Continuously educate yourself on real estate evaluation and management, have cash reserves set aside for vacancies and emergency repairs, and never rely on a single property as your only source of income.

## Glossary

**Asset** — Anything that puts money into your pocket on a regular basis. An asset generates cash flow whether you work or not.

**Bad Debt** — Debt used to borrow money for a liability where no income is generated, meaning you are personally responsible for making the debt payment from your own earned income.

**Cash Flow** — The movement of money into or out of your pocket over a given period. Cash flow direction — in or out — determines whether something is an asset or a liability.

**Cash Flow Test** — The method of classifying any item as an asset or liability by asking one question: does it put money into your pocket each month, or take money out?

**Financial Statement** — A record (typically an income statement or balance sheet) that maps every item in your financial life into income, expenses, assets, liabilities, and cash flow.

**FICO Score** — A credit score used by lenders to evaluate creditworthiness. Kiyosaki contrasts reliance on a high FICO score with the ability to manage cash flow, arguing that cash flow management is more critical to financial intelligence than a credit score.

**Good Debt** — Debt used to borrow money for an asset where the monthly income generated by that asset covers the monthly debt payment, covers all monthly expenses, and still puts additional money in your pocket each month.

**Liability** — Anything that takes money out of your pocket on a regular basis. A liability drains cash flow regardless of your employment status.

**Six Words of Financial Intelligence** — The six foundational terms Kiyosaki identifies as the basis of financial literacy: income, expense, asset, liability, cash flow, and the financial statement.
