# If I Started Investing In 2026, This Is What I Would Do: The Venture Capital Playbook

## Overview

This Money Teaching shares the investment philosophy of Bill Gurley, one of the most renowned venture capitalists in the world, who backed Uber, Zillow, OpenTable, and other category-defining companies. Gurley explains that ideas are a dime a dozen, execution is everything, and the traits he looks for in founders are determinism, product obsession, and hyper-curious learning.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When evaluating startup founders or business opportunities
- When deciding whether to invest time or money in a new venture
- When building a team or partnership in a high-growth business
- When the user asks about contrarian investing or finding the next big thing

## Steps

### Step 1: Evaluate Founders on Determinism, Not Pedigree

Look for founders who will go do this come hell or high water, whether you get involved or not. Jeff Bezos looks for the determinism of the founder - the belief that nothing will stop them. Do not filter solely on track record or previous exits. Many great founders had failed ventures before their big success, including Travis Kalanick of Uber.

### Step 2: Require Product Obsession

The founder job requires salesmanship at every level - selling to customers, investors, employees, and the public. But the most underrated trait is obsession with product. The best founders can see through technology dislocations and disruptions better than others. If a founder has not put the latest tool or technology together themselves, that is a problem.

### Step 3: Find Hyper-Curious Learners

Founders must be obsessed with the edge of their field. With AI creating new edges in every industry, founders who are not learning every day will fall behind. Read biographies, study the craft, and constantly ask what the new techniques are. Exceptional founders are tremendous learners who are obsessed with technology, disruption, and where the world is going.

### Step 4: Assess Industry Structure and Network Effects

Before falling in love with a product, understand the industry structure. Michael Porter's Competitive Strategy explains that building into a consolidated industry is tough. You want fragmented industries where you can sell into. Look for network effects - the more users you get, the more compelling the product becomes, creating a flywheel toward a single dominant player.

### Step 5: Find a Go-to-Market Advantage

The best founders have a structural way to get customers that is advantageous to traditional methods. This could be a unique distribution channel, a viral loop, or a community-driven growth engine. Go-to-market advantage is often intertwined with product in service businesses. Study how successful founders acquired customers differently than competitors.

### Step 6: Use the Regret Minimization Framework

When making career or investment decisions, ask: "If I were 80 giving myself advice, what would I do?" Bezos used this when leaving his hedge fund job to start Amazon. Apply the same lens to your own decisions. Will your 80-year-old self be proud of the path you are on right now?

## Examples

### Example 1: The Uber Investment

In 2011, Bill Gurley invested in Uber when it was a scrappy startup putting a layer on top of black car services. His insight came from OpenTable, where he learned that network effects create massive value. Most startups at the time were trying to put a layer on top of taxis, which were monopolies or duopolies with regulatory lock-in. Uber's use of non-regulated black cars and dynamic pricing unlocked a digital marketplace that could scale globally.

### Example 2: The Google Miss

Bill Gurley passed on investing in Google when they had 25 employees. He and his partners failed to lay chase. He uses this as fuel to study why they missed winners. The lesson: you are not going to bat a thousand, but you must put yourself in positions to have the opportunity. Missing asymmetric returns is far more painful than making bets that don't work.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Evaluate founders on determinism and product obsession
- ✅ Look for hyper-curious learners who are in it every day
- ✅ Study industry structure before investing
- ✅ Find network effects and fragmented markets
- ✅ Require a go-to-market advantage
- ✅ Use regret minimization for major life decisions
- ✅ Write to think and build reputation
- ✅ Study competitors' wins obsessively
- ❌ Don't invest based on pedigree alone
- ❌ Don't let groupthink override contrarian analysis
- ❌ Don't ignore industry structure because you love the product
- ❌ Don't assume ideas are unique - execution is what matters

## Keep In Mind

- "Chips on shoulders put chips in pockets" - previous failures often create more firepower and determinism
- Venture capital bends toward youth - many of the best founders are young, and disruption tools are better absorbed by younger people
- The best way to get deal flow is through differentiated channels: blogs, podcasts, vertical expertise, or obsessive networking
- 98% of VCs are only looking at AI right now, creating opportunity in non-AI businesses for PE firms

## Security & Safety Notes

- Asymmetric returns mean missing a winner hurts more than a losing bet
- Always ask "what could go right?" rather than just listing risks
- Build mental models but don't let them become blinders

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Passing on a great founder because they don't have a previous exit
  **Solution:** Look for how they failed - was it effort or market timing? Previous failures often create stronger determinism
- **Problem:** Getting caught in groupthink around hot sectors like AI
  **Solution:** Contrarian investing is where the most money is made, but you have to be willing to be wrong
- **Problem:** Focusing only on the founder and ignoring industry structure
  **Solution:** Read the first two chapters of Michael Porter's Competitive Strategy before building into any industry
- **Problem:** Not having differentiated deal flow
  **Solution:** Create inbound flypaper through writing, podcasting, vertical expertise, or public commentary
