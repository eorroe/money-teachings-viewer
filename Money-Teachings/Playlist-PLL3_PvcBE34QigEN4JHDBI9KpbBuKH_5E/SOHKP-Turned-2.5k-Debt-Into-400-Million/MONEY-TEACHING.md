# Money Teaching: SOHKP Turned 2.5k Debt Into 400 Million

## Overview

This Money Teaching captures Dr. Jason Wersland's journey from chiropractor to inventor of the TheraGun (now TheraBody), which grew from a personal solution to a multi-hundred-million-dollar brand. It teaches that the most reliable path to building a valuable product is to solve a painful problem you have personally experienced—not to chase trendy ideas. The teaching also covers how to prototype and test a product using existing tools, why persistence and a sense of obligation to your creation matter more than initial market size, and how to decide when a product is ready to commercialize.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you want to start a product-based business but do not have a "bright" or trendy idea
- When you have experienced a personal pain point that existing products cannot solve
- When you are a practitioner (doctor, trainer, therapist) who has identified a gap in available tools
- When you need guidance on how long to develop a product before bringing it to market
- When you want to build a company rooted in genuine user need rather than market hype
- When you are recovering from a setback (injury, business loss, career change) and want to redirect that experience into value

## Steps

### Step 1: Identify a Problem You Have Personally Experienced That No Existing Product Solves

The most reliable product ideas come from your own pain, not from market research or trend reports. If you have tried every available solution and none worked, that gap is your opportunity. Example: After a 2007 motorcycle accident left Dr. Wersland with severe shoulder pain, he tried every treatment available at his clinic—all temporary. He realized no portable device could deliver the pain relief he needed outside of the clinic.

### Step 2: Prototype the Solution Using Available Tools

Do not wait for funding or a perfect manufacturing setup. Use any tools in your garage, clinic, or workshop to build the first working version. The goal of the first prototype is to prove the concept works, not to look like a finished product. Example: Dr. Wersland took an old vibrating instrument from the X-ray room of his clinic, used it on his shoulder, felt immediate relief, and realized a portable version could help others. That realization became the first TheraGun prototype.

### Step 3: Test the Prototype on Yourself Before Testing on Others

Put your own pain or need at the center of the testing process. If the device does not work for you at a pain level of 10 out of 10, it will not work for anyone else. Measure results in terms of pain reduction, speed of relief, and duration of effect. Example: Dr. Wersland used the first prototype on his own shoulder. When the vibration stopped, the pain returned. He realized he needed a device that moved back and forth rather than providing constant vibration—his body was adapting to the static stimulus.

### Step 4: Refine the Product Until the Effect Lasts Long Enough to Be Useful

Adaptation is the body's natural response to constant stimulus. A device that vibrates constantly will stop providing relief because the nervous system will filter it out as background noise. Design the product to interrupt that adaptation—for example, by moving back and forth rather than vibrating in place. Example: The TheraGun uses a percussive motion that moves back and forth, preventing the nervous system from adapting and ensuring that each use delivers consistent pain relief.

### Step 5: Develop the Product Over Months or Years Before Commercializing

Do not rush to market with a half-built solution. Dr. Wersland made the first TheraGun prototype in February 2008 and spent substantial time developing and perfecting it before commercializing. The sense of obligation to get it right—not just to sell something—should drive the development timeline. Example: Dr. Wersland described the development process as something he felt he owed to the universe. He made a promise to himself and to future users that the product would work before he would put it in anyone else's hands.

### Step 6: Validate the Product With Real Users Who Have the Same Problem

Before scaling production, test the product with people who have the same injury or condition you had. Their feedback will reveal whether the product works consistently across different body types, pain levels, and use cases. Example: Dr. Wersland invited a patient with the same injuries (a bus driver who had been in a head-on collision) to test the device. This real-world validation confirmed the product worked for others, not just for its inventor.

### Step 7: Scale Production Only After the Product Proven Itself

Once the product has been tested, refined, and validated, invest in manufacturing and distribution. Do not scale before you know the product works reliably. Example: Dr. Wersland did not begin mass-producing TheraGuns until he was certain the percussive therapy mechanism delivered consistent, lasting relief across multiple users. That patience built a brand that reached hundreds of millions of people worldwide.

## Examples

### Example 1: From Motorcycle Accident to Multi-Million-Dollar Brand

In 2007, Dr. Jason Wersland crashed his motorcycle into the side of a car, flew over it, and landed on the highway. After months of clinical treatment that provided only temporary relief, he built the first TheraGun prototype in his garage using a Makita jigsaw and parts from his clinic. The device he created to solve his own pain is now used by professional athletes, physical therapists, and millions of people around the world. TheraBody (formerly TheraGun) is valued in the hundreds of millions.

### Example 2: Necessity Driving Invention Across Industries

The "necessity is the mother of invention" principle applies beyond health and wellness. Jimmy John built his sandwich empire not because he wanted to dominate food service, but because he needed money for weed and beer. The owner of a gas station network bought his first station for $10,000 because that was his only goal—not because he planned to build a large company. The pattern is consistent: start with a real, immediate need, solve it well, and scale from there.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Start with a problem you have personally experienced—do not chase trends or copy existing products
- ✅ Build the first prototype with tools available to you right now
- ✅ Test the prototype on yourself first, with real pain or a real need
- ✅ Refine until the product effect lasts long enough to be genuinely useful
- ✅ Take the time to develop and perfect the product before commercializing
- ✅ Validate with real users who have the same problem before scaling production
- ✅ Let a sense of obligation to the user—not just profit—drive your development timeline
- ❌ Do not launch a product you have not personally tested under real conditions
- ❌ Do not assume a trendy idea will succeed if you have not experienced the pain it claims to solve
- ❌ Do not rush to scale before the product is proven reliable across multiple users
- ❌ Do not delegate the core development work before you fully understand the problem yourself

## Keep In Mind

- You do not need a bright, original, or trendy idea to build a successful company. You need a real problem, a personal connection to it, and the persistence to solve it.
- The body adapts to constant stimulus. Products that deliver constant vibration, pressure, or stimulation will stop working over time. Design for interruption and variation.
- Necessity is the mother of invention. Some of the biggest businesses in the world started because someone was trying to solve their own immediate problem—not because they were trying to get rich.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Protect the intellectual property of your product through patents, trademarks, or trade secret protections before scaling production
- Do not share unreleased product designs or manufacturing details publicly before filing for protection
- When testing with real users, obtain written consent and clearly communicate that the product is still in development

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Starting a product based on a market trend rather than personal need
  **Solution:** Ask yourself: "Have I experienced this problem directly, and have I tried every existing solution?" If the answer is no, keep looking.
- **Problem:** Rushing to commercialize before the product is fully developed
  **Solution:** Test on yourself first, then on others with the same condition. Do not scale until the effect is consistent and lasting.
- **Problem:** Designing a product that the body will adapt to and stop responding to
  **Solution:** Study how the nervous system filters constant stimulus. Build variation and interruption into the product mechanism.
- **Problem:** Delegating product development before you understand the problem deeply
  **Solution:** Build the first version yourself. You cannot direct others to solve a problem you have not personally wrestled with.
