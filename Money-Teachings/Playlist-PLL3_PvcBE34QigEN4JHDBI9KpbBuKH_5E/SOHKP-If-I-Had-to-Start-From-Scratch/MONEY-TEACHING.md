# If You Had to Start From Scratch Again, Here Is Exactly What To Do

## Overview

Vic Keller founded 17 companies, exited 9 of them (three to Berkshire Hathaway), and built businesses across industries he was not an expert in. His core advice for starting over is to build a durable business that customers cannot live without, implement systems and processes that run without you, hire for character and chemistry over credentials, and partner with people who have decades more experience than you do. This Money Teaching is the exact playbook for founders who want to build a company that lasts and attracts a strong acquisition offer without ever setting out to sell.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are building a company and want it to have enterprise value beyond your personal involvement
- When you want to attract acquisition interest without actively shopping your company
- When you are entering an industry you have no prior experience in
- When you need to hire people who will scale with the business
- When you are deciding between building one business well or chasing multiple opportunities
- When you want to build a business that survives economic downturns

## Steps

### Step 1: Build a Durable Business, Not One Designed to Be Sold

Keller never built any of his early companies with an exit strategy. The strategy was always how to build something that provided exceptional value to customers, generated strong cash flow, and created a great quality of life for his family. The exits happened naturally because the businesses were well-built. When you design for durability—for customers who cannot imagine leaving, for systems that run without you, for cash flow that survives recessions—acquisition interest follows. Do not build to sell. Build to last.

### Step 2: Architect Your Business to Be Durable and Attractive to a Strong Suitor

Today, when Keller builds a business, he designs it to be durable and to attract a strong acquirer. A durable business has customers who would be devastated if it disappeared, systems that run independently of the founder, and financial performance that holds up under stress. The most valuable companies are not the ones with the highest revenue—they are the ones with the most predictable, defensible cash flow. If you want to build enterprise value, prioritize durability over growth at all costs.

### Step 3: Implement Systems and Processes That Drive the Business Without You

The first principle of building a durable business is creating systems and processes that are systematic, consistent, and outlive the founder. Adrenaline and passion are not scalable. A business that depends on the founder's daily energy will never be valuable to an acquirer and will never survive the founder's absence. Document every process, create training materials, build dashboards, and set up feedback loops. Your goal is for the business to run on its systems, not on your presence.

### Step 4: De-Leverage Yourself From Daily Operations to Build Enterprise Value

Keller says the most important step in building enterprise value is deleveraging yourself from the business. If the business cannot operate without you, you do not own a business—you have a job. Test this by taking a two-week vacation with no access to work. If the team cannot make decisions, close deals, and solve problems in your absence, your systems are not ready. Keep removing yourself from day-to-day operations until the business runs without you. That is the point at which it becomes valuable and sellable.

### Step 5: Build a Feedback Loop That Captures Both Market and Employee Signals

Durable businesses have strong feedback loops. You need to know how the market is responding to your product, how your employees are responding to your leadership, and whether your operations are degrading before it shows up in financial results. Keller says adrenaline and passion are not scalable, but feedback loops are. Set up regular customer surveys, employee check-ins, and operational reviews. Make the feedback loop institutional, not dependent on your personal energy.

### Step 6: Hire for Character, Competency, and Chemistry

Keller uses the three C's framework: character (are they honest, reliable, and principled), competency (do they have the skills and curiosity to do the job well), and chemistry (do they make the people around them better). He adopted this from Chick-fil-A and discovered that Delta Force uses the exact same three criteria. No matter how smart or experienced a candidate is, if they lack chemistry and create friction on the team, they will destroy more value than they create. Hire people who are smarter than you, but only if they make everyone around them better.

### Step 7: Partner With People Who Are Decades Ahead of You

When Keller was 24 and running his first business, he met a multi-billionaire who became his business partner. He had no idea at the time how successful the man was, but the partnership accelerated his success exponentially. Keller says he learned more from that one relationship than he could have learned in 20 years on his own. If you are young and building something, seek out mentors and partners who have already solved the problems you are working on. The right partner collapses time and gives you access to networks, capital, and judgment you could never develop alone.

### Step 8: Enter Industries You Do Not Understand, but Go All In on Mastery

Keller has never let a lack of industry expertise stop him from entering a business. He entered HVAC, automotive chemicals, media, and other industries without prior experience. What matters is not what you already know—it is whether you are willing to become laser-focused on mastering the business you are in. Most people fail at multiple businesses because they scatter their attention across too many things. Keller says if you are going into an industry you do not know, align yourself with people who do, commit to mastering it deeply, and stay long enough to build something durable.

### Step 9: Stay Laser-Focused on One Business at a Time

Keller says the most common bottleneck for businesses stuck at $3 million to $5 million in revenue is the founder's inability to step away. The founder is still making every decision, closing every deal, and managing every employee. Breaking through requires professionalization: building systems, hiring a team that can operate without you, and acting bigger than your current size. Do not start a second business until your first business can run without you. Professionalization is the bridge between a job and an enterprise.

### Step 10: Build Recession-Proof and Depression-Proof Businesses

Keller's most durable businesses have one thing in common: customers cannot imagine life without them. This is what makes a business recession-proof, and if you build it deeply enough, depression-proof. Cash is important, but customer dependency is more important. Focus on building a value proposition so essential that customers stay with you through every economic cycle. The businesses that survive recessions are not the cheapest—they are the most valuable.

### Step 11: Use AI as Leverage, Not as a Substitute for People or Judgment

Keller says AI is extraordinary leverage. It compresses time, reduces costs, and creates opportunity for founders who do not have large capital reserves. But AI will not replace people, and it will not replace founder judgment. The big opportunity is not building an AI-only business or a boring business—it is building a durable business that uses AI as a tool to serve customers better. Use AI to accelerate research, automate repetitive tasks, and amplify your team's output. But the strategic decisions about what to build and why still belong to you.

### Step 12: Learn From Failures By Being Relentless About Engineering a Comeback

When Keller's first tire monitoring product failed catastrophically—every one of the 1,600 devices melted and left his customer with 1,600 flat tires—he had everything to lose. He flew back to Asia, re-engineered the product, begged the customer for another chance, and continued selling the improved version for 10 years. When the FAA showed up at his door because a warehouse manager shipped flammable automotive chemicals on a commercial flight, Keller chose to invest in the employee who made the mistake rather than fire him. That employee still works for the company today. Failure is inevitable. What matters is whether you engineer a comeback and whether you treat people with enough grace to earn their loyalty.

## Examples

### Example 1: The Tire Monitoring Failure That Taught Keller How to Handle Crisis

Keller installed 1,600 tire monitoring devices on a customer's fleet in Phoenix in August. The next day, every seal melted and all 1,600 tires were flat. He was effectively out of business. He flew to Asia, worked with the manufacturer to re-engineer the product, went back to the customer with humility, and continued selling the improved device for 10 years. That failure taught him more about manufacturing quality control, customer relationships, and resilience than any success could have.

### Example 2: Building a Media Publication That Sold Naturally

Keller's first company was a B2B industry trade magazine he started at 24 because he could not afford to advertise his products anywhere else. He created the magazine, filled most of it with his own ads, and grew it for years until the industry figured out he owned it and he had to sell. It was not a glamorous exit, but it was his first—and it came from building a business that served a real need, not from shopping the company to buyers.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Build a business to last, not to sell. Durability attracts buyers naturally.
- ✅ Implement systems and processes that run without you as the founder.
- ✅ Test whether your business can operate without you by taking an unconnected vacation.
- ✅ Hire for character, competency, and chemistry in that order.
- ✅ Partner with people more experienced than you to collapse time.
- ✅ Stay focused on one business until it runs without you before starting another.
- ✅ Build a feedback loop that captures market and employee signals.
- ✅ Use AI as leverage, not as a substitute for founder judgment.

## Keep In Mind

- You do not build a business to sell it. You build it to provide value to customers and a great life for your family.
- Adrenaline is not scalable. Systems and processes are.
- If your business cannot run without you, you have a job, not a business.
- The most common bottleneck for businesses stuck at $3M to $5M is the founder's inability to step away.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not disclose specific acquisition terms, sale prices, or Berkshire Hathaway deal details without authorization.
- Avoid sharing proprietary operational data, customer lists, or financial metrics from any business publicly.
- Do not reveal specific company names or deal structures without consent from all parties involved.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Starting multiple businesses before mastering the first one.
  **Solution:** Stay laser-focused on one business until it runs without you. Diversification preserves wealth; concentration builds it.
- **Problem:** Hiring smart people who damage team chemistry.
  **Solution:** Use the three C's framework—character, competency, and chemistry. Chemistry is non-negotiable.
- **Problem:** Building a business that depends entirely on the founder's daily involvement.
  **Solution:** Systematize every process, document everything, and test operational independence by stepping away.
- **Problem:** Entering an industry without committing to mastery.
  **Solution:** Align with subject matter experts who can teach you the business. Stay long enough to build real expertise.
