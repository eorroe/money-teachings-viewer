# Money Teaching: SOHKP Ran Out Of Money Sell Real Estate To Billionaires

## Overview

This Money Teaching captures Ryan Serhant's blueprint for building a real estate career that generates billions in sales, starting from zero after running out of money in New York City. It teaches that choosing success as an identity—rather than tying yourself to a single career path—allows you to pivot when circumstances change. The teaching also covers how to manufacture confidence without money, why storytelling beats facts in sales, how to project your future self in high-stakes situations, and why continuous learning is the single biggest differentiator between top performers and everyone else.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are starting a sales career with no connections, no experience, and no capital
- When you need to differentiate yourself from hundreds of competitors in the same market
- When you are preparing for a high-stakes audition, interview, or client meeting
- When you want to understand why storytelling is more effective than reciting facts in sales
- When you are deciding whether to align with a brand, brokerage, or mentor
- When you want to build a personal brand that generates leads without traditional advertising spend

## Steps

### Step 1: Choose Success, Not a Specific Career Path

Do not tie your identity to one job, one industry, or one outcome. Ryan Serhant wanted to be an actor, then tried law school, then discovered real estate—but his actual goal was always success, not any specific role. When acting did not work out, he pivoted without losing his identity because he had not built his self-worth around being an actor. Example: Serhant says "I didn't choose real estate. I chose success." That mindset let him pivot from theater to real estate to TV to a sales education business without a crisis of identity.

### Step 2: Manufacture Confidence by Learning Everything Available

If you do not have connections, you can still compete by memorizing all the publicly available information in your field. Confidence comes from knowing the information, not from having the connections. Study listings, market data, neighborhood history, and deal structures until you know more than the people who have been in the business longer. Example: Serhant studied real estate listings and market data obsessively after getting his license. He could not compete on connections, so he competed on knowledge—and knowledge built confidence.

### Step 3: Tell Stories About Properties, Do Not Recite Facts

Facts tell, stories sell. Human beings follow stories, not data. When showing a property, do not list square footage and price per square foot. Tell the story of the home, the neighborhood, and the lifestyle the buyer is buying into. Example: Serhant sold a $15 million Chelsea home by creating a vertically edited property tour that highlighted the narrative of the space—not just its dimensions. A 13-year-old girl saw the video, showed it to her mother, and they bought the home for $3 million. The first point of contact happened through story-driven content.

### Step 4: Project Your Future Self, Not Your Current Self

In high-stakes situations—casting calls, client meetings, partnership negotiations—show people who you are becoming, not who you are today. Businesses and audiences are valued on future potential, not past results. Position yourself as the person you will be in 12 or 24 months. Example: At his Million Dollar Listing audition, Serhant had been a real estate agent for only 14 months. When asked how he got around New York City, he did not describe taking the subway (which he actually did). He said "black car by day, Range Rover by night"—projecting the successful agent he intended to become.

### Step 5: Never Lie, But Always Project

There is a critical distinction between lying and projecting. Lying is stating something false as current fact. Projecting is describing a future reality you intend to create. In casting, sales, and entrepreneurship, people are investing in your future trajectory, not your current balance sheet. Example: Serhant never claimed to own a Range Rover at the time of his audition. He said he could get one—because he knew he would. The casting team cast the person he was projecting, not the person he was that day.

### Step 6: Invest in Continuous Learning Every Single Day

The top athletes in the world spend 95% of their time practicing and only 5% in the actual game. Most people in sales and real estate spend 95% of their time in the game and only 5% practicing. Reverse that ratio. Build a learning routine that covers closing techniques, lead generation, personal brand building, and new media platforms. Example: Serhant built sellit.com—a sales education platform—because he believed in continuous learning for himself and for others. The platform exists to help salespeople at all levels invest in practice, not just performance.

### Step 7: Align With a Strong Brand or Mentor in Your First Three Years

Your first three years in any sales career are graduate school, not a money-making period. If you make money in your first three years, you are lucky—your real job is to learn. Align yourself with a strong brand or mentor who will teach you the craft, expose you to deals, and accelerate your development. Do not be "swipe happy"—jumping between opportunities slows your progress. Example: Serhant advises new agents to find a mentor and a brand that will invest in their education. The first three years are about learning the process, not about commission checks.

### Step 8: Be Passionate About the Process, Not Just the Outcome

You do not have to love every part of the job—crown molding, open houses, cold calls—but you must love the process of making deals and the lifestyle that the career can provide. If you only love the money, you will burn out before you get there. Example: Serhant says you do not have to love crown molding, but you have to love making deals. That passion for the process is what sustains you through the early years when income is inconsistent.

## Examples

### Example 1: From Zero to Billionaire Real Estate Broker

Ryan Serhant moved to New York City to pursue theater. When that did not work out, he ran out of money—his debit card was declined at a grocery store. He got his real estate license in September 2008, right as the stock market crashed and the Great Recession began. He rented apartments, met clients on Craigslist, and told stories instead of reciting facts. Nine years later, he starred in Bravo's Million Dollar Listing New York, built a $400 million to $500 million per year brokerage, and became one of the most recognized real estate figures in the world.

### Example 2: Selling a $60 Million Home Over Video Without Meeting the Buyer

Serhant's firm sold a house for $60 million entirely over video—the buyer never met the agent in person. This was possible because the firm had built a massive online audience through storytelling-driven content. The buyer found the property through social media, not through a traditional showing. This demonstrates the power of building a personal brand that generates inbound demand rather than relying on outbound prospecting.

- ✅ Tell stories about properties and deals—never just recite facts and figures
- ✅ Project your future self in casting calls, client meetings, and partnership discussions
- ✅ Never lie, but always position yourself around future potential
- ✅ Invest in learning every single day—spend more time practicing than performing
- ✅ Align with a strong brand or mentor in your first three years—treat that period as graduate school
- ✅ Be passionate about the deal-making process, not just the income it generates
- ❌ Do not tie your identity to a single job or industry—it will limit your ability to pivot

- ❌ Do not skip the learning phase because you are making money early

## Keep In Mind

- You do not need money, connections, or experience to start in real estate—you need the confidence that comes from preparation and the ability to tell a story
- The first three years in any sales career are graduate school. If you make money during that time, you are lucky—focus on learning, not earning
- Personal brand and social media content generate inbound leads at a fraction of the cost of traditional advertising. The buyers of luxury properties are on social media—so are their children, who frequently influence the purchase decision

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not share client transaction details, property addresses, or deal prices publicly without written consent
- Protect personal brand accounts and content assets—they are the foundation of your lead generation engine
- When showing properties virtually, ensure all parties have verified their identity before sharing sensitive property information

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Tying your identity to a single career path and being unable to pivot when it fails
  **Solution:** Build your identity around success and learning, not around any specific job title or industry
- **Problem:** Competing on connections when you have none
  **Solution:** Compete on knowledge. Memorize every listing, market statistic, and neighborhood detail. Knowledge builds confidence faster than connections.
- **Problem:** Reciting facts instead of telling stories in sales conversations
  **Solution:** Before every showing or pitch, write down the story of the property or deal—not the specs. Practice telling that story out loud.
- **Problem:** Spending 95% of your time performing and only 5% practicing
  **Solution:** Build a daily learning routine. Study top performers, practice scripts, and review deal recordings. The gap between top performers and average performers is almost entirely practice time.
