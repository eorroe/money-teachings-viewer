# Money Teaching Title: He Had $7,000 Left, Then Sold His Companies for $1.1 Billion

## Overview

Blake Johnson bootstrapped multiple companies from scratch and exited them for over a billion dollars without ever raising outside capital. This teaching outlines his formula for building acquisition-ready businesses, emphasizing fundamentals, discipline, and customer economics over growth-at-all-costs.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When building a business with the intention of selling it one day
- When deciding whether to raise capital or bootstrap
- When entering a new market and need to quickly assess viability
- When scaling a team rapidly without sacrificing culture or operations
- When negotiating with private equity or strategic buyers

## Steps

### Step 1: Master the Fundamentals First

Blake's core belief: business is simple math. Revenue minus expenses equals profit. If you do not understand your cost of goods, customer acquisition cost (CAC), overhead, and unit economics, you will fail. Strip away complexity and focus on the 10 questions that reveal whether a business is viable.

### Step 2: Bootstrap Instead of Raising Capital

Blake never raised outside capital. He argues that spending your own money creates discipline; spending other people's money makes you lazy. Perform like a trapeze artist without a net. Force yourself to make every dollar count, and you will build a stronger, more resilient business.

### Step 3: Target a Big Addressable Market

Only pursue opportunities where the total addressable market (TAM) is massive. You want a universal yes—whether you walk into a hospital, mosque, synagogue, church, or school, the answer to your core value proposition should be yes. Avoid niche products with tiny markets.

### Step 4: Optimize Customer Acquisition

Your acquisition cost is the billion-dollar question. Marketing is constantly changing, but the fundamentals remain. Place small bets across multiple channels, double down on what works, and obsess over converting leads once they knock on your door. Most businesses waste the opportunity after the lead arrives.

### Step 5: Build for Acquisition From Day One

Blake built every company to be sold. Understand what private equity and strategic buyers look for: clean books, recurring revenue, strong leadership, and room to grow. Keep your accounting, contracts, and data immaculate. Avoid cutting corners on taxes, write-offs, or legal structure.

### Step 6: Hire Top-Down and Build a Band of Gypsies

Start with key leaders (CMO, CPO, Head of Operations) rather than filling every role at the entry level. Blake brings about 50 percent of his core team from venture to venture. These "traveling gypsies" have meaningful equity and share your culture. Their skills transfer across businesses; hire for fundamentals, not industry-specific jargon.

### Step 7: Balance the Wings of the Airplane

Growth engines (marketing and sales) are the thrust, but everything else is the wing. Supply chain, personnel, customer service, and accounting must scale in sync. If one wing is heavier than the other, the plane crashes. Run fire drills before you need them.

### Step 8: Compete on Brand, Not Just Category

When entering a market with established giants, let them spend the money educating consumers. Then compete on brand level. Like drafting behind a semi on the freeway, you benefit from their drag. Differentiate through positioning, messaging, and customer experience.

## Examples

### Example 1: Starting Bite While Working at LA Fitness

Blake did not quit his corporate job to start Bite. He built it part-time for four years while maintaining his executive role, reaching $62 million in revenue before going full-time. This reduced risk and allowed him to validate the business before making the leap.

### Example 2: Pivoting During COVID-19

When the pandemic hit, Blake ran drills on how to respond if acquisition costs spiked. Competitors SmileDirect and Candid failed because they were not prepared. Bite pivoted to 3D printing medical equipment for first responders, kept employees paid, and gained market share as competitors collapsed.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Keep business and personal finances completely separate
- ✅ Maintain clean books, CRM data, and legal contracts from day one
- ✅ Hire specialists for key roles rather than generalists
- ✅ Obsess over converting leads after they arrive, not just generating them
- ✅ Run scenario drills before crises hit
- ✅ Treat every business as a platform that could be acquired

## Keep In Mind

- Your first company will look like a Ferrari with two sick mice in a hamster wheel—you will make mistakes
- The best entrepreneurs are curious but not overthinkers; have a direction, not a rigid plan
- Selling when you can, not when you want to, is the right timing strategy
- Perspective breadth creates happiness; narrow perspective creates fear

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never mix personal and business funds; this destroys enterprise value during due diligence
- File taxes correctly and avoid aggressive write-offs that could trigger audits
- Protect customer data and proprietary formulas with legal agreements
- Maintain proper insurance on products and operations

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Raising capital and losing financial discipline
  **Solution:** Bootstrap as long as possible. If you must raise, raise enough to afford failure, but remember that spending your own money builds the right instincts
- **Problem:** Overcomplicating business assessment with theoretical problems
  **Solution:** Stick to 10 key questions about market size, acquisition cost, and unit economics. Avoid rabbit holes
- **Problem:** Hiring friends or family without assessing role fit
  **Solution:** Treat the business like a professional sports team. A great shortstop does not automatically make a great pitcher
