# Money Teaching Title: He Killed 600 Men, Then Built the Most Famous Gym on Earth

## Overview

Edward Connors served in Vietnam as a military intelligence officer, transitioned to architecture, and ultimately bought the original Gold's Gym in 1979, growing it into over 700 locations before selling for $44 million. This Money Teaching distills how he survived combat by embracing minimalism, spotted opportunity before others did, bought failing businesses at the right price, built a brand through community and vertical integration, and stayed adaptable across decades of industry change. Apply these lessons when buying an existing business, scaling a franchise, building a fitness or community brand, or reinventing yourself after a major life transition.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When acquiring a failing business and turning it around
- When scaling a physical location-based business through franchising
- When building a brand around community and lifestyle rather than just a product
- When reinventing yourself after military service or a major career change
- When seeking to vertically integrate and own multiple revenue streams

## Steps

### Step 1: Embrace Minimalism and Resilience

Ed survived 21 months in Vietnam with only a sleeping bag and a duffel bag. He learned that the veneer of civilization is thin and that people can survive and adapt with very little. When you face scarcity or uncertainty, strip away the nonessential and focus on what actually matters: shelter, food, and purpose.

### Step 2: Visualize What Others Miss

Ed walked into Gold's Gym in the late 1970s and thought, "God, it would be neat to own this." He did the same with a house in Venice. Visualization is not daydreaming; it is a practical tool for spotting opportunity. Train yourself to look at spaces, businesses, and situations and ask what they could become rather than what they are.

### Step 3: Buy Businesses at the Right Price for the Right Reasons

When Ed bought Gold's Gym, it was failing because the owner's wife had died and he did not want to deal with the headaches. Ed knew the three pillars of a gym are location, capital, and management. If you can buy a business cheaply because the owner is burnt out, and you believe you are a better manager with access to capital, the purchase makes sense.

### Step 4: Negotiate Creative Deals Instead of Cash-Heavy Ones

When Ed wanted advertising in Joe Weider's magazine, he did not pay for it. He offered Weider access to film at Gold's Gym in exchange for ad space. This gave Weider the Southern California bodybuilding aesthetic he needed, and it gave Ed priceless exposure. Always look for value exchange rather than cash outlay.

### Step 5: Build Community as a Competitive Moat

Gold's Gym grew from one location to 700+ because Ed built an annual convention in Las Vegas where gym owners could learn from each other. He brought 537 house guests through his home over 45 years, mentoring bodybuilders, wrestlers, and entrepreneurs. Community creates loyalty that no marketing budget can buy.

### Step 6: Innovate Continuously or Die

Ed invented rubber gym flooring because the turf they were using wore out. He pioneered co-ed fitness when most gyms were men-only or segregated by gender. He introduced cardio theater when big-screen TVs became available. Industries evolve; if you are not innovating, you are falling behind.

### Step 7: Vertically Integrate Whenever Possible

Ed did not just own gyms; he owned the real estate, the clothing line, the conventions, and the training systems. He insisted on owning real estate because bad landlords can kill a business. Every additional revenue stream you own increases your control and your margins.

### Step 8: Stay Positive and Belief-Driven

Ed says the people who succeeded under his mentorship believed in themselves more than he did. He remained relentlessly positive, refused to let people quit on themselves, and used encouragement as a management tool. Negativity destroys teams; positivity builds them.

## Examples

### Example 1: Buying a Failing Local Restaurant

You discover a restaurant in your neighborhood that is closing because the owner is burnt out after 20 years. The location is prime, the equipment is paid for, but the owner wants out. If you have access to capital and believe you can run operations better, buy it at a discount rather than starting from scratch. Ed bought Gold's Gym under almost identical circumstances.

### Example 2: Building a Fitness Brand Through Community

You run a CrossFit box with 50 loyal members. Instead of only collecting membership fees, host a quarterly throwdown competition, sell branded apparel, and create a private Facebook group for members to share wins. Ed built Gold's Gym into a global brand through conventions, merch, and mentorship, not just equipment.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Survive scarcity by embracing minimalism
- ✅ Visualize opportunity before others see it
- ✅ Buy failing businesses at the right price for the right reasons
- ✅ Negotiate creative deals that preserve cash
- ✅ Build community as your competitive moat
- ✅ Innovate continuously to stay ahead of trends
- ✅ Own the real estate and multiple revenue streams
- ✅ Lead with relentless positivity and belief in your team
- ❌ Do not buy a business without understanding why it is failing
- ❌ Do not rely on cash payments when value exchange is possible
- ❌ Do not let your gym or brand become stagnant
- ❌ Do not underestimate the power of community

## Keep In Mind

- Ed killed over 600 people as a military intelligence officer in Vietnam; he compartmentalized that experience and used it to fuel resilience
- He discovered John Cena in a Venice Beach gym parking lot and convinced him to pursue wrestling
- Ed sold Gold's Gym for $44 million after nearly asking Vince McMahon for $40 million and being told no
- He wore a blue blazer every day and was known as "The Blue Blazer" in the fitness industry
- Ed's house guests included 102 pro bodybuilders, 70 gym owners, and 23 supplement company founders

## Security & Safety Notes

- Vet business sellers thoroughly before acquiring their company
- Ensure proper licensing and insurance before opening a fitness facility
- Protect member data and payment information in your gym management system
- Consult an attorney before signing franchise agreements

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Buying a business without understanding why it is failing
  **Solution:** Ask direct questions about the owner's motivation and inspect all financial records
- **Problem:** Relying on a single revenue stream
  **Solution:** Add apparel, events, and real estate to diversify income
- **Problem:** Losing community trust due to poor facility maintenance
  **Solution:** Inspect locations regularly and set non-negotiable standards
- **Problem:** Becoming obsolete because the market shifted
  **Solution:** Innovate before you have to; study emerging trends and test them early
