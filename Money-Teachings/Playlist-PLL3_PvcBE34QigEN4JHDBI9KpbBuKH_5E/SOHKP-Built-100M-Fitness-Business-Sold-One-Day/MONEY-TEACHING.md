# Money Teaching: Build and Exit a $100M Fitness Empire by Going All-In and Planning Your Succession

## Overview

A guide to bootstrapping a fitness empire from $175,000 in savings, scaling to over 100 locations, and exiting for a nine-figure payday by building community, hiring for culture, and succession planning before you ever go to market. Anne Mahlum started Solid Core with no plan B, passed out flyers at marathons, and refused a $75,000 offer for 30% of the business before her first studio opened. She raised multiple rounds of private equity, took money off the table in every round, never sold controlling interest, and built a leadership team so strong that the business could run without her. This Money Teaching teaches you how to bootstrap with conviction, scale brick-and-mortar with precision, exit with grace, and preserve wealth after the sale.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you need to bootstrap a brick-and-mortar or fitness business with minimal capital
- When you are planning an exit and must build succession before you go to market
- When the user asks about scaling physical locations, raising private equity, or managing wealth after a liquidity event

## Steps

### Step 1: Go All-In With No Plan B

Mahlum put every dollar she had—$175,000 in savings—into Solid Core before her first studio opened. She turned down a $75,000 investment for 30% of the business because accepting it would have signaled self-doubt. If you have a plan B, you will use it. Passing out flyers at triathlons, negotiating contractor payment terms, and living on ramen is not sustainable unless you are all-in. Make the decision that burning your boats is the only option, and your brain will find solutions you did not know existed.

### Step 2: Build Community Before You Build a Workout

Solid Core clients kept coming back not because the workout was the best in the world, but because they felt seen, celebrated, and known. Mahlum instilled a rule: every coach must say every client's name, in a positive association, during every class. She hired coaches based on personality, humor, and warmth—not fitness certifications. Clients will forgive a technical mistake, but they will not return if they feel invisible or unwelcome. Design every touchpoint of your business around making people feel like they belong.

### Step 3: Scale by Saturation, Not by Scattering

Solid Core does not open a new market unless it can open five studios within a 15-to-20-mile radius. Mahlum learned that brand equity compounds when customers see multiple locations. A second studio in the same city costs less to open and fills faster because word of mouth is already established. Before you expand to a new city, prove that your model works in one neighborhood, then saturate that neighborhood before you move.

### Step 4: Use Momentum to Stay Ahead of Competitors

When Solid Core's flywheel started spinning, Mahlum opened 32 locations in a single year by working backward from the goal. She frontloaded all major lease terms into the letter of intent so negotiations did not drag on for 45 days. She started permit applications the moment the LOI was signed, not after the lease was finalized. While competitors were still picking architects, she was opening doors. Identify the bottlenecks in your expansion model and eliminate them before your competitors even know they exist.

### Step 5: Hire Attitude and Teach the Rest

Ninety-nine percent of Solid Core's coaches had full-time jobs outside fitness. They coached six classes a week because they loved the community, not because it was their career. Mahlum recruited from her existing client base because those people already believed in the brand and embodied its energy. Hire people who love your product and your people, then train them on the rest. Technical skills can be taught; genuine warmth and charisma cannot.

### Step 6: Plan Your Exit Before You Need to Exit

You cannot exit a business successfully if you are not succession planning. Mahlum built a leadership team so strong that private equity was interviewing the entire team, not just her. She promoted a CEO and moved into a chair role two years before the sale so she could adjust to life after the founder title. If you want to exit with grace, invest in your people, document your systems, and make yourself replaceable before you ever speak to a buyer.

### Step 7: Take Money Off the Table in Every Round

Mahlum raised three to four rounds of private equity. In every round, she took some money off the table—not because she needed it, but because it changed her psychology. When you have already taken $15 million off the table, you will not sell yourself short on the big exit out of fear or desperation. She never sold controlling interest. Even when she owned only 30% of the business, she remained the controlling party of the board. Keep control of the timeline, or someone else will set it for you.

### Step 8: Invest Wealth Like a Long-Term Game, Not a Lottery Win

After the exit, Mahlum allocated her wealth across equities, real estate, private equity, direct startup investments, and hedge funds. She does not sell assets to fund her lifestyle; she borrows against them to avoid taxes. The secret to preserving wealth is leverage, not liquidation. If your money is sitting in a savings account, the bank is making 10% on it and paying you half a percent. Invest in assets that compound, and let your wealth work while you play volleyball.

## Examples

### Example 1: The $75,000 Offer She Turned Down

Before opening her first studio, Mahlum received a $75,000 investment offer for 30% of Solid Core from a board member who had seen her succeed with Back on My Feet. She nearly took it until she realized that accepting the deal would signal self-doubt and erode her confidence when things got hard. She went all-in on her own instead. Lesson: taking easy money too early can cost you more in confidence and control than it is worth.

### Example 2: The 32-Studio Expansion Year

Mahlum used a massive wall chart to map backward from the goal of opening 32 studios. She frontloaded lease terms, started permits early, and kept multiple real estate negotiations running simultaneously so that one deal falling through would not derail the year. While competitors were still negotiating leases, she was opening doors. Lesson: momentum is a moat. Once you are moving faster than everyone else, no one can catch you.

### Example 3: Taking Money Off the Table

Mahlum raised $18 million in her first round, put $12 million back into the business, and took $6 million off the table. Many founders put every dollar back in and feel forced to sell when the first good offer arrives because they have no liquidity. Because she already had skin in the game, she could walk away from deals that did not meet her nine-figure exit requirement. Lesson: liquidity gives you leverage. Take it early and often.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Go all-in with no plan B; burning your boats forces creativity and resilience
- ✅ Build community before you build a workout; people return where they feel seen
- ✅ Open multiple studios in a radius before you expand to a new city
- ✅ Use momentum as a moat; work backward from goals and eliminate bottlenecks early
- ✅ Hire from your client base; people who love your brand will defend it
- ✅ Plan succession before you need to exit; build a team that can run the business without you
- ✅ Take money off the table in every round so you never feel pressured to sell
- ✅ Never sell controlling interest if you want to decide your own exit timeline
- ✅ Invest wealth in long-term compounding assets and borrow against them to fund lifestyle
- ❌ Do not take dumb money just because you are running low on cash
- ❌ Do not expand to new markets before you have saturated your home market
- ❌ Do not hire based on credentials alone; personality and warmth matter more in fitness
- ❌ Do not let your identity be tied to the company; build a life outside the business before you exit

## Keep In Mind

- Easy choices create a hard life. Hard choices create an easy life. Wake up early, do the hard work, and delay gratification.
- The secret to wealth is not selling assets; it is borrowing against them. Selling triggers taxes. Borrowing does not.
- If you are not having fun, you are doing the wrong thing. Business should feel like play, not punishment.
- Your worst boss is your best teacher. Pay attention to what they did wrong so you do not repeat it.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not put all your savings into a single business unless you are willing to lose it. Mahlum did, but she had no dependents and a high risk tolerance. Most people should keep a safety net.
- Do not ignore succession planning. If you are hit by a bus or decide to leave tomorrow, the business should not collapse.
- Do not commingle personal and business funds. Clean accounting is non-negotiable for private equity and future buyers.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You put all your savings into the business and run out of cash before you reach profitability.
  **Solution:** Negotiate payment terms with contractors and vendors to extend your runway. Keep some personal liquidity if possible.

- **Problem:** You scale too fast, open studios in new markets before demand is proven, and burn through capital.
  **Solution:** Saturate your home market first. Open five locations in a 20-mile radius before you consider a new city.

- **Problem:** You sell controlling interest in a funding round and lose the ability to decide when and how to exit.
  **Solution:** Never sell more than 49% of the company if you want to remain the controlling party. Control the board, control the timeline.

- **Problem:** You exit and have no plan for your identity, purpose, or wealth, and you spiral into depression or reckless spending.
  **Solution:** Build a succession plan and a post-exit life plan before you sign the term sheet. Know what you will do, who you will be, and how you will invest your money before the deal closes.
