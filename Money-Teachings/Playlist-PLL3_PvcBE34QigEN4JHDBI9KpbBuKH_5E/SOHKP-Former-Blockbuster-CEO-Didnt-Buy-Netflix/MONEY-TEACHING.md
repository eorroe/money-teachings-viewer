# Why Blockbuster Did Not Buy Netflix and What Every Leader Can Learn From It

## Overview

James Keyes served as CEO of both 7-Eleven and Blockbuster. He explains why Blockbuster did not buy Netflix when given the opportunity, how over-leveraging and the 2008 financial crisis destroyed the company, and the three leadership principles—change, confidence, and clarity—that he believes are essential for navigating disruption. This Money Teaching covers the disciplines of strategic decision-making under uncertainty, cash flow management, brand building, and creating a culture that outlasts market shifts. These lessons apply to every leader running a company facing technological disruption or industry transformation.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When your industry is being disrupted by a new technology or competitor
- When you are deciding whether to acquire a potential competitor or build the capability internally
- When you are managing company debt and need to understand refinancing timing
- When you are building a brand and need to understand what creates lasting value
- When you need to lead a team through a major strategic pivot or crisis
- When you are deciding whether to stay in a role or move to a new opportunity

## Steps

### Step 1: Reframe Change as Opportunity, Not Threat

Keyes teaches that change equals opportunity, and that is the core job of a CEO. Blockbuster had the chance to acquire Netflix in 2000 for $50 million and again in 2007 when Netflix approached them. In both cases, the deal did not make strategic sense at the time because Netflix was still running a DVD-by-mail business that Blockbuster could replicate on its own. The real threat Netflix represented was not the DVD model—it was the shift to streaming and original content, neither of which was predictable in 2000 or 2007. When evaluating a disruptive competitor, ask: what capability do they have that we cannot easily replicate, and how long will it take for that capability to become dominant?

### Step 2: Build Your Brand Around the Product, Not Marketing Alone

Keyes explains that Apple's brand was not built through marketing—it was built through product innovation. The original Mac was flawed, and Apple's brand was not great until the iPhone transformed the customer experience. A brand is only as valuable as the products and services behind it. Focus your energy on product quality, customer experience, and operational excellence before investing in brand marketing. If the product does not deliver, no amount of brand building will sustain the business.

### Step 3: Refinance Debt Early, Not When You Are Forced To

Blockbuster had $1 billion in debt from a 2004 Viacom spin-off, with $300 million due in 2009. When the financial crisis hit, banks refused to refinance, and Blockbuster could not access the capital it needed to execute its digital transformation. Keyes says the single biggest mistake was not refinancing earlier when the terms were favorable. If you have variable-rate debt or debt coming due within the next 3 to 5 years, refinance when markets are stable, not when you are under pressure. Cash flow conditions can change rapidly, and a refinancing window that exists today may vanish in a crisis.

### Step 4: Treat Cash as Your Lifeblood and Manage It Obsessively

Keyes says cash is always king. Debt is a useful tool as long as the business's cash flow can support it comfortably. The danger is over-leverage: borrowing more than the business can service under stressed conditions. Build a cash reserve, stress-test your cash flow against a 30% revenue decline scenario, and never take on debt that requires perfect market conditions to service. When the financial crisis hit, Blockbuster had improved cash flow but not enough cushion to survive the credit freeze.

### Step 5: Choose Your Battles With Conformity—Do Not Let the Crowd Dictate Your ceiling

Keyes grew up in a factory town where working harder was punished because it raised expectations for everyone else. In the corporate world, he found the same pressure: do not work too hard, do not stand out, do not challenge the way things are done. He refused to conform at every stage. As a McDonald's employee, he ran extra laps while cleaning the parking lot because he believed hard work would be rewarded. As a 7-Eleven executive, he pushed for pay-at-the-pump technology against the entire management team's opposition. The data eventually proved him right, but only because he was willing to be the outlier. Refuse to let organizational pressure force you into mediocrity.

### Step 6: Stay in a Role as Long as You Are Learning, Even If Promotion Is Slow

Keyes stayed at 7-Eleven for 20 years despite multiple moments when he wanted to leave. He stayed because he was still learning, and the cumulative knowledge he gained made him a better CEO than any external hire could have been. Jumping between companies for faster promotion often means missing the deeper organizational learning that only comes from staying long enough to see the long-term consequences of decisions. Define your own criteria for staying or leaving, and make sure it is based on your growth, not external benchmarks.

### Step 7: Build a Culture of Performance Starting at the Top

Culture is determined by what leadership tolerates, not what it says in a mission statement. Keyes observed that when a foreman allowed workers to cheat the timeclock and leave early, the entire shop culture normalized underperformance. At 7-Eleven, he fought against that same dynamic by insisting on performance accountability. If you want a high-performance culture, model it yourself, reward it visibly, and do not tolerate low standards from high-performers who are bad cultural fits. Hire people who raise the average, not people who lower it.

### Step 8: Prioritize Customer Convenience Over Internal Margin Assumptions

When Keyes proposed pay-at-the-pump technology at 7-Eleven, the management team opposed it because they believed gas was only there to lure customers inside to buy high-margin snacks. Keyes ran a controlled experiment in 20 stores: the stores with pay-at-the-pump had 30% more traffic at the pumps and 20% more inside sales. Customers using pay-at-the-pump still came inside for a Slurpee. The data overruled the internal assumption. Always test assumptions about customer behavior with data rather than internal logic.

### Step 9: Do Not Second-Guess Historical Decisions With Hindsight Bias

When asked whether Blockbuster should have bought Netflix, Keyes says no—at the time, Netflix was a DVD-by-mail company Blockbuster could easily replicate. The real question is why Blockbuster did not build its own streaming and content exclusivity strategy sooner, which would have left Netflix with nothing to stream. Second-guessing past decisions is easy and usually wrong. Focus instead on what you would do differently with the same information you had at the time, and build decision frameworks that reduce the likelihood of similar errors in the future.

### Step 10: Innovate Continuously or Accept That You Will Eventually Be Disrupted

Netflix survived Blockbuster not because it was smarter, but because it innovated repeatedly: DVDs by mail, then a recommendation engine, then streaming, then original content. Each time Blockbuster matched a Netflix move, Netflix innovated again. Keyes says the same pattern applies to every company: if you stop innovating, you will eventually be overtaken by a competitor who does. Build innovation into your organization's rhythm, not as a one-time initiative. Reward experimentation, tolerate intelligent failures, and never assume your current advantage is permanent.

## Examples

### Example 1: The Pay-at-the-Pump Experiment That Proved Customer Convenience Wins

When Keyes proposed pay-at-the-pump technology at 7-Eleven, the management team opposed it unanimously, arguing it would destroy inside sales. Keyes tested the technology in 20 stores instead of fighting ideologically. Six months later, the data showed 30% more pump traffic and 20% more inside sales. The convenience of pay-at-the-pump brought more people into the store, not fewer. The experiment became a company-wide rollout and proved that customer convenience is always worth pursuing even when internal assumptions say otherwise.

### Example 2: Why Blockbuster Could Not Just Buy Its Way Out of Disruption

Blockbuster had a billion dollars of debt from the Viacom spin-off, with $300 million due in 2009. When the financial crisis hit and credit markets froze, Blockbuster could not refinance and could not fund its digital transformation simultaneously. Even if Blockbuster had bought Netflix, the debt load would have made it impossible to invest in streaming infrastructure at the scale Netflix did. The lesson is that financial structure determines strategic optionality. Take on debt only when you are confident you can survive a credit freeze.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Reframe every disruption as an opportunity to respond better than your competitors
- ✅ Refinance debt when markets are favorable, not when you are under pressure
- ✅ Test customer assumptions with data, not internal logic
- ✅ Stay in roles as long as you are learning, not just for external status
- ✅ Model and reward performance from the top to build culture
- ✅ Innovate continuously; never assume your current advantage is permanent

## Keep In Mind

- Conformity will kill your individual potential. Fight the pressure to be average.
- Education gives you optionality. A narrow shortcut today can become a dead end tomorrow.
- Hindsight is 2020. Judge past decisions by the information available at the time.
- Cash flow is everything. Debt is fine only if the business can service it under stress.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not disclose specific debt terms, refinancing amounts, or financial covenants from past roles without legal clearance.
- Avoid sharing proprietary strategic plans, internal meeting details, or board discussions from former employers.
- Do not reveal specific acquisition terms or deal structures from Blockbuster or 7-Eleven transactions without authorization.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Second-guessing past decisions with hindsight bias instead of learning from the decision-making process.
  **Solution:** Review past decisions based on the information available at the time, not on outcomes you could not have predicted.
- **Problem:** Taking on debt that requires favorable market conditions to service.
  **Solution:** Stress-test your cash flow against a 30% revenue decline before taking on new debt, and refinance before you are forced to.
- **Problem:** Assuming customer behavior based on internal assumptions rather than testing with real data.
  **Solution:** Run small controlled experiments before rolling out changes company-wide.
- **Problem:** Matching a competitor's move instead of finding a better next innovation.
  **Solution:** Ask what the competitor's move reveals about the underlying customer need, and design a solution that is better, not identical.
