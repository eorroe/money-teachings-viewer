## Steps

### Step 1: Use the 5630 Method to Discover Your True Content Niche

Pick five topics or content styles that genuinely interest you. Make six pieces of content in each of those five buckets — 30 total. Post one per day for 30 days. Do not delete anything. Do not look at the data until the 30 days are over. At the end of the month, evaluate: which bucket energized you the most? Which one performed best? Focus month two on the winning buckets and abandon the rest. The goal is action, not perfection — you cannot discover your niche by thinking about it; you must create your way to the answer.

### Step 2: Accept That You Will Make 100 Bad Videos Before You Make a Great One

Quantity breeds quality. Every successful creator has a graveyard of bad content behind them. You cannot skip the 100 bad videos — they are the training ground where you develop your voice, your timing, and your understanding of what your audience actually wants. Post consistently even when the content feels mediocre. If you post 400 times and still have under 50 followers, keep posting — eventually one video will change everything, but it will only come if you stay in the game.

### Step 3: Engineer Virality by Designing Your Comment Section in Reverse

Never make a video without knowing exactly what you want the comment section to look like. Write the comments you want to see into the video itself — plant prompts, questions, and controversial or relatable moments that naturally trigger those comments. Small details are often the difference between one million views and 10 million views: an anchor object shown early that pays off later, a dog in the background, a person walking by at the wrong moment. These details create open loops that keep people watching until the end and compel them to comment.

### Step 4: Master the Hook — It Is the First Thing That Matters

The hook is the first 2–3 seconds of your video, and it is the single most important element for stopping the scroll. You have approximately one second to interrupt the viewer's pattern and create curiosity. Use an open loop — ask a question, show something unexpected, or introduce a tension that demands resolution. The hook is not the whole story; it is a promise that the rest of the video will deliver on. If nobody watches past the first three seconds, nothing else matters.

### Step 5: Tell Stories Using Open Loops — Do Not Reveal the Payoff Early

Apply age-old storytelling principles to short-form video. Every good story — every movie, every sitcom, every viral video — uses the same structure: a hook, a curiosity gap, rising tension, and a delayed payoff. If Ross and Rachel had stayed together in Season 2 of Friends, there would be no 10-season show. If Liam Neeson had rescued his daughter halfway through Taken, we would have stopped watching. Plant an anchor early, do not scratch the itch until the very end, and viewers will watch through to completion — the single biggest signal to every algorithm.

### Step 6: Do Not Reinvent the Wheel — Innovate Within Existing Formats

Street interviews, prank videos, and challenge content have all been done before. You do not need to invent a new format — you need to become an innovator within an existing one. Study the top-performing videos in your niche every day for at least one hour. Understand what worked six months ago, what works now, and what rules you can break while keeping the core structure intact. What worked six months ago will not work today — the algorithm and audience tastes are always evolving. You must be a perpetual student of the format.

### Step 7: Build a Relatable, Multi-Dimensional Personal Brand

Your audience does not want a one-dimensional caricature — they want a real human being. If you are a real estate investor, also post about your family, your failures, your bad investments, and what you are learning. People connect with authenticity, not perfection. The most successful personal brands have multiple content pillars: your expertise, your personal life, your failures, your values. When you abandon the audience that got you to where you are — like the creator who quit his corporate job content and lost his entire following — you will lose the people who believed in you first.

### 8: Protect Your Creative Freedom — Do Not Sign Away Your Rights Too Early

Hold out on agency, management, and record label deals for as long as possible. Addy signed away commercial rights to her name for life in a bad record deal that she later described as "pretty unconscionable." Partnerships are very difficult even between best friends — do not live with your best friend, and do not sign away your creative or commercial rights before you fully understand the deal. Bet on yourself for as long as you can. If you do take a deal, negotiate fair outs and never do an all-in arrangement. The worst decision you can make is an emotional one made when you are getting your first wave of attention.

### Step 9: Use Facebook as a Primary Distribution Platform — Do Not Sleep On It

Most creators ignore Facebook and focus exclusively on TikTok or Instagram — this is a massive mistake. Facebook's algorithm rewards consistency and has built-in monetization tools that other platforms do not match. Addy's network has generated over a quarter million dollars from a single Facebook video that took less than an hour to make, posted 40+ times across Meta properties. Facebook still rewards long-form, high-retention content. Post there consistently and you will find an underserved audience with massive monetization potential.

### Step 10: Build Systems That Turn Your Content Operation Into a Repeatable Business

At peak, Addy's team was producing 18 Facebook videos per day with 15 full-time actors and creators. Build systems that allow you to produce content consistently without burning out — hire a team, create content templates, and build a network of creators who can execute your vision. Your personal brand and your content machine are two separate things; one without the other caps your growth. Invest in building the machine so you can focus on strategy and creative direction.

## Examples

### Example 1: Going From Bankruptcy to 1.3 Billion Monthly Views

After a bad record deal left Addy bankrupt and feeling trapped, she started making funny videos for a meme network. When her footage was corrupted at 1AM and her boyfriend told her to go play with her backyard chickens, she improvised a video putting her chickens — named Ellen DeGeneres, Oprah Henfrey, Chuck Norris, and Shakira Shakira — in a bathtub. The video got 19 million views overnight and over 100,000 followers. Authenticity and leaning into her genuine sense of humor became her guiding creative principle.

### Example 2: Doubling a Client's Revenue by Making Him More Relatable

Addy's client Brian Mark was stuck at $6M/year as a business coach for fitness trainers because his content was one-dimensional — just yelling aggressively at the camera. By spending one hour a week with him and adding content pillars that showed his humor, his relationship with his wife, and his sales systems, she doubled his following and took him from $6M to $10M/year in under six months. The lesson: people connect with multi-dimensional humans, not caricatures.

## Keep In Mind

- Virality is not luck — it is a formula. Every video over 100 million views shares the same structural elements
- Attention is the new currency — where attention goes, revenue flows
- The algorithm rewards watch time, retention, and engagement — not fancy production or high budget
- People want to follow humans, not brands — relatability and authenticity outperform perfection every time
- Your first viral video changes your life by showing you what is possible, but consistency compounds that change over time

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never sign away commercial or intellectual property rights to your name, likeness, or content without a lawyer reviewing the contract
- Do not share exact view counts, revenue figures, or client deal specifics publicly — this data can be exploited by competitors
- When building a content team, have clear contracts that define content ownership, usage rights, and confidentiality obligations
- Protect your personal information and family details when sharing your life online — draw clear boundaries between your brand and your private life

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Analysis paralysis — spending months deciding what to post instead of just posting
  **Solution:** Use the 5630 method and commit to 30 days of posting before evaluating — action creates clarity
- **Problem:** Copying other creators instead of developing an authentic voice
  **Solution:** Study viral videos to learn the structure, but infuse every piece of content with your genuine personality and experience
- **Problem:** Chasing trends instead of building a sustainable personal brand
  **Solution:** Prioritize ethics over optics — do not copy what is working for someone else; find your own lane and own it
- **Problem:** Abandoning the audience that got you traction by pivoting to a new identity too fast
  **Solution:** How you get someone is how you keep them — take your audience with you when you evolve, do not leave them behind
- **Problem:** Signing an all-in management or agency deal before you understand your own leverage
  **Solution:** Bet on yourself for as long as possible; if you do take a deal, always negotiate fair outs and retain creative control
