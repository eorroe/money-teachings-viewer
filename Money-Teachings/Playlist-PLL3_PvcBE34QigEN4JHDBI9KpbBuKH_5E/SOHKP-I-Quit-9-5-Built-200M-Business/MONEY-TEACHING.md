# Money Teaching Title: I Quit My 9-5, Then Built a $200M Business

## Overview

Paul Hedrick left a stable consulting job in New York to found Takova, now a $200 million direct-to-consumer western boot and apparel brand with 30 retail locations. This Money Teaching distills his path from Harvard graduate to entrepreneur, including why he rejected business school, how he used rejection as fuel, the exact playbook he used to survive COVID with 10 signed leases, and how he built a brand that commands loyalty. Apply these teachings when starting a consumer brand, scaling retail, or transitioning from corporate employment to entrepreneurship.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When starting a consumer brand in an underpenetrated category
- When scaling from online-only to brick-and-mortar retail
- When facing a market crisis that threatens your business
- When building a team that can execute a long-term vision
- When learning to think like both a creative and a financial operator

## Steps

### Step 1: Use Rejection as a Signal, Not a Stop Sign

Paul was rejected by Harvard and Stanford for business school, rejected from his first-choice jobs three times, and faced near-failure during COVID when he had 10 signed retail leases. Each rejection forced him to think differently about his path. When you face repeated no's, sit back and ask what unique opportunity the rejection is pointing you toward.

### Step 2: Build Hard Skills First, Then Soft Skills

Paul credits his management consulting background with teaching him to think in 30,000-foot and 1,000-foot views simultaneously. Hard skills like Excel, financial modeling, and operational analysis are table stakes. Soft skills like sitting in a room with executives twice your age and delivering value without faking it are equally essential. Combine both to become a credible leader.

### Step 3: Start With a Product You Love and Know

Paul did not pick boots because market research told him to; he picked them because he wore ostrich boots in New York and realized there was no approachable way for outsiders to buy high-quality western footwear. Build something you would use yourself. Authenticity in the founder creates authenticity in the brand.

### Step 4: Validate Before You Scale Marketing

Paul launched Takova with a thousand email addresses and no marketing budget. He sold boots from the trunk of his car at farmers markets before investing in advertising. Only after proving that people would buy did he hire agencies and build an in-house marketing team. Let the product prove itself before paying to acquire customers.

### Step 5: Hire for the Long Game, Not Just the Gap

Paul's first hire was someone he got along with and who could take over growth and digital marketing. Later he learned to hire people better than him at specific functions and make himself redundant. The goal is not to do everything yourself but to build a team that can execute the vision without you.

### Step 6: Protect the Brand at Every Touchpoint

Paul built Takova on "radical hospitality" as a core value. Every interaction, from website design to warehouse smell to store greeting, is designed to make the customer feel special. Protect your brand by ensuring your team understands that small details like thread color, box quality, and employee warmth are not optional.

### Step 7: Plan for Crisis Before It Happens

When COVID hit and Paul had 10 signed retail leases, he created a three-part game plan within 48 hours: cut costs by 60% overnight, keep all leases but delay openings, and introduce new products to drive repeat business. Most brands cut orders and worked through stale inventory; Takova launched new products and grew $10 million in profit in 2020. Have a crisis playbook ready.

### Step 8: Keep Your Eye on the Three Growth Levers

Paul says consumer businesses have only three levers: number of customers, number of products per customer, and number of channels. Break your business into these three components and optimize each one. Do not try to do everything at once; master one lever before adding another.

## Examples

### Example 1: Launching a Direct-to-Consumer Apparel Brand

You want to start a sustainable clothing brand. Before investing in a website or ads, make 10 pieces by hand and sell them at a local farmers market. Collect emails from every buyer. If you sell out, you have proof of demand. If you do not, you have feedback before spending capital. Follow Paul's path from trunk-of-car sales to retail expansion.

### Example 2: Navigating a Market Downturn

Your restaurant supply company has 5 signed lease locations and a pandemic hits. Instead of panicking and canceling leases, delay openings by 6 months, cut variable costs by 50%, and launch a new home-use product line that your existing customers can buy online. Paul did this and grew profit while competitors shrank.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Use rejection to force strategic reassessment
- ✅ Master hard skills before relying on soft skills
- ✅ Build a product you personally use and love
- ✅ Prove demand with zero marketing before scaling ad spend
- ✅ Hire people who complement your gaps, not clones of yourself
- ✅ Make yourself redundant by hiring better people
- ✅ Protect the brand at every customer touchpoint
- ✅ Have a crisis playbook ready before crisis hits
- ✅ Focus on three levers: customers, products, channels
- ❌ Do not start a consumer brand unless you are passionate about the category
- ❌ Do not scale marketing before proving organic demand
- ❌ Do not commingle business and personal finances
- ❌ Do not hire friends who cannot perform

## Keep In Mind

- Paul was rejected from Harvard and Stanford business schools; that rejection redirected him toward entrepreneurship
- He sold his first $100,000 in boots before hiring his first employee
- During COVID, Paul signed 10 leases in February 2020 and had to adapt one month later
- Takova grew $10 million in profit in 2020 while most retailers shrank
- Paul stepped down as CEO after seven years to focus on his role as founder and creative visionary

## Security & Safety Notes

- Maintain strict separation between business and personal bank accounts
- Do not use business funds for personal expenses; the IRS will penalize this
- Protect your brand formula and supplier relationships; keep manufacturing details confidential
- Vet investors carefully; ensure they align with your long-term brand vision

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Running out of cash before reaching product-market fit
  **Solution:** Bootstrap longer by proving demand with zero marketing dollars
- **Problem:** Micromanaging every decision as the company grows
  **Solution:** Hire people better than you at specific functions and let them own outcomes
- **Problem:** Expanding product lines before mastering the core offering
  **Solution:** Get really good at selling one thing before adding adjacent categories
- **Problem:** Being forced to close locations during a crisis
  **Solution:** Keep leases but delay openings; preserve optionality
