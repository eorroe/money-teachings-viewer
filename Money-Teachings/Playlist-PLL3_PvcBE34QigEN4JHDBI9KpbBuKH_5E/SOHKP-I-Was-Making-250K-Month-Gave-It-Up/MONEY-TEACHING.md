# How to Build a Multi-Million Dollar Business in an Unsexy Industry

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills the key principles from Hugo Sánchez's journey of building a multi-million dollar dry cleaning empire from a single $40,000 purchase, and then pivoting to build a tech startup (Modi). It focuses on how to find profitable opportunities in unsexy, overlooked industries, the importance of vertical integration, location strategy, and how to scale through logistics and systems.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are looking for business ideas in overlooked or "unsexy" industries
- When you want to understand how to vertically integrate to control costs and margins
- When you are evaluating locations for a brick-and-mortar or local service business
- When you need to scale a service business through logistics and operational efficiency
- When you are preparing to pitch investors and want to understand how to read different investor types

## Steps

### Step 1: Find Profitable Opportunities in Unsexy, Overlooked Industries

Hugo made millions in dry cleaning — an industry most people ignore because it is not glamorous. He discovered that the markup was enormous: it cost about $1 to clean a suit, but customers paid $30. Look for industries where there is a massive gap between cost and price, where technology has not disrupted the business model, and where most entrepreneurs are not looking. Unsexy businesses often have less competition and higher margins.

### Step 2: Buy an Existing Business With Equipment and Learn From the Team

Hugo bought an existing dry cleaner in Bushwick, Brooklyn for $40,000. He kept the existing team because they knew the business — pricing, costs, operations, and customer expectations. When acquiring a business, prioritize keeping experienced staff who can educate you. Ask about hanger costs, wrap costs, sleeve clip costs, and every other line item. This inside knowledge is priceless.

### Step 3: Vertically Integrate to Control Costs and Margins

The key to Hugo's success was owning the factory (the "plant") rather than just the storefront. By owning the cleaning equipment and processing, he could charge $30 on one block and $18 on another because he controlled the entire supply chain. Vertical integration creates a moat — competitors cannot match your prices because they do not own the production. Identify the bottleneck or highest-cost step in your industry's supply chain and own it.

### Step 4: Choose Locations Strategically — Walk Five Blocks in Every Direction

Hugo's rule: never open a dry cleaner on a block with only houses. Instead, target buildings with 30-40+ apartments. People in apartment buildings use dry cleaning; people in houses with washers and dryers do not. Before buying any location, walk five blocks in every direction. If there is already a cleaner on the block, get their price sheet — you will beat them because you own the factory. Location determines 80% of success in local service businesses.

### Step 5: Scale With Smart Logistics, Not Just More Locations

Hugo scaled to 10 pop-up stores by using color-coded tags to route laundry to the correct location and color-coded rush tags for same-day service. He offered two daily pickups (7 AM and 12 PM) to keep turnaround under 24 hours. He negotiated pickup times with external cleaners to avoid double deliveries. Systems and logistics are how you scale a service business without losing quality or going crazy.

### Step 6: Be of Service First — Give Value Without Keeping Score

Hugo's mentor taught him: "If you are $1,000 away from a yes, how fast would you get those 1,000 nos?" He lives by the principle of being of service — doing things for people without expecting immediate return. He gave free dry cleaning to doormen to gain access to luxury apartment buildings. He stopped to do an impromptu interview with James because he felt the interviewer was being rude to a hustler. Generosity and service create unexpected opportunities and goodwill.

### Step 7: Take Responsibility for Mistakes — Replace Rather Than Argue

If you lose a customer's shirt, do not argue about the $3 cleaning fee vs. the $200 replacement cost. Replace the shirt, give them a credit, and move on. Customers in service businesses are emotional — losing their favorite shirt feels like a personal loss. Taking responsibility builds loyalty that no competitor can break. Never make the customer feel like it is their fault.

### Step 8: Read People and Adapt Your Pitch to the Investor

Hugo learned that every investor is different. The relaxed investor in a zip-up shirt wants margins. The billionaire in a suit wants to know how he gets his return and whether you will IPO or sell. Some want the story, some want the numbers. When you walk into a room, you have three and a half seconds to read the person. Listen to their questions and answer exactly what they ask — do not dance around topics. If you cannot answer honestly, you are not ready.

### Step 9: Do Not Take a Salary From Investor Money Until You Are Profitable

Hugo raised $1M but does not take a salary. He knows his chances of getting funded as a Latino founder are less than 2%. He cannot afford to look like he is inflating his lifestyle with investor money. This builds trust with investors and forces you to stay lean. Only take what you need to survive; everything else goes back into the business.

### Step 10: Persevere Through Rejection — Every "No" Is Feedback

Hugo has been told "no" hundreds of times by investors. His strategy: after every rejection, ask what specifically turned the investor off and what he could have done better. Every investor is different, so adapt your pitch each time. The "no" is not personal — it is data. If you are $1,000 away from a "yes," you need to get through 999 "no"s to find it.

## Examples

### Example 1: From $40K Dry Cleaner to $250K/Month Profit

Hugo bought a failing dry cleaner for $40,000, kept the team, and immediately identified the factory as the real asset. He opened pop-up stores in apartment buildings, used color-coded tags to manage 10+ locations from one factory, and offered free service to doormen to gain access to luxury buildings. At peak, he was printing $250K/month in cash profit. The business was unsexy, but the margins were enormous.

### Example 2: Pivoting From Dry Cleaning to Tech After 20+ Years in the Game

At age 48, Hugo decided to sell his dry cleaning empire and build a video-calling app (Modi) — an industry he knew nothing about. He had to learn tech in real time with CTOs who had built products for Google. He raised $1M, faced rejection from hundreds of investors because he is Latino (only 2% of Latino tech founders get funded), and still refuses to take a salary. He is now building a tech platform after running a blue-collar business for two decades.

### Example 3: Using Free Service to Gain Access to High-Value Customers

Hugo offered to clean doormen's uniforms for free in exchange for putting his bags in luxury apartment buildings. The cost was 80 cents per uniform. The return: access to 400-apartment buildings where residents paid $30+ per bag. This "free" service cost almost nothing and unlocked recurring revenue from customers who could afford to spend thousands on dry cleaning.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Look for unsexy industries with huge markup gaps between cost and price
- ✅ Buy existing businesses with equipment and retain experienced staff
- ✅ Vertically integrate to own the highest-margin or highest-cost step
- ✅ Choose locations with high apartment density, not single-family homes
- ✅ Walk five blocks in every direction before buying any location
- ✅ Scale with systems: color-coded tags, multiple pickups, negotiated delivery times
- ✅ Be of service first — give value without keeping score
- ✅ Replace lost or damaged items rather than arguing about liability
- ✅ Read investors fast and adapt your pitch to what they care about
- ✅ Do not take a salary from investor money until profitable
- ✅ Treat every "no" as feedback — ask what you can improve
- ❌ Don't open a business on a block with only houses for a service that requires repeat visits
- ❌ Don't spend investor money on personal salary or lifestyle inflation
- ❌ Don't argue with customers about liability — replace and retain loyalty
- ❌ Don't give the same pitch to every investor — read the room first
- ❌ Don't scale locations before you have the factory and logistics locked down

## Keep In Mind

- Unsexy businesses often have less competition and higher margins than trendy startups
- Vertical integration creates a moat that competitors cannot easily cross
- Location is everything for local service businesses — walk the block before buying
- Service businesses are emotionally charged — customers care deeply about their laundry, suits, and belongings
- Being of service and building genuine relationships creates unexpected opportunities
- Rejection is not personal — it is data that makes your pitch stronger

## Security & Safety Notes

- Dry cleaning involves chemicals (perc) that can be hazardous — ensure proper ventilation and compliance with environmental regulations
- Keep customer data (credit cards on file, personal information) secure and encrypted
- When raising money, understand securities laws and investor accreditation requirements
- Do not commingle personal and business funds — especially when taking investor capital
- If handling expensive items (designer suits, wedding dresses), consider insurance for loss or damage

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Choosing the wrong location and running out of customers.
  **Solution:** Walk five blocks in every direction. If there are only houses, do not open there. Target apartment buildings with 30+ units.
- **Problem:** Scaling locations before owning the factory, causing quality and delivery failures.
  **Solution:** Lock down your production capacity and logistics before opening new stores. You cannot scale a service business without a repeatable system.
- **Problem:** Arguing with customers about liability instead of solving the problem.
  **Solution:** Replace the item, give a credit, and move on. The cost of losing a customer is far higher than the cost of a replacement.
- **Problem:** Taking a salary from investor money and burning trust.
  **Solution:** Do not take a salary until the business is profitable. This keeps you lean and shows investors you are serious.
- **Problem:** Giving generic pitches to investors and getting rejected.
  **Solution:** Read the investor's style in the first 3.5 seconds. If they want margins, talk margins. If they want the story, tell the story. Listen and answer exactly what they ask.
