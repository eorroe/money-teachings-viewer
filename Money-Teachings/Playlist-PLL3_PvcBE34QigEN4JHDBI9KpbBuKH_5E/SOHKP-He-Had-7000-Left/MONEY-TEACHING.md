# He Had $7,000 Left and Sold His Companies for $1.1 Billion: The Bootstrap Billionaire Playbook

## Overview

This Money Teaching reveals how Blake Johnson built and sold multiple companies for nine and ten figures by bootstrapping with his own capital, focusing on business fundamentals, and building every company from day one with the intention to get acquired. His approach strips away complexity and returns to the basic math that makes any business work.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you want to build a company designed for acquisition
- When you are deciding whether to raise capital or bootstrap
- When you need to scale quickly without losing control
- When the user asks how to evaluate a business opportunity

## Steps

### Step 1: Master the Fundamentals of Business Math

Understand that businesses are binary: they either spend more than they make and go out of business, or they make more than they spend and get applauded. Focus on four quantifiable aspects: cost of goods sold, customer acquisition cost (CAC), overhead, and profit margin. Your goal is to sell more than you expend.

### Step 2: Choose a Business with a Big TAM

Find a market so large that you can ask one universal question and get a "yes" from anyone in the world. Examples: "Do you want straighter whiter teeth?" "Do you want easy access to money?" "Do you want to look better in your clothes?" Avoid niche products like poodle sweaters that have no mass appeal.

### Step 3: Bootstrap With Your Own Money

Never raise outside capital unless you can raise so much that you can afford to fail. Bootstrapping forces discipline because when you spend your own money, it hits differently. Raising capital makes founders lazy and undisciplined because it is not personal. Treat spending like a trapeze artist without a safety net.

### Step 4: Obsess Over Customer Acquisition Cost

CAC is the billion-dollar question and the hardest thing to get right. Marketing methods constantly change, but the math remains the same. Layer on the basics: know your product cost, your team size, your office expenses, and most importantly, who you have to pay and how much to get your product in front of a willing buyer.

### Step 5: Build the Team Like a Sports General Manager

Businesses are like professional sports teams. A great pitcher makes a terrible first baseman. Do not promote your best salesperson to sales manager just because they are crushing it. Understand each person's strengths and weaknesses. Hire 50% of your team from previous ventures because fundamentals like accounting, legal, and consumer marketing transfer across industries.

### Step 6: Keep the Wings Balanced

Think of your business as an airplane. Marketing and product are the two engines providing thrust. Everything else - supply chain, personnel, sales, customer service, accounting - are the wings. If the wings are misbalanced, the plane crashes. Run fire drills before you need them so you know exactly what to cut when acquisition costs spike.

## Examples

### Example 1: The $7,000 Rescue

During the 2008 financial crisis, Blake's first company was on the brink of collapse. He got down to his last $7,000 with a $43,000 payroll due in two weeks. He made $62,000 in those two weeks, bringing his total to $69,000, and paid the payroll. That thin margin taught him to obsess over building companies that could get acquired rather than fail.

### Example 2: The Bite Exit

Bite was Blake's fifth company, founded from his head and financed entirely by himself. He started the company in December 2015 after a conversation with an orthodontist friend. Despite being his fifth company, he called it a "15-year-old startup" because he brought all the learnings from previous failures. He sold it on the last day of 2020 - less than four years later - for over a billion dollars.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Build every business to get acquired from day one
- ✅ Focus on a finite checklist of acquisition criteria
- ✅ Keep customer acquisition cost as the top priority
- ✅ Bootstrap with your own capital for maximum discipline
- ✅ Hire for fundamentals that transfer across businesses
- ✅ Run fire drills before you need them
- ❌ Don't raise capital unless you can raise enough to afford to fail
- ❌ Don't go down rabbit holes overcomplicating simple math
- ❌ Don't promote people based solely on past performance without testing fit for the new role
- ❌ Don't let one wing of the business lag while others accelerate

## Keep In Mind

- There is a methodology and playbook for getting acquired
- Private equity and strategic buyers want meat on the bone - they want to take what you built and dramatically increase revenue
- Sell when you can, not when you want to
- Businesses either get acquired or go out of business - there is no middle ground

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never create positions or equity splits that people are not ready for
- Don't blur the lines between employees and friends
- Keep strategic distance for the good of the company

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Running out of cash during an economic downturn
  **Solution:** Always maintain a paranoid state and run financial fire drills
- **Problem:** Growing too fast and becoming unbalanced
  **Solution:** Keep the airplane wings balanced - if marketing is the engine, ensure supply chain, personnel, and accounting scale proportionally
- **Problem:** Hiring friends or family without testing role fit
  **Solution:** Treat the business like a sports team - match skills to positions objectively, not based on personal relationships
