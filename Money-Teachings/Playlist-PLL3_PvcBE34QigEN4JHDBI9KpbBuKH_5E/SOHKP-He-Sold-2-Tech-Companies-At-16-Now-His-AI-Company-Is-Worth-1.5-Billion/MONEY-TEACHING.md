# Money Teaching Title: He Sold 2 Tech Companies At 16, Now His AI Company Is Worth $1.5 Billion

## Overview

John Imah built a $1.5 billion AI company (Spree AI) after selling two tech companies before age 16. This teaching distills his principles for entrepreneurs who want to build valuable businesses by solving real problems, leveraging networks, and maintaining relentless persistence.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you need to identify and solve a genuine market problem instead of chasing a trend
- When building a business in a traditional industry that lacks technological innovation
- When entering corporate America to learn skills before returning to entrepreneurship
- When facing self-doubt or imposter syndrome around highly credentialed peers

## Steps

### Step 1: Solve Real Problems, Don't Create Them

Great entrepreneurs identify pain points from actual customers and build solutions around those needs. John validated Spree AI by meeting with fashion executives in Europe to understand their specific challenges around returns, sizing, and personalization before writing any code.

### Step 2: Assemble the Right Team

You do not need to be the best at everything. John's strength was spotting talent and assembling specialists who shared his vision. He looked for people who bought into the concept, not just those with impressive resumes. Motivate them by treating them well and ensuring they believe in the mission.

### Step 3: Use Corporate Jobs as Training Grounds

John worked at Samsung, Take-Two Interactive, Twitch, Meta, and Snapchat not for the paycheck, but to learn processes, discipline, marketing, sales, and networking. Each role taught him skills he later applied to Spree AI. Corporate America provides a safety net and invaluable experience if you approach it with intention.

### Step 4: Master Networking and Follow-Up

John's superpower was persistence and follow-up. He built relationships with VCs, celebrities, and executives long before he needed them. In business, who you know frequently matters as much as what you know. Attend events, meet people, and maintain those connections.

### Step 5: Present Yourself According to Your Industry

When John started Spree AI, he showed up in tech hoodies to fashion meetings and it did not resonate. He adapted by dressing like the industry he was serving, putting on fashion shows for presentations, and attending exclusive events to build credibility. Match the culture of the market you are entering.

### Step 6: Maintain Humility and Hunger

John grew up poor, which kept him grounded. Even at the height of his success, he views every day as a battle. Avoid getting comfortable. Surround yourself with people who will challenge you and keep you honest. Your backstory is an asset, not a liability.

## Examples

### Example 1: Building Spree AI in Fashion Tech

John identified that fashion had a high return rate due to sizing issues and lack of personalization. He traveled to Europe, met with luxury brand executives, listened to their pain points, and built a B2B SaaS platform to solve those exact problems. He then offered free trials to get brands hooked before converting to paid plans.

### Example 2: Leveraging Past Corporate Experience

At Meta, John won Employee of the Year by outperforming peers with PhDs from Stanford and Harvard. He learned that success in corporate America requires both intellectual competence and social navigation. He applied this lesson at Spree AI by building relationships with MIT, Carnegie Mellon, the CFDA, and Vogue to establish credibility.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Solve a real, validated problem before building anything
- ✅ Use corporate jobs to learn processes, sales, and networking skills
- ✅ Hire for cultural fit and commitment, not just credentials
- ✅ Follow up relentlessly with contacts and potential partners
- ✅ Adapt your presentation style to match the industry you are serving
- ✅ Stay humble and keep your early struggles as motivation

## Keep In Mind

- Your network is frequently more valuable than your knowledge
- Imposter syndrome is common, but consistent performance wins respect
- You do not need a traditional background to succeed in tech or fashion
- Being different (e.g., a nerdy Black kid in tech) can be a competitive advantage if you own it

## Security & Safety Notes

- When meeting with executives or partners in person, always verify identities and meetings through official channels
- Protect proprietary technology and formulas with NDAs and legal agreements
- Be cautious about sharing sensitive business metrics publicly

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Building a product before validating demand with real customers
  **Solution:** Talk to potential customers first, offer free trials or sandbox environments, and iterate based on feedback before full-scale development
- **Problem:** Underestimating the importance of industry culture and relationships
  **Solution:** Invest time in understanding how decisions are made in your target industry and build personal relationships before pitching
- **Problem:** Getting comfortable after early success and losing the hunger to improve
  **Solution:** Keep your early struggles visible, stay close to the work, and continuously set new goals
