# Money Teaching Title: I Started Knocking Doors at 42, Then Sold My Company for Over $100M

## Overview

Kirt Linington started door-knocking at 42 years old, built a nine-figure roofing company, and exited to private equity. This teaching proves that age is not a barrier to entrepreneurship and that grassroots sales, relentless follow-through, and a focus on one lane before diversifying are the keys to extraordinary success.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you believe you are too old or too young to start a business
- When entering a fragmented, unglamorous industry with huge upside
- When building a sales-driven organization from scratch
- When preparing to exit your company to private equity or a strategic buyer
- When dealing with rejection, doubt, or imposter syndrome in entrepreneurship

## Steps

### Step 1: Eliminate Self-Imposed Limitations

Age, background, and credentials do not determine success. Kirt was a corporate executive vice president who left to knock doors at 42. He refused to let pride stop him. If you are willing to do what others will not, you can create a lane for yourself regardless of your starting point.

### Step 2: Lead With Sales, Not Backend Infrastructure

Do not build operations before you have customers. Kirt started with four guys, no systems, no plan—just hit the streets, knock doors, and close sales. Sales solve everything. Without revenue, perfect processes are meaningless.

### Step 3: Reframe Door-Knocking as Promotion, Not Sales

Rejection is personal only if you let it be. Kirt frames door-knocking as promoting a solution to a problem, not selling something. You are a facilitator helping homeowners fix damage and prevent future issues. This mindset shift removes the stink of desperation and attracts rather than repels.

### Step 4: Hire for Attitude, Teach the Rest

Kirt's mantra: hire the attitude, teach the rest. Competency can be trained; character cannot. Look for people who show up early, work hard, and do not make excuses. One quiet, persistent salesperson who never gave up outperformed natural-born closers because attitude wins long-term.

### Step 5: Master One Thing Before Diversifying

The "seven streams of income" myth destroys more entrepreneurs than bad luck. Kirt warns against shiny object syndrome. Become great at roofing first. Once you have made enough money and truly mastered your craft, then diversify. Buying a portfolio of houses is another job; it will drain time and energy from your main business.

### Step 6: Build Systems That Scale

As the company grew to $62 million in revenue, Kirt reinvested profits into training, recruiting, and leadership development. He created a platform that could be replicated in multiple markets. Private equity buyers pay a premium for businesses that can scale without the founder's daily involvement.

### Step 7: Prepare Your Business for Exit Early

Kirt researched private equity long before selling. He cleaned up books, built a strong leadership team, demonstrated geographic diversification, and proved the model worked in multiple markets. He negotiated from a position of strength, walking away from an initial low offer and waiting seven months for a better deal.

## Examples

### Example 1: Knocking Doors While Keeping a Corporate Job

Kirt kept his executive position for four years while building the roofing company part-time. He and his wife worked Saturdays and Sundays, knocking doors and inspecting roofs. This dual-income approach reduced risk and allowed the business to reach $62 million in revenue before he went all-in.

### Example 2: Walking Away From a Low Offer

When a broker represented the company, the first offer was just under nine figures. Kirt refused it, believing the business was worth more. He spent seven months strengthening operations, then went back to market. The second round yielded a deal that reflected the company's true value. Patience and preparation beat urgency.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Start with sales before building backend infrastructure
- ✅ Hire attitude over skill; character cannot be taught
- ✅ Master one industry before diversifying
- ✅ Treat your business like a marriage with private equity; alignment matters
- ✅ Keep accounting, CRM, and legal contracts clean from day one
- ✅ Be a good person first; business problems are frequently personal problems in disguise

## Keep In Mind

- There is no such thing as business problems, only personal problems that roll into the business
- Door-knocking is unsexy but highly profitable if you treat it as promotion, not sales
- The 10,000-hour rule applies: keep showing up even when it feels unnatural
- Your people will mimic your attitude; if you are humble and hardworking, they will be too

## Security & Safety Notes

- Maintain proper insurance and licensing for all operations
- Protect customer and financial data with secure systems
- Ensure subcontractors and crews are properly vetted and insured
- Do not cut corners on taxes, write-offs, or legal compliance

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Thinking you are too old to start over
  **Solution:** Age is irrelevant. Kirt knocked doors at 42. Focus on what you can control: effort, attitude, and follow-through
- **Problem:** Diversifying before mastering your core business
  **Solution:** Seven streams of income is a myth. Get great at one thing first
- **Problem:** Taking rejection personally in sales
  **Solution:** People are rejecting the offer, not you. Be a facilitator of a process, not a salesperson begging for a yes
