# Money Teaching: Start a Nine-Figure Company at 42 by Knocking Doors and Staying Focused

## Overview

A guide to proving that age is not a barrier to entrepreneurship and that the fastest path to a nine-figure exit is often the most unsexy work no one else wants to do. Kirt Linington started door-knocking at 42 years old, built a roofing company to $62 million in revenue part-time while keeping his corporate job, and eventually sold for over $100 million. He never had a grand plan, never raised venture capital, and never followed his passion. This Money Teaching teaches you how to start late, stay focused on one thing, hire for attitude, and sell your company for maximum value by building real infrastructure before you go to market.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you feel it is too late to start a business or switch careers
- When you need to build a service or home-service business with minimal upfront capital
- When the user asks about door-to-door sales, cold outreach, or unglamorous growth channels

## Steps

### Step 1: Eliminate the Noise and Do What No One Else Wants to Do

Linington's core advice is to eliminate distractions and stop chasing the unglamorous opportunities everyone else is chasing. Door-knocking, plumbing, roofing, and similar trades are "shitty" in the sense that most people will not do them. That is exactly why they are lucrative. If you can tolerate the discomfort of rejection and the physical grind of door-knocking, you will have very little competition. Find the lane no one wants, then become the best in the world at it.

### Step 2: Get In Flight to Get Feedback

Linington did not spend months writing a business plan. He put money up with a friend from the roofing space and started knocking doors the same week. The theory is simple: you cannot steer a parked car. Get in flight, collect feedback from real customers, and adjust direction as you learn. Do not wait for perfect infrastructure, a fancy website, or a five-year plan. Sell first, then build operations to support the demand you created.

### Step 3: Keep Your Day Job Until the Business Is Too Big to Ignore

Linington kept his executive vice president position for four years while building the roofing company nights and weekends. His wife sat in the car while he inspected roofs and gave Sunday presentations. He reached $62 million in revenue before walking away. This approach de-risks the venture, keeps your health insurance and income stable, and forces you to be disciplined with your time. Do not burn your bridges until the new business is clearly outpacing what you could earn elsewhere.

### Step 4: Lead With Sales, Then Build Operations

The biggest mistake new entrepreneurs make is building perfect back-end systems before they have any customers. Linington started with four guys knocking doors. He hired people with the skills he did not have only after sales demanded it. Sales solves every problem: no clients, no money, no matter how good your backend. Hire attitude first, teach competency second. You can teach a motivated person to close a deal; you cannot teach a skilled person to care.

### Step 5: Treat Subcontractors and Labor Like Family

Roofing requires crews who will show up in 100-degree heat and freezing cold. Linington paid his crews on time, showed up with breakfast and water, and never short-changed them. The result was a loyal workforce that would travel the country and work weekends when demand spiked. Your labor force will make or break you in any physical or service business. Treat them better than anyone else treats them, and they will protect your customers and your reputation.

### Step 6: Stay in Your Lane Until You Are Great

Linington's team once took a high-rise Dallas condo roofing job they were not qualified for. The seams were installed wrong, the top-floor condos flooded, and it cost hundreds of thousands of dollars to rebuild. The lesson: do not chase bigger money outside your expertise before you have mastered your core offering. Master residential roofing before you touch commercial. Master one neighborhood before you expand to another state.

### Step 7: Maximize Enterprise Value Before You Go to Market

When Linington decided to sell, he spent seven months cleaning up the business before listing it. He hired a broker who had already sold a large roofing company, role-played buyer conversations, tightened accounting, and made sure the CRM and data were clean. He also built geographical diversity by acquiring eight businesses and centralizing them under one brand. Private equity buyers today want a platform, not an assembly of mom-and-pop operators. Show them a repeatable model, clean books, and a leadership team that can run the business without you.

### Step 8: Take Money Off the Table and Remain the Controlling Party

Linington took a million-dollar check for his business partner early on to demonstrate that reinvesting beats taking small distributions. When he eventually sold, he had already taken significant money off the table in previous rounds, which gave him leverage in negotiations. He also never sold a controlling interest. Even with 30% equity, he remained the controlling party of the board. If you want to decide when and how you exit, never give up board control.

## Examples

### Example 1: From $700 to a Nine-Figure Exit

Linington came to America with $700. He spent 20 years in corporate America, then started door-knocking at 42. Within four years, he built a $62 million revenue business part-time. When he finally sold in 2022, the deal closed in Vegas with attorneys, bankers, and accountants on a speakerphone. He went downstairs and played blackjack while waiting for the money to hit the account. Lesson: your past is not your ceiling if you are willing to start from zero and outwork everyone around you.

### Example 2: The Offer He Walked Away From

The first time Linington went to market, he received an offer just below nine figures. His team wanted to take it because of the excitement of joining a private equity platform. He refused because he knew the business was worth more. He spent seven more months building, preparing, and cleaning the company, then went to market again and got a far superior offer. Lesson: your first offer is never your best offer. If you know your value, wait for it.

### Example 3: Choosing Not to Diversify Too Early

Linington launched a landscaping company, a swimming pool company, and a land development company only after the roofing business was stable and generating excess capital. He rejected the "seven streams of income" myth because you cannot become great at one thing if you are splitting your attention across seven. Master your core business first, then deploy capital into adjacent opportunities once the main engine is running itself.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Start late if you have to—age is an advantage because you have experience, networks, and discipline
- ✅ Get in flight before you build a perfect plan; feedback is worth more than planning
- ✅ Keep your day job until the side business is too big to ignore
- ✅ Hire for attitude and teach the rest; you can teach competency, but you cannot teach character
- ✅ Treat subcontractors and labor like family; pay on time and show up with breakfast
- ✅ Stay in your lane until you are the best before expanding into new services or markets
- ✅ Clean up your books, CRM, and accounting before you go to market for a sale
- ✅ Take money off the table in every round so you never feel pressured to sell
- ✅ Never sell controlling interest if you want to decide your own exit timeline
- ❌ Do not chase passion over opportunity; passion is created through exposure, not the other way around
- ❌ Do not diversify into seven businesses before you have mastered one
- ❌ Do not take the first offer when selling; negotiate from a position of strength

## Keep In Mind

- There is no such thing as business problems, only personal problems that roll into the business. Fix your personal life and your business will improve.
- Money amplifies who you already are. If you are a great person with strong values, money will make you greater. If you are a bad person, money will expose you faster.
- "Overnight success" is a myth. The 20 years you spent in corporate America, the failures, and the lessons are what allowed you to make good decisions when it mattered.
- The best time to start was yesterday. The second-best time is today. Stop waiting for the perfect moment.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not cut corners on taxes, insurance, or licensing. A clean legal and financial record is required for any future sale.
- Do not mix personal and business funds. Private equity buyers will audit every transaction, and commingled accounts will kill your valuation.
- Do not hire friends and family unless they can perform at the same standard as every other employee. Favoritism destroys culture.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You take on commercial work before you have mastered residential, and a costly mistake wipes out your profits.
  **Solution:** Stay in your lane until you have repeatable systems and proven expertise. Turn down work outside your core competency until you have the team to execute flawlessly.

- **Problem:** You sell your first offer out of fear or excitement, leaving money on the table and losing control of the timeline.
  **Solution:** Build enterprise value for 6 to 12 months after your first offer. Go to market only when your books, team, and systems are bulletproof.

- **Problem:** You diversify into multiple businesses before your core business is stable, and attention fragmentation kills growth everywhere.
  **Solution:** Master one business, take profits from it, and only then invest in adjacent opportunities. Seven half-built businesses will never beat one dominant one.

- **Problem:** You let your ego drive expansion into new cities or markets before demand is proven.
  **Solution:** Open new locations only where existing customers are asking for you. Data-driven expansion beats vanity addresses every time.
