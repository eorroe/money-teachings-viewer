# Money Teaching Title: She Started With $67, Now Her Soap Company Makes $20 Million a Year

## Overview

Jhalesa Seymour started Salt XO with $67 in her college dorm, built an 8-figure feminine hygiene soap empire, and now generates over $20 million annually. This Money Teaching distills how she identified a shame-based market gap, validated demand through viral organic growth, hired slowly and fired fast, kept business and personal finances strictly separate, and built a brand through bold authenticity. Apply these teachings when launching a consumer product from scratch, building a brand in a stigmatized category, or scaling from side-hustle to 8 figures.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When starting a business with almost no capital
- When entering a stigmatized or taboo market
- When building a brand around personal experience rather than market research
- When scaling from organic social media to paid advertising
- When managing explosive growth without burning out

## Steps

### Step 1: Solve Your Own Problem First

Jhalesa created a soap that treated odor, BV, yeast infections, and UTI because she needed it herself. She did not conduct market surveys; she mixed ingredients in her dorm room and gave samples to friends. When they demanded more, she knew she had a product. Start with your own pain point and validate with real users before spending on marketing.

### Step 2: Build Authentic Trust Before Running Ads

Jhalesa ran Salt XO organically for five years before spending a single dollar on paid advertising. She posted her face, shared her story, and talked openly about taboo feminine hygiene topics. Trust built through vulnerability cannot be bought; it must be earned. Only after her audience knew, liked, and trusted her did she add paid ads, and the business immediately skyrocketed.

### Step 3: Hire Slow, Fire Fast

Jhalesa hires for capacity, not potential. She looks for what a person can demonstrate right now, not what they might become. When she had to let people go, she did it quickly. Hiring the wrong person slows down execution and damages team morale. Take your time to find the right person, but do not hesitate to remove someone who is not performing.

### Step 4: Protect Your Formula and Operations

Jhalesa kept her soap formula in-house for years because she feared someone would steal it. Only when she could no longer scale without outside manufacturing did she bring in a partner. Protect your intellectual property until you are ready to relinquish control, and then do so gradually with trusted partners.

### Step 5: Keep Business and Personal Finances Separate

Jhalesa learned the hard way that mixing business and personal funds leads to disaster. When 20,000 orders were lost in transit during hurricane season, she had reserves to remake everything because she never spent business money on personal expenses. Maintain separate bank accounts, pay yourself a salary, and keep reserves for emergencies.

### Step 6: Be So Bold That Copycats Cannot Compete

Jhalesa has had brands copy her entire product line, ingredient deck, and website description verbatim. She does not worry because authenticity cannot be replicated. Her community follows her voice, her story, and her cadence. Copycats can mimic your product but not your relationship with your audience.

### Step 7: Dominate Marketing, Do Not Play Nice

Jhalesa branded every bathroom at InvestFest, put stickers on every surface, and created a VIP night that made the entire venue feel like a Salt XO takeover. She wants her customers to see her brand so many times that they cannot scroll past it. Passive marketing does not work; aggressive, omnipresent marketing does.

### Step 8: Surround Yourself With People Who Edify You

Jhalesa says your circle defines you. If the people around you are not inspiring you to be better, you are in a cage, not a circle. She networked with wealthy friends not for their money but for their wisdom. Proximity to people who think bigger than you expands your own thinking.

## Examples

### Example 1: Launching a Taboo Product in College

You are in college and notice a hygiene product that does not exist for a common problem people are too embarrassed to discuss. Mix a prototype in your dorm room, give samples to 10 friends, and ask for honest feedback. If they fight over more samples, you have validation. Post your journey on TikTok and let organic demand drive initial sales before investing in ads.

### Example 2: Surviving a Logistics Crisis

You receive 20,000 orders in one month and a hurricane knocks out shipping for all of them. If you commingled funds and spent your business cash on personal expenses, you would be unable to remake the products. If you kept reserves and separated accounts, you absorb the hit and rebuild. Jhalesa did this and her business came back 10 times stronger.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Maintain strict separation between business and personal bank accounts
- Do not share proprietary formulas with manufacturers until you have legal protections in place
- Reserve 10-20% of profits for unexpected crises like shipping losses or supply chain disruptions
- Vet manufacturers thoroughly before sharing your formula at scale

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Running out of cash because you mixed personal and business expenses
  **Solution:** Open a separate business bank account on day one and pay yourself a fixed salary
- **Problem:** Hiring people who cannot perform because you see potential instead of proof
  **Solution:** Create a skills test or trial project before making any hire
- **Problem:** Losing money on ads before proving organic demand
  **Solution:** Run organic for at least one to two years before adding paid channels
- **Problem:** Getting discouraged by copycats
  **Solution:** Focus on your unique story and community; authenticity is your moat
