# Money Teaching: SOHKP Bought Challenged Brands Generate 38B

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills Jamie Salter's philosophy for building Authentic Brands Group (ABG), which now generates over $40 billion in annual revenue across brands including Champion, Reebok, Forever 21, Quiksilver, and Care Bears. It explains why acquiring established but struggling brands—then reviving them through product focus, strategic licensing, and global content distribution—is typically faster and more reliable than building a brand from scratch. The teaching also covers how to evaluate brand acquisitions using the "heartbeat" test and why family involvement and personal relationships are non-negotiable in a brand-licensing business.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are evaluating whether to acquire an existing brand or start a new one from scratch
- When you want to understand what makes a brand acquisition succeed or fail
- When you need a framework for deciding which challenged brands to buy and which to walk away from
- When you are building a licensing business and need to structure partner relationships
- When you want to expand a brand globally and need criteria for selecting which markets to enter first
- When you are deciding whether to involve family members in your business

## Steps

### Step 1: Use the Heartbeat Test to Evaluate Brand Acquisition Targets

Before acquiring any brand, go to the street and ask real people if they have heard of it. Do not hire a large consulting firm to do this for you. If ordinary people on the street recognize the brand and respond positively, the brand has a heartbeat. If they have never heard of it, walk away. A brand without a heartbeat cannot be revived through marketing alone. Example: Walk through New York City and ask 10 strangers if they have heard of Care Bears. If most say yes, the brand has a heartbeat. If most say no, do not acquire it.

### Step 2: Focus Relentlessly on Product Quality Over Marketing Spend

Marketing cannot compensate for a bad product. The most important part of any brand acquisition is improving the product itself—not the advertising campaign. Before spending on marketing, ensure the product meets the quality standard that made the brand beloved in the first place. Example: Before relaunching Tapout, ensure the merchandise quality matches what UFC fans expect. No amount of advertising will make up for poor fabric or construction.

### Step 3: License to Best-in-Class Partners, Not Generalists

If a brand is great at making shoes, do not ask it to also make clothing. License each product category to a partner who is truly the best in that specific category. This prevents the brand from diluting its quality across categories it does not master. Example: If your brand is strongest in underwear, partner with an expert underwear manufacturer. If another partner is best-in-class for socks, license socks to them—not to the underwear partner.

### Step 4: Evaluate Global Reach Before Acquiring

A brand must have global recognition or global potential to be worth acquiring at scale. Check social media follower distribution across countries before buying. If the brand has no presence in Asia, Latin America, or Europe, factor the cost and timeline of building that presence into your acquisition price. Example: Before buying Reebok, confirm that the brand has meaningful social media followings in China, India, and Africa—not just in the United States.

### Step 5: Understand Why the Brand Is For Sale

The reason a brand is on the market reveals its true condition and your negotiating leverage. Common reasons: owners want to take money off the table, private equity has reached its fund cycle and must return capital, or the brand is in trouble. Avoid acquiring at the peak of a hype cycle or when the seller is motivated only by liquidity. Example: If a brand owner is selling because private equity is forcing a sale after 5 years, that is a normal market event—not necessarily a red flag. But if the brand is failing and the owner is bailing, demand a lower price.

### Step 6: Build a Real Written Business Plan for Each Region

Before entering any new country, write out exactly how many countries are in that region, which product categories you will sell there, and what revenue each category should generate. Do not delegate this to AI entirely—write it yourself so the strategy becomes a habit. Example: Africa has 54 countries. Break down how many of those 54 you can do business in, and what product categories (footwear, apparel, accessories) you will sell in each.

### Step 7: Hire and Promote Family Members Who Can Give You Honest Feedback

Family members in the business will tell you the truth when external employees will not. They have no filter in the boardroom. Use that honesty as a strategic advantage, but also recognize that family dynamics can create boardroom tension. Example: Jamie Salter's four sons run the entertainment, lifestyle, kids', and collaboration divisions of ABG. They call out bad ideas directly—and their input has shaped decisions as large as acquiring Reebok and entering the kids' licensing market.

### Step 8: Build Global Content to Drive Commerce

Content is the new engine of commerce. Billboards, TV commercials, and print ads no longer drive purchasing decisions at scale. Instead, build mini entertainment companies that create content people actually consume. Partner with creators, influencers, and social media personalities who align with your brand values. Example: Authentic Brands Group shifted from being primarily a commerce company to a content company because content drives commerce. The companies winning today are those running mini entertainment companies through social media influencers.

## Examples

### Example 1: Acquiring and Reviving Tapout

Authentic Brands Group acquired Tapout when it was the biggest brand in the UFC. The initial mistake was licensing it only to large department stores, ignoring the independent retailers who were the brand's core audience. When Kohl's dropped Tapout after a parent complained about a fight-related uniform, every major retailer followed. ABG was forced to return to the independent retailers they had abandoned—and Tapout survived because those relationships still existed.

### Example 2: Entering the Kids' Licensing Market

ABG's four sons—all with children at home—identified that the kids' licensing market was recession-proof because adults make the purchasing decisions. The family argued the case internally, and ABG acquired Care Bears as its first kids' property in 16 years. The acquisition was driven by internal expertise (employees with children) rather than external market research.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Always test brand recognition by going to the street yourself before acquiring
- ✅ Make product quality the priority before investing in marketing
- ✅ License categories to best-in-class partners rather than generalists
- ✅ Verify global social media reach before acquiring any brand
- ✅ Write out your regional expansion plan by hand before delegating to AI
- ✅ Keep family in the business for their honest feedback, but prepare for boardroom tension
- ✅ Treat content creation as a core commerce driver, not an afterthought
- ❌ Do not hire large consulting firms to validate brand acquisitions—do it yourself
- ❌ Do not let a great marketing campaign mask a bad product
- ❌ Do not acquire a brand without first understanding why it is for sale
- ❌ Do not expand globally before writing a specific plan for each country and product category

## Keep In Mind

- Building a brand from scratch takes years of marketing, advertising, and grit. Acquiring a brand with existing recognition and reviving it is typically the faster path to revenue.
- Steve Jobs did not invent the iPod—Sony did. Apple made it better. Similarly, Jamie Salter did not invent brand licensing—Neil Cole from Iconix did. Salter made it better. You do not need to invent something new; you need to do a better job at an existing business model.
- The licensing business scales because it partners with best-in-class operators for each product category, avoiding the trap of trying to be great at everything.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not disclose deal terms or acquisition prices publicly, as this undermines future negotiating leverage
- Protect brand partner agreements and licensing contracts as confidential commercial information
- When expanding globally, ensure compliance with each country's intellectual property and trademark laws before entering the market

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Acquiring a brand based on a consulting firm's report rather than street-level consumer recognition
  **Solution:** Go directly to the public yourself. Ask people on the street. If the brand does not have a heartbeat, walk away regardless of what the consultants say.
- **Problem:** Focusing on marketing spend while product quality remains poor
  **Solution:** Fix the product first. No marketing budget can overcome a bad product long-term.
- **Problem:** Licensing everything to one generalist partner instead of category-specific experts
  **Solution:** Identify the best-in-class partner for each product category. An underwear expert should not also be your socks partner.
- **Problem:** Expanding globally without a written plan for each country and product category
  **Solution:** Write out every country, every category, and every revenue target before entering a new region. Let AI assist—but write it yourself first.
