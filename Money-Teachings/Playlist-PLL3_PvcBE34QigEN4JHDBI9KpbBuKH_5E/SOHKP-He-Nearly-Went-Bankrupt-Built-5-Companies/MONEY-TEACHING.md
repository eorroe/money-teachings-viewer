# How to Build and Sell 5 Companies Worth Over $1 Billion Starting From Near Bankruptcy

## Overview

David Royce went from a broke college student doing door-to-door pest control sales to building and selling five companies for over $1 billion total. Royce attributes his success to mastering sales first, building replicable systems and training programs, prioritizing recurring revenue, and focusing on the unglamorous blue-collar businesses that most entrepreneurs overlook. This Money Teaching covers the playbook for building high-value businesses through disciplined sales execution, operational systems, and strategic exits.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are entering a new industry and need to build domain expertise before launching
- When you are building a business that relies on door-to-door or direct sales
- When you need to create a sales training program that scales
- When you are evaluating whether a business has a durable, recurring revenue model
- When you want to structure company exits that preserve your ability to keep growing
- When you are deciding between hiring commission-only or base-plus-commission salespeople

## Steps

### Step 1: Gain Direct Experience in the Industry Before Launching Your Company

Royce did not start his pest control company from a desk. He worked as a door-to-door salesman for an existing pest control company, got fired from McDonald's and a pizza parlor in high school, and learned firsthand what worked and what did not. By the time he launched his own company, he had a PhD in what customers did not like about the industry. Before starting any business, spend at least one year working inside that industry in a direct customer-facing role. The gaps you observe as an employee become your competitive advantages as an owner.

### Step 2: Master Sales Through Deliberate Daily Practice

Royce went 5 days without a single sale, then bought six sales books and read 90 minutes every day until he cracked the code. He discovered that his closes were weak—he would finish his pitch and ask "so what do you think?"—which invited rejection. He replaced that with option closes, then developed a "rack system" that layered multiple rebuttals, new information (aces), and alternative closes in sequence. Sales is a skill you can study and improve like any other discipline. Read sales books, record your own pitches, watch your body language, and practice closing techniques until they become automatic. The difference between top performers and everyone else is deliberate practice, not natural talent.

### Step 3: Build a Replicable Sales Training System as Your First Competitive Advantage

Royce's companies grew 7 to 10 times faster than traditional pest control companies because he built a sales training program that turned ordinary people into top performers. He recorded sales reps with camcorders, watched their body language, wrote training manuals, and systematized every part of the sales process. Most small business owners never build a second location because they do not document what works. Write down your best practices in a training manual, record your top performers, and build an onboarding process that does not depend on you. Your sales organization is either your greatest asset or your greatest liability—invest accordingly.

### Step 4: Prioritize Recurring Revenue Models Above All Else

Pest control customers pay monthly or quarterly for ongoing service, creating a revenue stream that compounds over time and commands a higher multiple when you sell the business. Royce compares recurring revenue models to SaaS businesses: the lifetime value of each customer is dramatically higher than one-time transactions, and customers who experience the fewest problems stay the longest. When selecting a business model, prioritize recurring revenue. If you are already in a transactional business, build a subscription or retainer layer on top of it.

### Step 5: Structure Your Early Deals to Preserve Cash Flow and Avoid Bankruptcy

In Royce's first year, he overcommitted to customer acquisitions and almost ran out of cash paying sales commissions. He had to ask salespeople to defer payment with a 10% interest premium. The experience taught him that cash flow, not revenue or profit, is the real constraint. When structuring deals, make sure you receive payment before you owe large commissions. Build a cash reserve before scaling aggressively. Revenues are vanity, profits are sanity, and cash flow is reality.

### Step 6: Hire Commission-Only Salespeople to Attract Hungry Performers

Royce hires only commission-based salespeople. Base-plus-commission roles attract people who want security; commission-only roles attract people who want to win. The top performers in any sales organization are the ones who prefer to eat what they kill. When hiring salespeople, structure compensation around performance, not time served. You will get better talent, lower fixed costs, and a team that is aligned with the company's growth.

### Step 7: Track Every Sales Interaction With Data and Treat Rejection as a Numbers Game

Royce's team tracked every door knocked, every objection, and every outcome on a digital map. They discovered that knocking 150 doors in a day produced approximately 2.5 sales on average. If you have not made a sale after 74 doors, the math says you are likely to close on the 75th. Rejection in sales is not personal—it is statistical. Build a tracking system, understand your conversion rates, and show up consistently. The outcome of one door does not affect the next.

### Step 8: Build Businesses in Boring, Blue-Collar Industries With Strong Margins

Royce cites a Wall Street Journal study: 43% of the top 0.1% of income earners (those making $2.3 million or more per year) made their money in boring blue-collar businesses—not tech, not finance, not glamour industries. Pest control, medical waste, car washes, and similar businesses have high margins, essential demand, and almost no competition from other entrepreneurs. When choosing a business to build or acquire, prioritize boring industries where most people are not looking. Competition is the biggest destroyer of profit, and boring industries have the least of it.

### Step 9: Acquire Businesses From Retiring Owners at Fair Terms

Many baby boomer business owners have no succession plan and children who do not want to take over. These sellers often prefer a reasonable offer from a motivated buyer over walking away with nothing. Royce recommends self-financing or structuring seller-financed deals to acquire these businesses. It gives you training wheels on a proven cash-flowing business and access to a mentor in the current owner. If you have experience in the industry, you can identify undervalued businesses, acquire them on favorable terms, and scale them using your systems and sales playbook.

### Step 10: Plan Your Exit Strategy Around Retaining Your Most Valuable Assets

When Royce sells a company, he keeps the customer base and technicians but retains the sales force, executive team, and key operators. The buyer wants the recurring revenue stream, not your brand or leadership team. This structure lets Royce redeploy his team and grow the next business faster with proceeds from the exit. When selling, identify what assets the buyer actually wants and what you need to keep building the next version of the business. Structure the deal to maximize your optionality, not just your exit payout.

## Examples

### Example 1: From Broke Student to $13 Million Exit in Four Years

Royce started his pest control company after working as a door-to-door salesman in college. He almost went bankrupt in year one from overcommitting on customer acquisition costs, but by year four he sold his first company for $13 million. The exit was the result of a replicable sales model, recurring customer contracts, and a team he had trained from scratch—not luck or a single breakthrough.

### Example 2: Using Silicon Valley Perks to Build a Unique Culture and Scale Faster

On his third and largest pest control company, Royce adopted Silicon Valley-style perks—NCAA basketball courts, golf simulators, DJ events, retreats in Hawaii, Thailand, and Africa—when no other pest control company was doing anything similar. The differentiated culture attracted the best sales talent, retained top branch managers, and created a brand that customers and employees talked about. Perks that are cheap to copy but rare in your industry can create a compounding talent advantage.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Gain hands-on experience in your target industry before launching your own company
- ✅ Read sales books and practice closing techniques deliberately every day
- ✅ Build a written sales training manual and record your best performers
- ✅ Prioritize businesses with recurring revenue models
- ✅ Track every sales interaction and understand your conversion math
- ✅ Hire commission-only salespeople to attract hungry, high-performers
- ✅ Buy boring, blue-collar businesses with strong margins and low competition
- ✅ Structure exits to retain your sales force, leadership team, and optionality

## Keep In Mind

- Cash flow is reality. Revenues are vanity, profits are sanity, and cash flow is what keeps you in business.

- The wealthiest people in America made their money in unsexy industries, not tech startups.
- Your first year in business will be harder than you expect. Plan for it.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not disclose specific deal terms, acquisition prices, or portfolio company financial data publicly.
- Avoid sharing proprietary sales training methodologies or customer data publicly.
- Do not expose specific cash flow management strategies or commission structures that competitors could replicate.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Hiring salespeople before building a training system, leading to inconsistent performance and high turnover.
  **Solution:** Write your training manual and record your best performers before hiring your first sales rep.
- **Problem:** Expanding into a new industry without operational expertise, leading to costly mistakes.
  **Solution:** Hire the best operator you can find in that industry, pay them well, and give them equity. Do not try to figure it out alone.
- **Problem:** Taking rejection personally and letting a bad day destroy your momentum.
  **Solution:** Treat sales as a numbers game. Track your metrics, know your averages, and show up at the next door with a clean slate.
- **Problem:** Overdiversifying into multiple business lines before mastering one.
  **Solution:** Stay concentrated until you have true mastery and a durable competitive advantage in one business. Diversification preserves wealth; concentration builds it.
