# Money Teaching: Build a Fast-Food Empire by Outworking and Outcaring the Competition

## Overview

A blueprint for disrupting a crowded fast-food market by staying lean, controlling quality, treating employees like family, and making fairness the core of your brand. Patrick Terry started P. Terry's at age 45 with a 527-square-foot building across the street from McDonald's, Jack in the Box, Wendy's, Taco Cabana, Taco Bell, and Schlotzsky's. Every one of those competitors is now gone. He built 38 locations in Texas without franchising, without a big menu, and without raising outside capital. This Money Teaching teaches you how to compete against giants by refusing to compromise on quality, people, or community.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you need to compete against established, well-funded competitors in a crowded market
- When working with a small team and must protect culture while scaling
- When the user asks about bootstrapping a restaurant or consumer brand without outside capital

## Steps

### Step 1: Control Your Supply Chain to Protect Quality and Margins

P. Terry's operates its own commissary, grinding its own meat, baking its own buns, making its own salsa, cookies, and veggie burgers. By owning production, Terry controls quality and keeps costs predictable even when commodity prices spike. He accepts lower margins rather than passing costs onto customers. If you are in food or product, build or partner with your own production facility before you scale. Do not become dependent on third-party suppliers who can raise prices or cut quality without your consent.

### Step 2: Keep the Menu Dead Simple

Terry hates big menus. P. Terry's sells burgers, fries, milkshakes, veggie burgers, and chicken sandwiches. A small menu means no waste, faster service, lower training costs, and consistent quality. Before adding a single item, ask whether it serves the core customer or dilutes the brand. If the menu is hard to navigate, you are losing sales at the drive-thru.

### Step 3: Take Care of Employees Before You Take Care of Customers

The customer is always last, not first. Terry offers interest-free loans to employees in crisis, delivers birthday cakes to every team member, and enforces the "Maggie Rule": never do anything that disrespects the frontline staff. If office workers go wine tasting on a workday while grill staff pull doubles, that is a fireable offense in spirit. Employees who feel valued will protect your brand, show up early, and treat customers like family. Pay them well, respect their time, and never ask them to do something you would not do yourself.

### Step 4: Give Back to the Community Like You Live There

P. Terry's gives back nearly $3 million per year to local charities. The community did not have to support a new burger stand, but they did because Terry showed up as a neighbor, not a landlord. Sponsor local teams, show up to school events, and make giving a line item in your budget from day one. The community will defend your brand and bring their friends.

### Step 5: Stay Local and Ignore Ego-Driven Expansion

Terry has 38 locations, all in Texas. He refuses to expand to Denver, Dallas, or beyond until the local market is saturated. Franchising is off the table because it threatens culture and quality. The biggest mistake in business is bringing your ego with you. Your name on the cup does not mean you need a sexy address in Beverly Hills or New York. Go where your customers already are, and do not open a new market until you have proven the model locally.

### Step 6: Be Transparent and Honest, Even When It Hurts

Terry does not fake it until he makes it. When his office was a shack, he told people that. When he made mistakes, he admitted them before anyone else pointed them out. This honesty built trust with his team and his customers. If you screw up, say it first. If you do not know the answer, say so. People respect authenticity, and it will attract better partners, employees, and customers.

### Step 7: Show Up Every Single Day

Terry's final message to the younger generation is simple: show up every day. You can outwork a lot of problems. Entrepreneurship is a game of odds, and showing up consistently is the single highest-leverage habit you can build. Outwork bad luck, outwork slow markets, and outwork competitors who think they are too clever to be in the weeds.

## Examples

### Example 1: Outlasting Six Competitors on One Intersection

When P. Terry's opened across the street from McDonald's, Jack in the Box, Wendy's, Taco Cabana, Taco Bell, and Schlotzsky's, every competitor was a large franchise with rules, frozen food deliveries, and distant management. Terry lived above the store, made fresh deliveries daily, handed out cookies and lemonade, and knew every customer by name. Within a few years, every competitor on that block was gone. Lesson: scale is not a moat when the incumbent has no relationship with the customer.

### Example 2: The Maggie Rule

When office staff rented a bus and went wine tasting on a workday, Maggie—an employee who had been there since day two—pulled a double shift on the grill. She came home, opened her phone, and saw her coworkers drinking wine on her workday. Terry fired the manager who approved the trip and institutionalized the Maggie Rule: never disrespect the people doing the hardest work. Lesson: culture is not what you put on a poster; it is what you enforce when no one is watching.

### Example 3: Choosing Not to Franchise

Terry turned down repeated offers to franchise and private equity deals that would have accelerated growth but sacrificed control. He chose slower, organic growth because he wanted to protect the mothership. The result is a brand with unmatched loyalty and consistency across 38 locations. Lesson: money is secondary once you have enough. Protect what makes your business special.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Control production and supply chain before scaling to protect quality
- ✅ Keep the menu simple and eliminate anything that does not serve the core customer
- ✅ Treat employees better than your customers; they will do the rest
- ✅ Give back to the community like you are a neighbor, not a corporation
- ✅ Stay local and ignore ego-driven geographic expansion
- ✅ Admit mistakes before anyone else points them out
- ✅ Show up every single day, especially when it is not glamorous
- ❌ Do not franchise if it will destroy your culture or quality
- ❌ Do not expand to new cities before you have saturated your home market
- ❌ Do not raise prices just because you can; accept fair profits instead
- ❌ Do not hire for resume alone—hire for attitude and work ethic

## Keep In Mind

- Your reputation is built on the worst day you have ever had, not the best. How you behave during a crisis defines your brand.
- "Playing business"—raising money, renting fancy offices, hiring secretaries before you have revenue—is not the same as doing business.
- The customer has to love you, not just like you. Liking is not enough to keep them from chasing a shiny new competitor across the street.
- If you do not know your capabilities, you will overpromise and underdeliver. Know what you can do, and hire people for what you cannot.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not commingle personal and business funds. Keep clean books so you know whether the business is actually profitable.
- Do not sign personal guarantees on leases or loans you cannot afford to pay back if the business fails.
- Do not skip payroll taxes or cut corners on insurance; these will destroy you in a downturn.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You expand too fast, quality drops, and customers abandon your brand.
  **Solution:** Open only as many locations as you can personally visit and protect. If you cannot walk into every store and know the staff by name, you have expanded too far.

- **Problem:** You hire a manager who looks great on paper but does not embody your culture, and they demoralize the team.
  **Solution:** Hire attitude and work ethic first. You can teach skills, but you cannot teach character. Fire culture mismatches quickly.

- **Problem:** You take outside capital or franchising offers that force you to sacrifice quality for speed.
  **Solution:** Define your non-negotiables before you take money. If a deal requires you to compromise on fresh ingredients, local giving, or employee treatment, walk away.

- **Problem:** You start raising prices because competitors did, and you erode the trust you built with customers.
  **Solution:** Accept lower margins during inflation rather than passing the full cost to customers. Make it up on volume and loyalty.
