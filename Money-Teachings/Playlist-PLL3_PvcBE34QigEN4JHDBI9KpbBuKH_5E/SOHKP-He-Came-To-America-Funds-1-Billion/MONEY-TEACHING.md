# How to Fund Billion-Dollar Companies Starting From Nothing

## Overview

Mike Sherbakov immigrated to the United States from Russia at age 5 with nothing, served 5 years in the Marine Corps, built multiple companies, and now runs the Veteran Fund with 130 portfolio companies doing billions in revenue. This Money Teaching captures his core principles: find a purpose big enough to drive your actions, cultivate the resilience and discipline forged through struggle, partner with people whose standards exceed yours, and understand how venture capital actually works before deciding to raise money. These lessons apply to founders building companies and investors evaluating early-stage opportunities.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are deciding whether to bootstrap or raise outside capital
- When you are evaluating whether entrepreneurship is right for you
- When you are a first-time founder seeking to understand venture capital before raising a round
- When you want to assess whether a potential investor adds value beyond capital
- When you are building a team and need to identify what traits to prioritize
- When you have overcome hardship and want to translate that experience into a competitive advantage

## Steps

### Step 1: Find a Purpose Big Enough That You Cannot Fail

Sherbakov's guiding principle is: when your why becomes big enough, you figure out your how. He joined the Marines at 18 not because he wanted to be a soldier, but because he felt personally attacked by the 9/11 attacks and believed it was his responsibility as an American to serve. That deep purpose carried him through boot camp, combat, and the transition into entrepreneurship. A shallow motive like making money or getting rich is rarely enough to sustain you through the hardest days of building a company. Define a purpose that is bigger than yourself, and your resourcefulness will surprise you.

### Step 2: Test Yourself in the Hardest Environment You Can Find

Sherbakov chose the Marines because it was the hardest thing he could imagine at 18. He wanted to know what he was actually made of. The military taught him that when your brain tells you are done, you are only about 40% of the way there. That lesson applies directly to entrepreneurship: the moment you want to quit is often right before the breakthrough. Seek out struggle early in life, whether through military service, competitive sports, or deliberate hardship. The resilience you build becomes an irreplaceable competitive advantage when you are building a company.

### Step 3: Partner With People Whose Standards Exceed Yours

When Sherbakov was 24 and running his first business, he landed a meeting with the man who owned every major car dealership in Dallas. He went in with humility and a focus on how he could add value to the man's businesses. That meeting turned into a partnership with a multi-billionaire who became his most important mentor. Sherbakov learned that bringing in partners with more experience, capital, and higher standards does not reduce your success—it exponentially increases it. Do not let ego or fear of equity dilution prevent you from partnering with people who are more accomplished than you are.

### Step 4: Build Resilience, Leadership, and Purpose as Your Core Leadership Traits

The Veteran Fund invests only in veteran-led companies because military service builds three essential qualities: leadership from the ground up, resilience in the face of repeated setbacks, and a sense of purpose beyond personal financial gain. Sherbakov looks for founders who do not quit, who have led teams through hardship, and who are building something that matters to them. If you are evaluating founders or assessing your own readiness, ask: do they keep going when things get hard? Do they lead from experience, not theory? Are they building for a reason bigger than a paycheck?

### Step 5: Understand the Power Law Before Raising Venture Capital

Sherbakov explains the power law: the majority of companies in a VC portfolio will not succeed, but one or two companies will return the entire fund. A $20 million fund investing $1 million per company needs one 100x return just to break even. This means diversification across 20 to 30 companies is essential, and early-stage investors are looking for outcomes that justify the risk of the entire portfolio. If you are raising capital, understand the math your investors are working with. If you are considering investing, understand that most bets will fail and that is expected—you need enough shots on goal for one or two to pay off.

### Step 6: Bet on the Founder, Not Just the Business

At the earliest stage, when the product is unproven and the market is small, Sherbakov bets on the founder's character and resilience above everything else. His most successful investments have been in founders who had already demonstrated they would not quit, even when the odds were against them. He cites a second-time founder with a nine-figure exit who started a defense technology company, and the Veteran Fund wrote one of the first checks. When you are raising money or investing, the team is the most important variable in the early stage. A great founder with an imperfect plan will find a way. A weak founder with a perfect plan will fail.

### Step 7: Vet Your Investors as Carefully as They Vet You

Sherbakov advises founders to ask potential investors: beyond capital, what can you and your firm actually support my company with? Can you introduce potential customers? Open doors to contracts? Connect me to follow-on investors? Provide operational expertise? The best investors are value-add on the cap table, not just check-writers. Sherbakov's first investment was a $25,000 check to a second-time founder he knew personally. He did not properly diligence the underlying business model and the investment underperformed. Always underwrite the business, not just the person. Do not let a famous name or personal friendship substitute for real due diligence.

### Step 8: Prove Yourself as an Investor Before Taking Other People's Money

Before launching the Veteran Fund, Sherbakov invested his own capital across multiple companies and sectors to make sure he was a good investor. He never took outside capital until he had a track record of not losing investor money. That discipline built the credibility that attracted his partners and limited partners. If you want to raise a fund or take on investors, first prove you can allocate capital wisely with your own money. A track record of positive outcomes is your most valuable asset when seeking outside capital.

### Step 9: Choose Your Industry Based on Intersection of Skill, Passion, and Market Need

After leaving the Marines, Sherbakov used an exercise to find his first business: he listed what he was good at, what he was passionate about, and what kind of impact he wanted to have. The intersection pointed him toward health and fitness, which led to FitBody Boot Camp. He chose an industry where he had genuine interest and existing knowledge rather than chasing whatever was trendy. When selecting an industry or business to enter, use the same intersection framework. If you have no experience or genuine interest in the space, you will not sustain the energy required to succeed.

### Step 10: Use AI and Technology as Leverage, Not a Replacement for Judgment

Sherbakov describes AI as extraordinary leverage that compresses time and creates opportunity for people without large amounts of capital. But he emphasizes that AI is a tool, not a replacement for founders. The big opportunity is not building a boring business or an AI-only business—it is building a durable business that delivers real value to customers. Use AI to accelerate research, ideation, and execution, but the core judgment about what to build and why still comes from the founder.

## Examples

### Example 1: Bootstrapping FitBody Boot Camp to a Successful Exit

Sherbakov started FitBody Boot Camp with almost no capital after leaving the Marines. He identified the model from a DVD, replicated it exactly, and reached profitability in under four months by acquiring 33 members at $250 per month. He scaled to multiple locations across San Diego and Orange County, then sold the business to his first hire. The bootstrapped approach gave him full ownership, full control, and a clean exit without external investors.

### Example 2: Building the Veteran Fund With a Clear Investment Thesis

The Veteran Fund was launched when Sherbakov's business partner Ryan had a portfolio company exit that generated a $35 million outcome for an Air Force veteran founder. That liquidity event, combined with years of co-investing and accelerators, made it the perfect moment to formalize the fund. The fund focuses exclusively on veteran-led companies in critical technology sectors, with a portfolio of 26 companies per fund. The thesis is that military service builds the exact traits—resilience, leadership, purpose—that make founders successful.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Define a purpose bigger than yourself before committing to entrepreneurship
- ✅ Test your limits through hardship early in life
- ✅ Bring in partners and mentors whose standards exceed yours
- ✅ Vet your investors for value-add beyond capital
- ✅ Prove your investment judgment with your own money before raising outside capital
- ✅ Use AI as leverage, not as a substitute for founder judgment

## Keep In Mind

- When your why is big enough, you will figure out the how.
- The military teaches that your perceived limits are mostly mental. The same is true in entrepreneurship.
- Most venture investments will fail. The power law means one or two winners carry the entire fund.
- Always underwrite the business model, not just the founder's reputation.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never share specific portfolio company financial data, fund performance metrics, or LP information publicly.
- Do not disclose specific deal terms, investment amounts, or company names without investor and founder consent.
- Avoid sharing personal financial details or capital allocation strategies that could be exploited by competitors.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Raising outside capital before proving you are a good investor or operator yourself.
  **Solution:** Invest your own money first, build a track record of positive outcomes, then raise outside capital.
- **Problem:** Making first investments based on personal relationships without underwriting the business model.
  **Solution:** Require the same rigor for every investment decision, regardless of how well you know the founder.
- **Problem:** Choosing an industry based on trends rather than genuine skill and interest.
  **Solution:** Use the intersection of skill, passion, and market impact to guide your industry selection.
- **Problem:** Underestimating the power law in venture capital and expecting most investments to succeed.
  **Solution:** Build a diversified portfolio knowing that most companies will fail and one or two must carry the fund.
