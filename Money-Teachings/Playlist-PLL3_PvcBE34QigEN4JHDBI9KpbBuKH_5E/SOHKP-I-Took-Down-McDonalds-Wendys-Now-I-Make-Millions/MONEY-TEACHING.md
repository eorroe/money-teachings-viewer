# Money Teaching Title: I Took Down McDonald's and Wendy's, Now I Make Millions

## Overview

Patrick Terry founded P. Terry's, a 38-location fast-casual burger chain in Texas, by outcompeting McDonald's, Wendy's, and Jack in the Box at their own game. This teaching reveals his counterintuitive principles: keep the menu tiny, treat employees like family, accept lower profit margins, protect culture above all, and never let ego drive expansion.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When starting a customer-facing business where experience and loyalty matter
- When competing against large, well-funded incumbents
- When deciding whether to franchise or grow organically
- When building a brand rooted in community and authenticity
- When facing pressure to expand rapidly or sell the business

## Steps

### Step 1: Keep the Menu Simple

Patrick hates big menus. A limited menu reduces waste, speeds up service, and ensures quality. Every item must justify its place. Simplicity keeps costs down, operations tight, and customers clear on what you stand for. If you cannot explain your core offering in one sentence, it is too complicated.

### Step 2: Treat Employees First, Customers Second

Patrick's philosophy: take care of employees, and they will take care of customers. Offer interest-free loans for emergencies, birthday cakes, and genuine care. The Maggie Rule—never do anything to disrespect your longest-tenured frontline employees—keeps morale high and turnover low. Happy employees create raving fans.

### Step 3: Accept Fair Profit, Not Maximum Profit

Patrick refuses to raise prices just because he can. During inflationary periods, he accepts lower margins rather than gouging customers. Fairness builds loyalty. If customers love you, they will return even if a cheaper option opens nearby. Liking is not enough; they must love you.

### Step 4: Protect Your Culture Relentlessly

Culture is the killer of businesses once it erodes. Patrick will not franchise because he cannot guarantee culture in another city. He keeps offices small and lean, expects office staff to work burger stands when needed, and refuses to expand outside Texas until the mothership is secure. Ego has no place in business decisions.

### Step 5: Compete on Freshness and Authenticity

While competitors relied on frozen, delivered food, Patrick built a commissary that produces fresh salsa, veggie burgers, cookies, and banana bread daily. Freshness became a competitive advantage that large chains could not easily replicate. Find what the big players cannot do and build your moat there.

### Step 6: Know Your Capabilities and Hire for Weaknesses

Patrick knows he cannot do everything. He hires people who complement his skills and fills gaps with experts. He also admits mistakes publicly and quickly, creating a culture where others feel safe doing the same. Authenticity and self-awareness build trust faster than perfection.

## Examples

### Example 1: Outcompeting Six Chains at One Intersection

On the day P. Terry's opened, the intersection across the street hosted McDonald's, Jack in the Box, Wendy's, Taco Cabana, Taco Bell, and Schlotzsky's. Not a single one remains. Patrick attributes this to freshness, simplicity, employee care, and community giving. Big chains follow rules; he operated like he was fighting in the jungle.

### Example 2: The Maggie Rule

Maggie has worked at P. Terry's for 20 years. When office staff took a wine tour on a workday while Maggie pulled a double shift on the grill, Patrick made it clear that disrespecting frontline workers is unacceptable. The rule became sacred: never do anything that makes Maggie feel undervalued.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Keep menus narrow and operations simple
- ✅ Treat employees better than competitors do
- ✅ Accept fair margins rather than chasing maximum profit
- ✅ Protect culture over growth
- ✅ Be transparent about mistakes and model accountability

## Keep In Mind

- Franchising is not always the fastest path; controlled growth can be more profitable and sustainable
- Customers must love you, not just like you, to survive competition
- Ego is the biggest mistake you can bring into business decisions
- Your office should reflect your values, not your status

## Security & Safety Notes

- Maintain strict food safety and quality standards as you scale
- Protect proprietary recipes and processes with trade secret policies
- Ensure compliance with labor laws, especially when offering employee benefits like interest-free loans

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Expanding too fast and losing control of quality or culture
  **Solution:** Stay in your home market until you have mastered it. Only expand when you can replicate the experience perfectly
- **Problem:** Raising prices during inflation to protect margins
  **Solution:** Absorb cost increases when possible. Customer loyalty built through fairness pays dividends over time
- **Problem:** Hiring for resume instead of attitude and character
  **Solution:** Hire attitude, teach skills. A bad attitude poisons culture regardless of competence
