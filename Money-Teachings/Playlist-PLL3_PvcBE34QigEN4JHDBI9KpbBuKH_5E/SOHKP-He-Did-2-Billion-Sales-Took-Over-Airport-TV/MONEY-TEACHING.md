# How to Build a Billion-Dollar Business by Serving Underserved Markets

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills the key principles from Lynnwood Bibbens' journey of building multiple companies, doing $2B in sales, and eventually founding Reach TV, which took over airport TV advertising. It focuses on the consistent business principle of serving your customer, how to identify underserved audiences, the power of distribution over content, and how to build relationships that compound over decades.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are building a media, content, or distribution business
- When you want to identify an underserved audience that competitors are ignoring
- When you need to structure partnerships across complex ecosystems (airports, airlines, advertisers)
- When you are negotiating with suppliers or partners who hold power over your business
- When you want to build long-term relationships that lead to deals years later

## Steps

### Step 1: Identify the Consistent Principle — Serve Your Customer

Lynnwood's one consistent principle across every era and industry is: serve your customer. Not just serve them, but overserve them. Ask three questions: Who am I serving? How do I serve them? How do I keep serving them more efficiently? Then, look at the entire ecosystem — who else are you serving through this? Suppliers, partners, and end customers all matter.

### Step 2: Find an Underserved Audience and Own the Distribution

Lynnwood realized that travelers are a massive, affluent audience, but no one was talking to them specifically at scale. CNN Airport existed in 55 airports, but the concessionaire (the company running food and shops) was in 68 airports. He launched Reach TV with the concessionaire, instantly reaching more airports than CNN. Find an audience that is fragmented and ignored, then own the distribution to reach them.

### Step 3: Create a New Rights Window When Existing Ones Are Too Expensive

Netflix had created digital and television rights windows, but Lynnwood could not afford them. Instead, he created a new "closed circuit" rights window for airports, planes, and hotels — about 1,000th of the cost of digital rights. He bought content before he even had a network, exercising the rights window so aggressively that by the time studios realized what he was doing, he was the leader. If existing markets or rights are too expensive, create a new category.

### Step 4: Build Relationships Over Transactions — Never Make It Transactional

Lynnwood's biggest warning: do not make relationships transactional. He kept in touch with people he met in 1997-1999 and did deals with them in 2015. Relationships compound over time. When connecting people, only do it if you see genuine value for both sides. Ask yourself: "Have I provided enough value in this relationship to ask this question?" If not, keep giving before you ask.

### Step 5: Simplify Your Offer — Give People Recommendations, Not Catalogs

In 1997, Lynnwood launched Pickadw.com, a "six deals a day" electronics site, at a time when every website was just a digital catalog. People do not want to browse a catalog — they want an expert to tell them what to buy. If you are the expert in your business, give recommendations. Simplify the offer so much that a customer can understand it in 10 seconds.

### Step 6: Research Obsessively and Get Third-Party Advice on Your Value

When Lynnwood's college business was doing $38M in revenue but only making $1M profit, he asked his professor for advice. The professor told him to call his biggest supplier and say he was thinking of selling — within 30 minutes, both suppliers flew in, and by Friday they had a deal on the table. Always ask for outside perspective on where your real value lies. You are too close to your own business to see it clearly.

### Step 7: Negotiate Structures That Serve the Entire Ecosystem

When Lynnwood's customer (a small company with a $22M AT&T contract) could only afford $4M of his service, he created joint ventures with small companies so they could partner with AT&T as a tier-one vendor. This financed the gap without touching his credit line. Look at the entire ecosystem and find structures that serve everyone — not just you.

### Step 8: Use "Dream Sessions" to Unlock Future Innovation

During the pandemic, when travel stopped, Lynnwood's team held "dream sessions" where nobody was allowed to talk about current business. They imagined future possibilities. One idea: become a live sports network. Five years later, Reach TV is one of only two companies (alongside Amazon) with every NFL game, playoff game, and Super Bowl for five years. Give your team space to dream without constraints.

## Examples

### Example 1: Buying CNN Airport and Scaling in 65 Days

When CNN Airport was for sale, Lynnwood had 65 days to convince every airport authority to switch to Reach TV before everything went dark on March 31st. He and his team did not sleep for 65 days, visiting every single airport authority and signing agreements. They went live on March 31st. This was possible because his team had already decided they believed in travel and the traveler — so buying CNN Airport was not a question.

### Example 2: Creating Closed Circuit Rights and Buying Content Before Building the Network

Lynnwood created a new rights window (closed circuit for airports, inflight, hotels) that did not exist before. He went to studios and bought the rights to content before he even had a screen on an airport wall. By the time the industry realized what he was doing, Reach TV was the leader in that rights window. He avoided competing with Netflix, YouTube, and CBS by creating his own category.

### Example 3: From $38M Revenue and $1M Profit to a $250M Business

Lynnwood's first post-college company was doing $38M in revenue but only making $1M because they were in their own bubble. After his professor pointed out that suppliers were making more than they were, he discovered he was serving multiple parties in the ecosystem without realizing it. By understanding his true value and forming the right partnerships, he scaled from $87M to $250M in revenue without acquiring new customers — just by financing the gap for existing ones.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Serve your customer — and overserve them
- ✅ Look at the entire ecosystem: who else are you serving?
- ✅ Find an underserved audience and own the distribution to reach them
- ✅ Create new rights windows or categories when existing ones are too expensive
- ✅ Build relationships, not transactions — keep in touch for decades
- ✅ Simplify your offer: give recommendations, not catalogs
- ✅ Research obsessively and get third-party advice on your real value
- ✅ Negotiate structures that serve all parties in the ecosystem
- ✅ Use dream sessions to imagine future possibilities without constraints
- ✅ Ask for help and stay humble — you are never too experienced to learn
- ❌ Don't make relationships transactional — people will feel leveraged
- ❌ Don't compete head-to-head with giants — create a new category instead
- ❌ Don't scale before you have the infrastructure and rights locked down
- ❌ Don't ignore suppliers and partners — they are part of your ecosystem
- ❌ Don't assume you know your value — get outside perspective

## Keep In Mind

- Content is king, but distribution is emperor — without distribution, content is worthless
- The best deals often come from relationships you built 10-20 years earlier
- Simplifying the offer is more powerful than adding more features or products
- Dream sessions unlock innovation that incremental thinking cannot reach
- You are serving more people than you realize — look at the full ecosystem
- Ego is the enemy — stream everything from a stored device rather than showing off with expensive real-time streaming

## Security & Safety Notes

- When buying content rights, ensure contracts clearly define the rights window, territory, and duration
- Protect your distribution infrastructure (screens, wiring, fiber) from competitors and bad actors
- When forming joint ventures, have clear agreements on profit sharing, decision making, and exit clauses
- Do not share your business plan or proprietary rights strategy publicly before executing
- Ensure compliance with FCC and local regulations for digital signage and advertising in airports

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Focusing only on the end customer and ignoring suppliers or partners.
  **Solution:** Map your entire ecosystem. Ask: who am I serving beyond the buyer? How can I serve them better?
- **Problem:** Trying to compete with Netflix or CBS on their own turf.
  **Solution:** Create a new rights window or category. Do not play their game — invent your own.
- **Problem:** Letting ego drive technology decisions (e.g., streaming everything in real time when stored playback works better).
  **Solution:** Choose the solution that works for the customer, not the one that makes you look innovative.
- **Problem:** Making relationships transactional and burning bridges.
  **Solution:** Always give before you ask. Keep in touch with people for years. The best deals come from long-term relationships.
- **Problem:** Not having a written business plan because "it is just for me."
  **Solution:** Write the business plan for yourself. It is your Superman cape — it prepares you for every objection and scenario.
