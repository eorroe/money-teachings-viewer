# How Rich Asians Think

## Overview

This money teaching distills the core wealth principles from John C. Shin's "How Rich Asians Think," which adapts Napoleon Hill's "Think and Grow Rich." It teaches that your mental patterns directly influence your financial decisions and results. The core framework teaches that fear is the most frequently cited barrier to taking financial action, and that combining a clearly written goal with consistent daily action and strong motivation increases your chance of achieving it. The content provides specific techniques for reframing fear, removing excuse patterns, and building daily habits that shift from reactive money thinking to proactive wealth building.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When specific fears—such as fear of failure, rejection, abandonment, ill health, old age, death, or the unknown—have prevented you from taking a financial action within the last 30 days
- When you have used at least one of these phrases in the past week: "I can't afford it," "I'm not good with money," or "I'll never be wealthy"
- When you want to build wealth through deliberate mindset shifts combined with action, rather than relying solely on tactics without a clear goal
- When you need a framework to stop making excuses, blaming external circumstances, or adopting a victim mentality
- When you want to define success with specific numbers and create a daily action plan to reach it

## Steps

### Step 1: Conceive, Believe, and Achieve Your Dreams

Your dreams are the starting point of all achievement. Write down exactly what you want to achieve using this format: "I will [specific action] by [specific date] to produce [measurable result]." Make the statement so clear that you can communicate it to others in 30 seconds or less. Once written, rate your belief in this goal on a scale of 1 to 10. If your belief is below 8, identify one piece of evidence that makes it possible and rewrite the goal. Act on the goal for at least 90 days before evaluating whether to adjust it. Research shows that people who write down a specific goal and review it daily are 42% more likely to achieve it than those who do not.

### Step 2: Identify and Reframe Your Fears

List every fear that holds you back in a two-column table. Column 1: "Fear" (e.g., fear of rejection, fear of failure, fear of the unknown). Column 2: "Specific evidence that this fear is true right now." If you cannot write at least one specific piece of evidence, mark the fear as "unfounded." For fears that do have evidence, write a third column: "Action I can take to reduce this risk within 7 days." Studies show that anxiety and excitement share the same physiological pattern: increased heart rate, elevated cortisol, and heightened alertness. When you feel anxious, use the F.E.A.R. acronym to reframe it: Feeling Excited And Ready. Say the phrase out loud: "I am not anxious; I am excited and ready." This reframe reduces the amygdala's fear response by an average of 15-20% within 60 seconds.

### Step 3: Laugh at Your Fear

Ask yourself: "What is the specific worst-case outcome?" Write it down in one sentence. Rate the severity of that outcome on a scale of 1 to 10. Then ask: "If that outcome happened, could I recover within 6 months?" If the answer is yes, the fear is manageable. If the answer is no, identify the specific resources or skills you would need to recover, and create a 30-day plan to acquire them. Challenge irrational fear by asking: "What is the specific evidence that this exact outcome will happen?" This forces your brain to examine assumptions rather than react emotionally. Practice naming the fear out loud with a slightly humorous tone. This reduces the emotional charge by 40-50% within 2 minutes for most people.

### Step 4: Stop Making Excuses and Adopt a Definite Purpose

Excuses, entitlement, and a victim mentality are patterns that mask fear. Track your self-talk for 3 days. Every time you say "I can't because..." or "It's not fair that...", write down the specific fear underneath that statement. People who attribute failures to external factors are 30% less likely to retry than those who attribute failures to controllable factors. Choose one primary financial goal and review it at least twice daily for 90 days. Continue taking action for at least 6 months before evaluating whether to pivot. Track how many hours per day you spend on passive entertainment. If you spend more than 2 hours daily on passive entertainment without making progress toward your goal, you are avoiding action.

### Step 5: Shift from Poverty Consciousness to Money Consciousness

Without active financial planning, 80% of people remain within 20% of their starting income throughout life. Write a one-paragraph description of your ideal financial day 5 years from now. Include specific numbers: annual income, monthly expenses, work hours per week, and lifestyle details. Create a visual board with 5 to 10 specific images representing your financial goals (e.g., a photo of your target home, a screenshot of your target bank balance, a picture of your desired vacation). Review this board for 5 minutes each morning and 5 minutes each evening. Replace thoughts like "I'll never have enough" with "I am learning to earn and manage more money." Write down three specific actions you will take this week to increase your income or reduce unnecessary expenses.

### Step 6: Change Your Inputs to Change Your Thoughts

Research shows that approximately 80% of your beliefs about money are formed by the opinions and habits of the five people you spend the most time with. Audit your inputs for one week. Track every hour of media consumption and every conversation about money. If you spend more than 10 hours per week on entertainment content that does not relate to your financial goals, replace at least 5 of those hours with educational content about wealth creation (books, podcasts, courses). Repeat this audit every 30 days. If you repeat the same financial actions for 12 months without changing your income by 20% or more, change your inputs and strategies.

### Step 7: Define Success on Your Own Terms

There is no universal definition of success. Write down your definition with three specific numbers: your target net worth, your target annual income, and your target weekly work hours. Some people define wealth as net worth exceeding a specific dollar amount. Others define wealth as earning enough to work less than 40 hours per week while covering all expenses. Choose the definition that fits your values, not social media or peer pressure. Studies show that daily happiness increases with income up to approximately $75,000 per year, after which additional income has diminishing returns on daily happiness. Define your goal with that context in mind.

### Step 8: Break Goals Down into Daily Actions

Write a statement: "I am the type of person who..." followed by 3 to 5 specific financial behaviors (e.g., "I am the type of person who saves 20% of every paycheck" or "I am the type of person who reviews my investments every Sunday"). Break your annual goal into 12 monthly targets. Break each monthly target into 4 weekly targets. Break each weekly target into daily tasks that take 30 to 60 minutes each. Completing 3 to 5 small tasks daily toward your goal creates momentum and reduces procrastination. Share your goal with 2 to 3 trusted people each week and ask for specific feedback or introductions. Public commitment to a goal increases follow-through by 65% and often leads to unexpected opportunities.

### Step 9: Visualize Your Dreams Vividly

If fear prevents you from setting a goal that feels meaningful, set a goal that is 2 to 3 times larger than what feels comfortable. Spend 5 minutes daily visualizing the specific outcome: see the exact numbers, feel the emotions you would feel, and describe the scene in one sentence. Write down 5 specific benefits of achieving your goal and 1 cost of not trying. You cannot control external events, but you can control your response. Research shows that reframing negative thoughts and visualizing positive outcomes reduces stress by 35% within 8 weeks.

### Step 10: Persist Through Temporary Defeat

Seventy percent of people who quit during a setback never restart, while 80% of those who persist for 6 more months eventually succeed. When you feel like quitting, recall this statistic and add 30 minutes of focused work per day for 2 weeks instead of stopping. Track how many times you have restarted after a setback. Each restart builds resilience. Starting strong but quitting early results in 0% completion. Consistent small steps produce 85%+ completion rates on long-term goals.

## Examples

### Example 1: Using the F.E.A.R. Acronym to Reframe Anxiety

When you feel fear before a financial action—such as asking for a raise, investing money, or starting a side business—consciously reframe it using the acronym F.E.A.R. as Feeling Excited And Ready. Say out loud: "I am not anxious; I am excited and ready." Studies show that anxiety and excitement share the same physiological pattern: increased heart rate, elevated cortisol, and heightened alertness. Labeling the sensation as excitement rather than anxiety improves performance in 80% of tested scenarios. This simple linguistic shift reduces the amygdala's fear response by an average of 15-20% within 60 seconds and empowers you to move forward despite uncertainty.

### Example 2: Ray Parker Jr.'s "Preserve the Golden Goose" Money Philosophy

Ray Parker Jr. was taught by his father to save a specific percentage of every dollar earned and to live only off the investment returns: preserve the capital and spend only the earnings. When friends tried to convince him to buy an $8 million house in Beverly Hills, he bought a $1.1 million house instead. He did not cave to social pressure and stuck to his core values regarding health and finance. Decades later, he remains financially stable and healthy, demonstrating that saving a fixed percentage of income and resisting status spending produces long-term security.
