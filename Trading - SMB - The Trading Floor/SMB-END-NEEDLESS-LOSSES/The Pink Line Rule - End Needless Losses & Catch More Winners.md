# SMB: The Pink Line Rule - End Needless Losses & Catch More Winners

## Overview

The pink line rule is a trading methodology where traders only take breakout trades above a level where the stock has been rejected at least three times with each failed test creating covering pressure from shorts, re-entry demand from stopped-out traders, and buying interest from new participants below the level (the "pink line"). This prevents losses from entries before the level is confirmed and enforces a rule-based process. The rule simplifies trading decisions by creating a defined boundary: below the line, wait and do not chase; above the line, the opportunity begins. It addresses the psychological traps of fear of missing out (FOMO) and overtrading by giving traders a concrete visual and process-based filter that removes emotion from the decision.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you chase breakouts and take losses from entries before the level is confirmed
- When you struggle with fear of missing out and overtrading around breakout levels
- When you want a rule-based framework with a single visual filter and two sets of rules for entering breakout trades
- When you are trading stocks with consolidation ranges and levels tested at least three times on volume, not untested intraday highs
- When you want to avoid the mistake of buying below the breakout level only to watch the stock reverse

## Steps

### Step 1: Identify and Mark Your Pink Line

Find the level on your chart where the stock has been rejected at least three times — a consolidation top or prior rejection level tested across sessions, not a single-session pivot high. A test qualifies if either condition is met: a price excursion above the level that reverses such that the close is at least half the 14-period daily ATR below the level, or a wick extending at least 1% of the level price above the level that reverses to a close below the level. Make this line bold and bright (pink) on your chart so it appears above all indicator and prior level lines in your chart's layer stack. Place it on a daily or weekly time frame, never an untested intraday high. The level should be one where the stock shows volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day simple moving average of daily volume and holds above it when it breaks. A strong pink line has been tested at least three times on volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average with each failed test creating covering pressure from shorts, re-entry demand from stopped-out traders, and buying interest from new participants below the level: shorts who need to cover, traders who got stopped out and want to re-enter, and new buyers waiting to step in.

### Step 2: Build Rules for When the Stock Is Below the Pink Line

When the stock is below the pink line, your default action is to wait, with exceptions described in Step 5. Do not enter within 1% below the pink line without confirmation. Do not place orders above the level expecting it to break (anticipate). Remember: if you follow a rule-based filter, you reduce the risk of missing confirmed setups. Without defined entry criteria, traders are prone to overtrading. The pink line simplifies the decision by showing you that you are not missing any confirmed breakout opportunity below this point under the default rules. Breakout opportunity only occurs above the pink line under normal conditions; specific exceptions are documented in Step 5. Wait for confirmation that the stock holds above the level before entering.

### Step 3: Build Rules for When the Stock Is Above the Pink Line

When the stock breaks above the pink line, that is when you enter or place orders. Do not buy the exact second it crosses. Wait for confirmation that it holds above the level for at least 5 minutes (measured as one fully closed 5-minute candle above the level, or 300 continuous seconds without a single tick below the level; any tick below resets the timer to zero), or wait for Level 2 (order book) data showing buy orders resting at or within one tick of the pink line price exceeding sell orders by at least 2:1 without sell orders exceeding 100,000 shares pushing price back below. This confirmation improves your win rate. There is a difference between buying through the level and waiting for it to hold - the latter gives you better information.

### Step 4: Consider Time of Day and Energy

Be especially careful in the first 10 minutes of regular trading hours (09:30–09:40 ET for U.S. equities). This period is price discovery with frequent fakeouts and frequent mean reversion. If a stock opens a distance of half the 14-period daily average true range (ATR) below the pink line and rallies to it, it has already moved half the 14-period daily ATR and the 5-period rate-of-change of price is less than or equal to zero. Ask yourself: what are you giving up by waiting? If you are looking for a 10-to-20-point move in a stock, giving up a small fraction of the expected move — for example, 50 cents on a stock expected to move 10 to 20 points — to see if it actually holds the level is worth it. Do not buy above a level that has already run at least half the 14-period daily ATR — the risk/reward is reduced and it pulls back.

### Step 5: Know When to Break the Rule (Nuances)

There are specific scenarios where buying below the pink line is allowed:
- **Catalysts**: A scheduled or unscheduled event with a visible price and volume impact on your chart occurs while you are already watching the trade. The context has changed and you can anticipate the breakout because the volume will lead to it. Examples: Gold on a Fed pivot announcement; SMCI before an earnings pre-announcement.
- **Compressed Ranges**: If the stock has been consolidating in a range whose width is less than half the 14-period daily ATR for two consecutive regular trading sessions (09:30–16:00 ET, excluding weekends and holidays) — a two-session consolidation — and breaks out of that compressed range, you can anticipate the pink line breakout because the range has narrowed and the stock has not already run half the 14-period daily ATR to reach the level.
- **Strong Closes**: For swing trades, if a stock closes at or within the top 2% of the day's range (calculated as (close - low) / (high - low) ≥ 0.98) after outperforming its sector by at least 2 percentage points (stock return minus sector return ≥ 2%) or ranking in the top quartile of S&P 500 performers by percentage gain and breaking a range spanning less than the 14-period daily ATR over three sessions, and block trades (≥ 10,000 shares) at the close exceed 20% of the day's total volume with buy volume exceeding sell volume by at least 2:1 during the final 30 minutes of the session, then the close carries a specific price/volume pattern indicating conviction, and you may start getting in below the pink line because this combination increases the probability of a breakout on the next trading session.
- **Outlier Scenarios**: When a stock is actively trading on volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average (where the 20-day average is the simple moving average of daily volume over the prior 20 trading sessions), confirmed alongside a catalyst event — a 1-hour or 4-hour time-frame level that meets the same test criteria (at least three rejections, volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average) as daily/weekly levels can serve as a high-grade pink line for that session only, but only in a stock with volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average and catalyst-driven interest.

### Step 6: Grade Your Pink Lines

Not all pink lines are equal. Grade them based on:
- Time frame (daily and weekly are best because if the stock gets above a daily or weekly level, new buyers step in — the stock experiences follow-through buying from new participants; it should not be just a line on a five-minute or lower-time-frame chart, except under the catalyst and compressed-range exceptions described in Step 5)
- Number of tests (at least three tests on volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average)
- How narrow the price range is relative to the average true range near the level — measure how many 14-period daily ATRs the range spans; a range spanning fewer than one 14-period daily ATR means compressed volatility is present
- Relative strength (stock outperforming its sector by at least 2 percentage points or ranking in the top quartile of S&P 500 performers) and sector relative strength ranking and overall market regime
A line that has been tested five times on volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average where prior sellers covered, evident from the rejection and volume profile, generating buy orders below the level is stronger than a simple pivot high. Each additional test increases the number of participants with orders below the level: shorts who need to cover, traders who got stopped out and want to re-enter, and new buyers waiting to step in — each failed test generates more buy orders below the level that execute when price finally breaks above, improving the follow-through rate of the eventual breakout.

## Examples

### Example 1: Tesla False Breakouts

Traders were watching Tesla in a range less than half the 14-period daily average true range (ATR) setting up for a breakout. Every day off the open, it would outperform its sector by at least 2 percentage points or rank in the top quartile of S&P 500 performers by percentage gain. It would run in a way that stood out from all other stocks, exhibiting relative strength, ranking in the top quartile of S&P 500 performers by percentage gain, getting attention. They chased it every morning only to watch it fall back to the prior day's lows before finally breaking out. The losses from the entries made before the level held offset any gain from the confirmed breakout. The difference between the successful breakout and the failed ones? The winning day opened much closer to the pink line, while the chasing days opened a distance of half the 14-period daily average true range (ATR) below it. They were buying above the breakout level before it held instead of waiting for confirmation.

### Example 2: The "No Trade" Decision

There was a breakout that traders were watching where the advance-decline line (a breadth indicator measuring advancing minus declining stocks) and up-volume exceeded down-volume — the QQQ (the Invesco QQQ ETF) was rallying with volume above the 20-day average and stocks were strong — and the stock had already moved one full average true range (ATR) to reach the pink line by midday. It looked like a bullish intraday setup with bid depth throughout the session. But it was still below the pink line and it was between 11:00 and 14:00 ET, when prior-day momentum declines and the time of day does not support sustained moves. They waited. It never closed above the level. The correct decision was not to take the trade - waiting was validated. The answer to "what am I giving up by waiting?" was zero.

### Example 3: Gold Breakout (Fed Pivot)

Gold was in the middle of a large weekly range when a Fed pivot announcement hit. Even though it was below the pink line and within the bottom 5% of that range (i.e., (price - low) / (high - low) ≤ 0.05), the catalyst combined with observable price and volume patterns made it an allowed entry. This was still a breakout trade - they were anticipating the breakout because the catalyst would generate the volume needed to break the level. The critical factor was recognizing the catalyst's impact on price and volume and seeing confirmation on the price and volume data.

### Example 4: SMCI Pre-Announcement

SMCI (Super Micro Computer, Inc.) issued an early earnings release and was trading at 360 before its stock split. It was below the pink line in pre-market but the catalyst was so large that the pre-market price was less than 2% above the prior session close, making the breakout probable. This shows that you are not ignoring the pink line - you are recognizing that the catalyst changes the conditions under which the rule applies.

### Example 5: QQQ Range Example

QQQ had been stuck in a range for at least three sessions with at least three days where it had set up for a breakout, but it never closed above the level. If you had been trying to buy every time it looked strong within the range, you would have overtraded. The pink line rule would have kept you out until a confirmed breakout. The answer to "what am I giving up by waiting for a close above the level?" was zero. Waiting preserves your capital and keeps you from making FOMO-driven decisions.

## Best Practices

- Build a full methodology around the line - rules for below and rules for above
- Wait for the stock to hold above the level for at least 5 minutes (measured as one fully closed 5-minute candle above the level; in the event of a conflict, the 5-minute candle takes precedence over continuous tick data and Level 2 (order book) data) before entering
- Ask yourself "what am I giving up by waiting?" when you feel urgency to enter
- Watch for the price to hold at the pink line without sell orders exceeding 100,000 shares pushing price back below, with limit buy orders visible at or near the pink line, as confirmation
- Grade your pink lines based on time frame, number of tests, and volume
- Be patient off the open - the first 10 minutes of regular trading hours (09:30–09:40 ET for U.S. equities) are price discovery with frequent fakeouts and frequent mean reversion; avoid entering on opening drive moves in this window
- Trade within 5% of the pink line price, not in the middle of the range where price/volume patterns are less distinct
- Monitor whether the stock has moved more than half the 14-period daily ATR to reach the level — if it has already run at least half the 14-period daily ATR, the 5-period rate-of-change of price is less than or equal to zero and the risk/reward is no longer there; do not chase moves where the 5-period rate-of-change of price is less than or equal to zero
- Look for closes at or within the top 2% of the day's range — where closing price is in the top 2% of the day's range with volume above the 20-day average — and sector outperformance of at least 2 percentage points or top-quartile ranking as bullish indicators
- Do not buy above a level that has already run at least half the 14-period daily ATR to reach the pink line
- Do not buy the exact second the stock crosses the pink line out of fear of missing out
- Do not use untested intraday highs as pink lines — use daily or weekly levels tested at least three times on volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average, where a test is a price excursion above the level that reverses such that the close is at least half the 14-period daily ATR below the level, or a wick extending at least 1% of the level price above the level that reverses to a close below the level
- Do not trade in the middle of ranges trying to predict direction
- Do not let fear of missing out cause you to buy above the level without waiting for confirmation
- When breaking the rule for a catalyst, apply the exception only after you can see the catalyst's impact on the price and volume data and confirm that the current session's volume is at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average; catalysts differ in their effect, and only those meeting the volume threshold qualify as exceptions

## Keep In Mind

- The pink line rule works across time frames, but daily and weekly levels are preferred because they capture the accumulation of participants over sessions and produce follow-through rates higher than those of intraday time frames.
- Breakouts at the open happen but are the exception, not the norm
- A sharp move off the open — where a stock opens at least 0.5 times the 14-period daily ATR below the pink line and rallies to it — can reverse quickly when the stock has not yet broken the level; a close at or within the top 2% of the day's range provides a stronger signal of buying pressure than a rapid intraday rally that reverses
- If you follow a rule-based filter, you reduce the risk of missing confirmed setups - patience comes from having defined rules
- The objective is not to catch every move - it is to avoid losses from entries before confirmation while catching confirmed breakouts
- Choosing not to take a trade is a validated decision when the stock never closes above the level
- At least three tests and failures at a level trap shorts who need to cover, traders who got stopped out and want to re-enter, and new buyers waiting to step in — all generating buy orders below the level that execute when price finally breaks above, improving the follow-through rate of the eventual breakout.
- Stocks in a range often exhibit regular volatility and mean reversion — typically involve price discovery, choppiness, and price reversals at support and resistance — look for three consecutive daily bars with higher highs and higher lows on the daily chart with volume above the 20-day average, indicating a shift from choppy to trending
- You can afford to wait because under the default rules, you are not missing any confirmed breakout opportunity below the pink line.
- The open is where fear of missing out is most intense in the first 10 minutes of regular trading hours — stocks often make rapid intraday rallies that reverse, causing traders to chase strength off the open — recognize this and do not let it drive your decisions

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Chasing stocks off the open that look strong but have not broken the pink line
  **Solution:** Wait for the breakout above the pink line with confirmation that it holds for at least 5 minutes (measured as one fully closed 5-minute candle above the level, or 300 continuous seconds without a single tick below the level; any tick below resets the timer to zero). Strength off the open often fails when the stock has not broken the level — the open involves price discovery, fakeouts, and mean reversion, especially when the stock opens at least half the 14-period daily ATR below the level and the 5-period rate-of-change of price is less than or equal to zero. If it opens a distance of half the 14-period daily ATR below, it has already moved too far before reaching the level.

- **Problem:** Buying the exact moment the stock crosses the pink line out of fear of missing out
  **Solution:** Let it get above the level and hold for at least 5 minutes (measured as one fully closed 5-minute candle above the level, or 300 continuous seconds without a single tick below the level; any tick below resets the timer to zero), or wait for Level 2 (order book) data showing buy orders resting at or within one tick of the pink line price exceeding sell orders by at least 2:1 without sell orders exceeding 100,000 shares pushing price back below. Require the stock to hold above the level for the confirmation period before entering.

- **Problem:** Trading in the middle of a range and interpreting noise as signal
  **Solution:** Focus on the edges of the range where there is a distinct price/volume pattern. Trades in the middle of the range have less distinct price/volume patterns and lead to overtrading and lower-probability decisions.

- **Problem:** Using untested intraday highs as pink lines
  **Solution:** Grade your lines. Use daily or weekly levels tested at least three times on volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average where a test is a price excursion above the level that reverses such that the close is at least half the 14-period daily ATR below the level, or a wick extending at least 1% of the level price above the level that reverses to a close below the level, and the stock shows volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average when it breaks. An intraday high with no prior tests on volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average is not a pink line.

- **Problem:** Entering a stock that has moved more than half the 14-period daily ATR to reach the pink line without breaking out
  **Solution:** Monitor whether the stock has already moved more than half the 14-period daily ATR to reach the level. If it has moved that far without breaking out, the 5-period rate-of-change (ROC) of price is less than or equal to zero, where ROC = (current price - price 5 periods ago) / (price 5 periods ago), indicating the risk/reward is no longer there — entering more than 1% above the prior close without confirmation is an entry to avoid.

- **Problem:** Using the catalyst exception as a reason to abandon process discipline
  **Solution:** When breaking the rule for a catalyst, confirm the catalyst's impact on the price and volume data with volume at least two standard deviations above the 20-day average. Catalysts differ in their effect - you need visible price and volume patterns on the chart, and the catalyst must meet the volume threshold.
