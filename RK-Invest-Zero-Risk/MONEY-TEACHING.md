# Learn To Invest Like Robert Kiyosaki - With ZERO Risk

## Overview

This money teaching demonstrates how to achieve an infinite return on a real estate investment with zero personal capital invested and effectively zero risk. Ken McElroy, Robert Kiyosaki's partner and co-author, walks through a live Tucson, Arizona apartment deal where he and Robert purchased a 144-unit property plus vacant land, built 112 additional units using a construction loan, grew rents to increase the property value, and then refinanced to pull out every dollar they had originally put in — leaving them with $0 invested while collecting $120,000 per year in cash flow going forward.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you want to invest in income-producing real estate using other people's money rather than your own savings
- When you want to eliminate personal financial risk from an investment by pulling all your capital out through refinancing
- When you want to create infinite return — recovering 100% of invested capital while the asset continues to generate income indefinitely
- When you want to understand how high financial IQ investors legally avoid capital gains taxes on investment profits
- When you are evaluating a property with expansion potential and want to model a zero-money-down, zero-risk deal structure

## Steps

### Step 1: Find a Property With Expansion Potential and Undervalued or Underutilized Land

Identify a property that already generates income but has physical capacity to add more units or square footage. In the example deal, Ken and Robert found a 144-unit apartment house in Tucson, Arizona that came with adjacent vacant land. They determined the vacant land could support an additional 112 units, nearly doubling the property's total unit count without acquiring new land.

### Step 2: Purchase the Property and Use the Land or Existing Equity to Secure a Construction Loan

Buy the property at a price that leaves room for added value. Use the land already owned as collateral for a construction loan rather than putting additional personal cash into the deal. In the example, the property was purchased for approximately $10 million with roughly $7 million down and a $7 million construction loan from the bank. The existing land secured the construction financing, meaning no more money out of pocket was required to build the additional units.

### Step 3: Build Additional Units at Wholesale Cost to Maximize Property Value

Use the construction loan to build out the additional units. Building at wholesale cost keeps total project expenses as low as possible, which preserves cash flow once the property is operating. In the example, the 112 additional units were built using the construction loan, bringing the total from 144 to 256 units. Total project cost including land and construction was approximately $12 million.

### Step 4: Conduct a Market Survey and Raise Rents to Boost Net Operating Income

Before or shortly after purchasing, commission a market survey to determine how much rents can be raised across existing units. Choose a market where population is growing and the supply of rental housing is undersupplied relative to demand — for example, near a large university. In the example, the market survey showed that rents on all 144 existing units could be raised by $50 to $75 per unit. Rents were also expected to grow naturally due to strong local demand from the University of Arizona. Rents grew approximately 20% over the following three years. The key formula is: Net Operating Income equals total income minus operating expenses. Keep expenses at or below market rates so that increased rent translates directly into higher net operating income and higher property value.

### Step 5: Operate the Property With a Management Company to Control Expenses and Grow Cash Flow

Manage the property yourself or through a company you control so that you can keep operating expenses aligned with market rates while pushing rental income higher. In the example, Ken operated the property through his own management company, which allowed him to grow both rents and net operating income in a controlled way.

### Step 6: Refinance the Property Once Its Appraised Value Has Grown Substantially

After 12 to 18 months (or once the bank appraises the property at a meaningfully higher value based on the improved net operating income), refinance the property to pull out the equity gained. In the example, after approximately 1.5 years, the bank appraised the property at $18.5 million — up from the roughly $15 million estimated completion value and far above the roughly $12 million total invested. Property appraisals are based on net operating income (total income minus operating expenses). Higher rents and controlled expenses drove the higher appraised value.

### Step 7: Pay Back Original Investors Their Principal Plus Any Preferred Return, Then Split the Remaining Equity

Use the refinance proceeds to first repay all original investors their original capital plus any agreed-upon preferred return. Only then split the remaining equity with your partners. In the example, the refinance produced $9.5 million in proceeds. After paying off the $7 million bank loan and returning original investors their capital plus preferred return, Ken recovered his own $1 million contribution and split the remaining balance with his partners.

### Step 8: Maintain Cash Flow With Zero Dollars Invested by Keeping the Property

Do not sell the property after refinancing. Selling triggers capital gains taxes and eliminates the ongoing income stream. By keeping the property, you retain full ownership with zero of your own money invested, and the asset continues to produce monthly cash flow indefinitely. In the example, after the refinance Ken had $0 of his own money in the deal. He still collects approximately $10,000 per month ($120,000 per year) in cash flow from the property, and the cash flow continues forever as long as the property is operated.

### Step 9: Repeat the Process to Scale the Portfolio

Once you have pulled all your capital out of one deal, you can repeat the same strategy on additional properties. In the example, Ken and Robert have accumulated thousands of units using this same zero-risk, infinite-return approach, and they continue to do so even during market downturns because their properties are always higher-occupied with rents rising, which pushes values further up.

## Examples

### Example 1: Tucson, Arizona 256-Unit Apartment Complex

Ken McElroy went to Robert Kiyosaki with an investment in a 144-unit apartment house with adjacent vacant land in Tucson, Arizona. They purchased the property for approximately $10 million and used the land to secure a $7 million construction loan to build 112 additional units, bringing the total to 256 units. Total investment including construction was approximately $12 million. A market survey showed they could raise rents by $50 to $75 per unit across all 144 existing units, and the local market had strong rental demand driven by University of Arizona. Rents grew nearly 20% over the following three years. After approximately 1.5 years, the bank appraised the property at $18.5 million based on the higher net operating income. They refinanced, pulled out $9.5 million, paid off the $7 million loan, returned all original investors their principal plus preferred return, and Ken got his $1 million back tax-free (because refinance proceeds are a loan, not taxable income). He still collects approximately $10,000 per month in cash flow from the property with zero dollars invested.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Choose a market with a growing population and an undersupply of rental housing relative to demand
- ✅ Conduct a market survey before purchasing to confirm the rent growth potential of every existing unit
- ✅ Use land or existing equity to secure construction loans rather than putting more personal cash into the deal
- ✅ Operate the property through your own management company to control expenses and maximize net operating income
- ✅ Refinance only after the property's appraised value has grown significantly based on increased net operating income
- ✅ Pay back original investors their principal plus any preferred return before splitting remaining equity with partners
- ✅ Keep the property after refinancing rather than selling, so you retain ongoing cash flow with zero invested capital
- ✅ Repeat the process on additional properties to scale your portfolio of zero-investment, cash-flowing assets
- ❌ Do not put more personal money into a deal than necessary — use other people's money and the bank's financing
- ❌ Do not sell the property after refinancing — selling triggers capital gains taxes and eliminates your passive income stream
- ❌ Do not skip the market survey — rent growth assumptions must be verified before purchasing the property
- ❌ Do not allow operating expenses to rise above market rates — higher expenses reduce net operating income and property value

## Keep In Mind

- Refinance proceeds are a loan from the bank, not income, so returning your original capital through a refinance is legally tax-free
- The property technically belongs to the bank until the mortgage is paid off, but you retain full ownership rights and all cash flow
- Infinite return means you have recovered 100% of your original investment while the asset continues to produce income every month
- Net Operating Income equals total rental income minus all operating expenses — this is the number that drives property value
- The bank is your partner in this strategy: they provide the financing, you own the appreciating asset
- This strategy works best in markets where population growth creates sustained demand for rental housing

## Security & Safety Notes

- All transactions in the example deal were conducted legally and in full compliance with tax law
- The $1 million return to Ken was tax-free because it came from a refinance loan, not from the sale of an asset
- Always consult a qualified tax professional and real estate attorney before structuring a deal of this type
- Securities laws may apply if you bring in outside investors — ensure proper disclosures and agreements are in place
- Construction loans and refinances must be structured through legitimate banking channels with proper documentation

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Overestimating rent growth or underestimating operating expenses, causing the property's net operating income to fall short of projections
  **Solution:** Conduct a thorough market survey before purchasing and track actual expenses against comparable market rates every quarter
- **Problem:** Running out of cash flow after refinancing because the higher loan balance creates larger debt service payments
  **Solution:** Ensure the property still generates positive cash flow after the new loan is in place before finalizing the refinance
- **Problem:** Selling the property after refinancing and triggering capital gains taxes on the appreciation
  **Solution:** Refinance instead of selling to extract equity without tax consequences, and retain the property for ongoing cash flow
- **Problem:** Choosing a market where rental demand is weak or declining, causing vacancies and rent stagnation
  **Solution:** Select markets with growing populations, major employers or universities, and documented undersupply of rental housing
- **Problem:** Partnering with investors without a clear agreement on how refinance proceeds are distributed
  **Solution:** Define the preferred return and equity split structure in writing before bringing in outside capital
