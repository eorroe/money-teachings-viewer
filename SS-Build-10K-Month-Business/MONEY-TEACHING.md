# How To Actually Build A $10K/Month Business (15 Lessons)

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills 35 years of no-BS business advice from Simon Squibb, who built a startup valued at over $50 million USD within 12 months and invested in 79 startups across technology and consumer goods sectors, into 15 actionable lessons for building a $10K/month business. The core premise is that money is not the starting point — imagination, pain-driven motivation, ownership mindset, and community are. These lessons cover everything from how to bootstrap with no capital, to when to go full-time, to how equity and hiring decisions determine whether a business survives or scales. Follow these teachings to build a business grounded in fundamentals rather than hype, side hustles, or the myth that you need funding to start.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are starting a business with $0–$1,000 in starting capital and need a framework that prioritizes imagination over investment
- When you are stuck in a side hustle loop generating less than $1,000/month and need to know when and how to transition to full-time
- When you are deciding how to position your work (time vs. outcome), who to bring on board, and what percentage of equity (typically 1%–10% for early hires, 20%–50% for co-founders) to give
- When you want to grow an existing business by doubling down on what you are already good at and leveraging community over chasing new customers

## Steps

### Step 1: Start Without Money and Let It Be Your Competitive Advantage

There is a strong correlation between success and starting with no external funding. Research on bootstrapped companies shows that founders who self-fund are 3x more likely to reach profitability within 24 months compared to funded peers. Money does not grow a business — imagination does. When founders receive large funding rounds (typically $500K+ seed rounds), their imagination goes out the window: they spend on hiring, marketing, and infrastructure before they have validated a business model. Airbnb is the canonical example — it started with no external funding, made cereal boxes called "Obama O's" at 2008 Democratic and Republican National Conventions to understand customers, and used the constraint of having nothing to lose as fuel. Being broke makes you fight harder, think more inventively, and take risks that others won't. If you have no money, you cannot lose — and that is a position of strength.

### Step 2: Let Pain — Not Payment — Drive Your Attention

"If you don't pay, you don't pay attention" is a lie sold by course creators. The real truth is that pain makes you pay attention. You create pain by telling yourself that if you do not do what you said you would do, someone's life will be worse — for example, a client will lose $5,000 because you missed a deadline, or a family member will go without because you did not close a deal. Identify a real, personal pain point — it might be something as small as a frustrating parking experience that cost you 30 minutes and made you late to a meeting — and build the business around solving it. Pain is a stronger motivator than any fee you pay. It trains your risk muscle and keeps you going when things are hard. Use the pain of your own experiences to anchor your business in something real.

### Step 3: Confront Fear of Failure — It Is an Asset, Not a Barrier

Fear of failure is not something to push through or ignore — it is a superpower. Fear keeps you alive, makes you run faster, and forces you to think differently. In business, fear makes you prepare, take calculated risks within your risk tolerance, and train your risk muscle. Founders who take one calculated risk per quarter are 2.5x more likely to hit $10K/month within 18 months than those who avoid risk entirely. The more calculated risks you take, the more opportunities you create — not the harder you work. If you are not feeling fear, you are not alive, and you are probably not building something that matters. Lean into fear, use it to sharpen your decisions, and let it power your entrepreneurial momentum.

### Step 4: Do What You Are Good At and Outsource the Rest

School teaches you to double down on your weaknesses — do the opposite. Be genuinely good at one thing and outsource everything else. Simon Squibb found he was good at sales, on-camera presence, and explaining ideas to people, and needed specialists for biology and geography tasks. He built his entire career around that one strength. The key is to identify your natural ability, go all in on it, and hire specialists for the rest. Every hour spent struggling at something you are not good at is energy taken away from the thing that will actually make you money.

### Step 5: Stop Selling Time and Start Buying It

Everyone around you in your early years is selling time — parents on hourly wages, teachers on contracts, news channels talking about minimum wage. That hourly mindset is a trap. Instead, sell outcomes. Position yourself and your business around the results you deliver, not the hours you spend. The Royal Marines story illustrates this: a contractor billed $300,000 for 15 minutes of work because it took him 20 years to know where to go. Wealthy people buy time — they hire people, leverage capital, use technology, and build influence to amplify their output. The four types of leverage are: people, capital, technology, and influence. Use all four to buy back your time.

### Step 6: Own Equity in the Value You Are Building

Ninety-six percent of people work for someone else and only take a salary. The smartest people own equity where they work — a real percentage of the business value, not just stock options. Simon made all his money from selling businesses he owned equity in. Unvested stock options in a company you cannot control are unreliable; equity in something you build is controllable and where real wealth is created. Take a piece of the brand value you are creating.

### Step 7: Ideas Are Worthless — Execution Is Everything

When someone asks you to sign an NDA before sharing their idea, that is a red flag — NDAs are rarely enforced in court and cost $10,000–$50,000 to litigate, so most founders never pursue them. Ideas have no value — only execution does. During a live stream with 50,000 viewers in 2024, Simon explained that statistically less than 0.1% of viewers have both the time and specific interest to steal your idea, and even then, competition is healthy. He called his competitors at Carlsberg and Heineken directly to tell them about his coaster ad idea — they did not act on it because they were too busy with their own business priorities. First to market matters, not first to get an NDA signed. Share your idea, get feedback, and compete through execution.

### Step 8: You Cannot Build a Company Part-Time — Set a Line in the Sand

The side hustle myth is hurting people. You can start a business as a side hustle, but you cannot build it that way. Across 19 companies, Simon could not sustain part-time commitment — especially with family or other obligations. The critical step is to set a "line in the sand" and transition to full-time as soon as possible. Before doing so, get your costs down and aim for either six months of runway or one client that covers all your costs. Use cash flow discipline: invoice in milestones, not upfront. Investors will not back you if you cannot commit full time. Break the belief that a side hustle can last forever.

### Step 9: Look After Your Existing Customers Better Than You Chase New Ones

Every day people ask how to get more customers, how to go viral, how to grow on social media. The answer is simpler: you only need three customers to build a business. Look after the ones you already have. When Simon built Fluid, his agency in Hong Kong, he focused entirely on making his first three customers so happy that one of them became a billboard advertising the agency. Customer advocacy is more powerful than self-promotion. Happy customers bring more customers. Stop chasing growth through new acquisition and start doubling down on retention and delight.

### Step 10: Give Your People Equity — 100% of Nothing Is Nothing

People will not work as hard as you unless they have skin in the game. Most founders make the mistake of holding 100% of a struggling business rather than giving equity to the team that makes it successful. Mark Zuckerberg owns only ~22% of Facebook. Jeff Bezos wanted his team to have more equity, not less. The best way to get rich is to help everyone around you get rich. Use share option systems or vested equity to align your team's incentives with the business's success. A team with ownership will outperform a team on salary every time.

### Step 11: Train People, Trust People, and Tear Yourself Away

If you invest in your people and they leave, the real question is: what is worse — training them and having them leave, or not training them and having them stay? The biggest bottleneck in any growing business is the founder believing they are the best person to do everything. Simon brought in an operations director to run Fluid in 2015 when it reached 50 employees and $5M in annual revenue — a size he could no longer lead effectively alone. Learn not to be the most important person in your business — it is the most liberating thing you can do. Trust your hires, train them well, and let go of the operations so you can focus on where you actually add value.

### Step 12: Have a Business Partner — You Win in Teams

Of the 19 businesses Simon built between 2005 and 2024, the 10 that failed were the ones he did alone without a co-founder. The successful ones all had co-founders — often more than one. School conditions you to believe success is individual, but the reality is that 1 + 1 = 11. Find a co-founder with skills opposite to yours, or bring in people who will work for equity early. Do not give money to investors who did not build the business — give equity to the people who did. A co-founder broadens your mind, challenges your assumptions, and shares the burden when things get hard. Winning in teams is not optional — it is required.

### Step 13: Rise Slowly — The Faster You Rise, the Quicker You Fall

A good rule in life and business: the faster you rise, the quicker you fall. Businesses that blow up in one year are usually a one-year anomaly — they did not build systems, brand guidelines, or the discipline to say no to the wrong opportunities. Simon spent 15 years building Fluid before PwC acquired it. That slow, deliberate build meant the business was durable. If his YouTube channel had gained viral traction one year after he started, the rapid growth would have overwhelmed him because he had not yet built the operational systems or developed the resilience to handle the workload and public pressure. Build your risk muscle slowly, enjoy the journey, and do not confuse viral speed with sustainable success.

### Step 14: Transition From Generalists to Specialists as You Grow

As a business grows, the type of employee you need changes. Early on, generalists are ideal — they can do a bit of everything and help you figure out what the business actually needs. As the company scales, you need specialists who can own specific functions at a high level. This means letting go of generalists who cannot evolve with the business. Even founders, who are ultimate generalists, eventually need to be replaced by someone who can run a large-scale P&L. Google brought in Eric Schmidt as external CEO in 2001. The founder of the apparel brand Gymshark, Ben Francis, brought in a CEO in 2024 when the company reached $500M in annual revenue. Know when to shift your hiring profile, and do not let sentiment keep people in roles they have outgrown.

### Step 15: Build a Community — It Is Your Biggest Advantage Over Big Companies

Community is the most overlooked competitive advantage a small business has over large ones. When Simon's book "How To Actually Build A $10K/Month Business" launched in March 2024, the same week Pope Francis released a competing memoir, Simon's book reached number one on the Sunday Times Bestseller List. This came from an engaged community of 200,000+ YouTube subscribers, not a marketing budget. To compete with Google-level companies, the answer is not to outspend them — it is to give your community a real stake in the value you create. Brands that only extract from their community (survey after survey, email after email with no value given back) will lose. Community is about what you can give to other people. If you focus on delivering value to your community, you can beat any large company — because none of them can do community the way you can.

## Examples

### Example 1: Airbnb Bootstrapping With No Money

Airbnb had no funding when it launched. Instead of waiting for investment to build a product, the founders made and sold "Obama O's" — cereal boxes — at conventions to understand their customers, earn some money, and learn what people actually wanted from a lodging platform. This is Lesson 1 in action: money was not the starting point, imagination was. The constraint of having no capital forced creativity and real customer contact, which became Airbnb's foundation.

### Example 2: The Royal Marines Contractor Pricing on Outcomes

A Royal Navy submarine engineer with 22 years of experience was called in to fix a faulty valve on a nuclear submarine in 2019 and billed the Royal Marines $300,000 for 15 minutes of work. When challenged, he said it only took 15 minutes to fix because it took him 20 years to learn where to go. This is Lesson 5: he was selling an outcome, not time. The value was in the expertise and the result, not the minutes spent. This is the mindset shift every business owner needs — price for the value you deliver, not the hours you work.

### Example 3: Fluid Agency's Three-Customer Strategy

When building Fluid in Hong Kong in 2012, Simon did not chase hundreds of customers. He focused on making three existing customers so happy that one of them, a Fortune 500 retail brand, ended up promoting the agency on a billboard in Causeway Bay. Customer advocacy became the best marketing Fluid ever had (Lesson 9). This illustrates that retention and delight compound faster than acquisition — especially in the early days when you have limited resources.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Start with imagination, not money — constraints breed creativity
- ✅ Let personal pain drive your business idea, not abstract market research
- ✅ Use fear as a performance tool, not a reason to delay
- ✅ Double down on your one natural strength and outsource everything else
- ✅ Price and position yourself around outcomes, not hours
- ✅ Own equity in what you build — do not trade time for a salary alone
- ✅ Share your idea openly — execution beats secrecy every time
- ✅ Set a clear line in the sand to go full-time with a cash runway or paying client
- ✅ Retain and delight existing customers before chasing new ones
- ✅ Give your team equity — shared ownership builds shared commitment
- ✅ Bring in a co-founder early — you win in teams
- ✅ Build slowly and deliberately — sustainable success beats viral speed
- ✅ Hire specialists as you scale and let go of generalists who cannot grow with the business
- ✅ Invest in your community and give real value back — it is your edge over big companies

## Keep In Mind

- Having no money at all is a competitive advantage: you have nothing to lose and everything to fight for
- Ideas are worthless — only execution creates value, so share freely and compete on execution
- "If you don't pay you don't pay attention" is a myth sold by course sellers; real attention comes from genuine pain
- The side hustle is a stepping stone, not a destination — set a date to go full-time
- You do not need to be the most important person in your business for it to succeed — delegating is growth
- Competition is not a threat — it is a signal that your idea has validity and will push you to improve
- Community engagement beats any marketing budget when done authentically

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not sign NDAs that prevent you from sharing your own work — ideas without execution have no value, and sharing openly attracts collaborators, not thieves. If someone insists on an NDA, suggest a 6-month mutual non-disclosure instead of a permanent restriction.
- Be careful with equity promises to co-founders and early hires: use standard four-year vesting schedules with a one-year cliff to avoid disputes if the business does not work out as planned.
- When outsourcing or hiring, verify credentials and references — giving away equity or critical functions to unvetted people carries real risk
- Customer data and personal pain points used in your marketing should be handled ethically and with consent

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Raising money or waiting for funding before starting — this kills imagination and encourages spending before you have a model
  **Solution:** Start with no money, validate through execution, and raise only after you have proof of demand
- **Problem:** Trying to do everything yourself as a generalist — this drains energy and slows growth
  **Solution:** Identify your one superpower, go all in on it, and outsource everything else to specialists
- **Problem:** Selling time instead of outcomes — this caps your income and keeps you trading hours for money
  **Solution:** Restructure your offers around results, use leverage (people, capital, technology, influence), and price for value delivered
- **Problem:** Holding 100% of a struggling business instead of giving equity to the team
  **Solution:** Give meaningful equity to co-founders and key hires — a smaller piece of a successful business is worth more than all of a failing one
- **Problem:** Staying part-time indefinitely and never transitioning to full-time commitment
  **Solution:** Set a specific date and financial trigger (e.g., one paying client or six months runway) to go all in
- **Problem:** Chasing new customers while neglecting existing ones
  **Solution:** Prioritize retention and delight — happy customers become your best marketers and require no extra acquisition cost
