# How To Set Up A Business In 47 Minutes

## Overview

A step-by-step guide to starting and building a successful business based on 35 years of entrepreneurial experience. This teaching covers location selection, idea formulation, cost management, monetization strategies, raising money, selling a business, and legal essentials—all synthesized into actionable steps for founders at any stage.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you need to decide where to locate your business for maximum opportunity and tax advantage
- When formulating or validating a business idea and turning it into a sustainable business model
- When you need to reduce costs, generate early revenue, and build a committed team without relying on outside capital
- When raising money, negotiating partnerships, or preparing to sell your company
- When setting up the legal and accounting foundation to protect your business

## Steps

### Step 1: Choose the Right Location

Select a location where the money, investors, and opportunity already exist. San Francisco is ideal for raising venture capital. Hong Kong offers a 15% flat corporate tax rate with no double taxation on director dividends. Shenzhen is the global hub for manufacturing and holds 90% of the world's patents. Dubai provides 0% tax and a nomad visa for lifestyle businesses. Thailand offers low-cost manufacturing with a better quality of life than mainland China. Do not stay in your birth country out of habit—move to where your business can thrive.

### Step 2: Formulate a Purpose-Driven Business Idea

Ask yourself: "What problem does my business idea solve?" Avoid pitching a market gap without purpose—purpose is what keeps you going when things get hard. Build a community around your business, not just a product. Ask "What would I do for free?" to identify genuine passion. Create a Blue Ocean strategy by building a new market instead of competing in a red ocean of existing competitors. Purpose, community, and a real problem to solve are the foundation of a billion-dollar idea.

### Step 3: Build a Purpose-Driven Business Model

Structure your business around community and purpose. Give your company a mission that matters to you and others will care about it too. Build accountability and responsibility into your team from day one. Example: A property agency that donates 100% of commission profit to homeless charities creates supply (listings) by aligning agent incentives with charitable purpose, winning clients who want to support that mission.

### Step 4: Get Costs Down Aggressively

Sell unnecessary assets. Replace car loans with cheaper used cars or no car at all. Eliminate mortgages if possible. Do not spend money on things that do not reduce long-term costs. Use barter and live in your office if needed. When hiring, offer equity instead of salary to test whether people truly care about your purpose—those who invest their own money will work harder than those you simply pay. A salary is not real value; equity is the only scalable form of compensation.

### Step 5: Monetize Through Service Businesses Before Your Main Idea Makes Money

You do not need your core idea to generate revenue on day one. Run service businesses—cleaning cars, windows, pools, copywriting—to fund your main venture. Airbnb initially sold cereal boxes at political conventions to fund platform development. Offer services for free to build trust and long-term client relationships when appropriate. Service income provides runway and can connect you with future investors (e.g., cleaning a potential investor's pool).

### Step 6: Get Your Team to Invest in the Business

Require early team members to invest equity in the company, not just receive a salary. This filters for people who genuinely care about your purpose. Equity aligns incentives, gives you runway without external capital, and builds an army of committed co-owners rather than paid employees. If someone will not invest in your business, they do not believe in it enough to be on your team.

### Step 7: Partner With Competitors, Not Fight Them

Seek partnerships with competitors using the 1+1=11 philosophy. Nearly every successful business partners with a competitor at some point. When Fluid had a client it could not handle, it passed the client to a competitor who then became a long-term referral partner. Elon Musk closed his direct competitor to PayPal and joined the PayPal team instead of fighting them. Identify companies you could merge with, buy, or collaborate with and put them on your mailing list and marketing radar early.

### Step 8: Let Clients Fund Your Expansion

Treat high-value clients as partners. If a client wants you in their market, let them fund your office expansion in exchange for sector-specific exclusivity (e.g., 6 months exclusive). This removes the need for external capital and creates aligned incentives. Be cautious with single-client dependency in regulated industries like banking, but use structured exclusivity agreements to make it work.

### Step 9: Learn to Say No

Set clear payment terms: no more than 15-day payment cycles and a 50% deposit before work begins. Dictate terms, do not let new clients dictate them. Say no to wrong staff, wrong partners, and wrong clients—especially wrong investors. One bad investor can block future funding rounds. Build slow with a cash-flow-positive business rather than growing fast and collapsing under payment problems.

### Step 10: Raise Money by Asking for Advice, Not Money

When approaching potential investors, ask for advice, not capital. If you ask for money, people will give you advice and deflect. If you ask for advice, they will feel valued and eventually offer money. Do due diligence on investors before accepting their capital. Make them feel like they are bringing a competitive advantage to your business, not just writing a check. Investors invest in people who can learn and adapt, not stubborn founders.

### Step 11: Target Potential Buyers Early

Write down the companies you want to merge with, acquire, or sell to years in advance. Add them to your marketing list. Run targeted campaigns toward them—e.g., a billboard outside a target company's office. Ensure every potential buyer knows who you are long before you consider selling. Consider a management buyout as an exit strategy: bring in high-profile team members who could eventually raise the capital to buy the business. Also consider the reverse: buying a bigger company that could have acquired you.

### Step 12: Set Up Legal and Accounting Foundations

Register your company properly. Get a trademark for your company name in every market where you accept money—this costs approximately £300 in the UK and is critical to preventing others from passing off your business. Keep proper accounts: spend 10 minutes daily collecting receipts and maintaining records. Bad accounting and wrong shareholders will block future investment and acquisition. Use NDAs when sharing sensitive ideas, but understand that execution speed is a stronger defense than legal paperwork—if someone wants to steal your idea, they will; beat them by building faster.

## Examples

### Example 1: Property Agency With Purpose

Start a property agency where 100% of commission profit funds homeless charities. Agents compete on supply (listings) because clients will choose the agent whose profit goes to charity. The agency wins supply, wins clients, earns commission, and funds its mission. This model creates a Blue Ocean by solving a real social problem rather than competing on brand.

### Example 2: Airbnb Funding Mechanism

Airbnb founders gave free accommodation to developers building the platform and sold custom cereal boxes at political conventions to generate revenue. They used that cash to fund the platform before raising venture capital. The lesson: your core idea does not need to make money on day one—use adjacent revenue streams to fund your real vision.

### Example 3: Fluid Agency Partnership

When Fluid had a client conflict it could not handle, it passed the client to a competitor (Ogy) instead of fighting. Ogy became a long-term referral partner, generating more revenue in one recommendation than Fluid could have earned in a decade of self-promotion. The halo effect of a respected competitor's referral is worth more than any marketing spend.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Start anywhere, but move to where the opportunity lives—location determines your odds of success
- ✅ Solve a real problem you care about, not just a market gap
- ✅ Build community and purpose into your business model from day one
- ✅ Get costs down before you get revenue up—no unnecessary expenses, live cheaply
- ✅ Use service businesses to fund your main venture before it is profitable
- ✅ Require team members to invest equity, not just receive salary
- ✅ Partner with competitors using the 1+1=11 philosophy
- ✅ Target potential buyers and investors years before you need them
- ✅ Ask for advice, not money, when fundraising
- ✅ Say no to wrong clients, staff, and investors—set strict payment terms
- ✅ Trademark your company name in every market where you accept money
- ✅ Spend 10 minutes daily on accounting—collect receipts and maintain proper records

## Keep In Mind

- Your business is a reflection of the place you live and the six people you hang out with—surround yourself with money, opportunity, and ambitious people
- Humans are inherently lazy—you need a purpose strong enough to get you out of bed every day
- A salary is a drug that pays bills; equity is the only real, scalable value
- If you do the hard thing now, your life gets easier over time; if you do the easy thing now, your life gets harder over time
- The system does not want you to get your costs down, but lowering costs is the fastest path to freedom
- You do not need money to move to opportunity markets—accelerators in Shenzhen, friends' couches in Hong Kong, and cheap tickets to San Francisco are all valid starting points
- Paper will always accept ink—if someone wants to rip you off, they will; your best defense is executing faster than they can copy you

## Security & Safety Notes

- Trademark your company name immediately in every market where you accept money to prevent passing off and brand theft
- Use NDAs when sharing sensitive ideas with external parties, but do not over-rely on legal documents—execution speed is your strongest protection
- Get proper shareholder agreements and accounts in place from day one to avoid blocking future investment and acquisition
- Avoid accepting investors with poor ethics or reputations—one bad shareholder can prevent later investors from joining
- Structure exclusivity agreements carefully when letting clients fund expansion to avoid regulatory issues in regulated industries like banking
- Do not approach companies to buy them unless you are genuinely serious—faking interest damages your brand and is morally wrong

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Starting a business in the wrong location with no access to money, talent, or clients
  **Solution:** Move to San Francisco for VC, Hong Kong for tax, Shenzhen for manufacturing, or Dubai for zero-tax lifestyle businesses—location is the single biggest factor in success

- **Problem:** Building a business around a market gap without a real purpose or problem to solve
  **Solution:** Ask "What problem does my business solve?" and ensure the answer drives you personally; purpose prevents burnout and creates competitive advantage

- **Problem:** Hiring people for salary without testing whether they care about the mission
  **Solution:** Require equity investment from early team members—those who put their own money in will work harder and stay longer than those you simply pay

- **Problem:** Accepting every client that comes along, leading to cash flow problems from bad payment terms
  **Solution:** Set strict terms: 50% deposit, maximum 15-day payment cycles, and learn to say no to wrong clients before they damage your business

- **Problem:** Asking investors for money directly, causing them to deflect or give unwanted advice instead of capital
  **Solution:** Ask for advice, make investors feel valued, and let them offer money on their own terms after building trust

- **Problem:** Failing to trademark the company name, leaving the business vulnerable to passing off claims and forced shutdown
  **Solution:** Register your trademark immediately in every market where you accept money—it costs approximately £300 and is non-negotiable

- **Problem:** Neglecting accounting until it becomes a problem that blocks investment or acquisition
  **Solution:** Spend 10 minutes every day collecting receipts and maintaining records; build the habit early so it never becomes a crisis

- **Problem:** Partnering with the wrong investor whose reputation blocks future funding rounds
  **Solution:** Do thorough due diligence on every investor before accepting their capital—one bad shareholder can kill your company's future fundraising ability
