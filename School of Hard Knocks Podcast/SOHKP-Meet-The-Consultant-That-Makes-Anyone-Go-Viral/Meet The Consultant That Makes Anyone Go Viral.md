# Meet The Consultant That Makes Anyone Go Viral

## Overview

This money teaching distills the key principles from Addy Kinsman's content strategy into actionable steps you can apply to build a profitable personal brand or content agency. Kinsman generates over 1.3 billion organic views per month by treating content creation as a science rather than an art. Her core insight is that virality is engineered, not lucky — every video is created with a specific intended comment section in mind, and the smallest intentional details can be the difference between one million and ten million views. Whether you are a creator, entrepreneur, or agency owner, these principles will help you build a content machine that drives consistent attention, trust, and revenue.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When building a personal brand or content-based business on YouTube, TikTok, Instagram, or other short-form video platforms
- When starting a content agency that helps other businesses or creators grow their audience
- When you want to understand why some content goes viral while most content gets ignored
- When trying to overcome the belief that you need to be lucky, charismatic, or naturally talented to succeed as a creator
- When the user asks about content strategy, viral video formulas, creator economy monetization, or building a media company

## Steps

### Step 1: Engineer Virality Before You Film — Start With the Comment Section

Kinsman's team never makes a video without knowing exactly what they want the comment section to look like. Before filming, ask yourself: what do I want people to say? What emotion do I want them to feel? What action do I want them to take? Every element of the video — the hook, the story, the visual details, the call to action — should be designed to produce that specific outcome. Virality is not accidental; it is the result of intentional design.

### Step 2: Understand That Content Quality — Not Luck — Determines Reach

The most pervasive lie in the creator economy is that shadow banning, algorithm changes, or bad timing are why your content does not perform. Kinsman is direct: it is never the content. People who blame external forces are avoiding the harder work of improving their actual creative product. If you want more views, make better content — not louder content, not more frequent content, but content that is more precisely engineered for the response you want. Accepting full responsibility for your results is the first step to actually improving them.

### Step 3: Start With a Proven Method, Not Originality

For new creators who do not know how to get started, Kinsman recommends starting with a proven method — a repeatable formula that has already worked for others in your niche. Do not try to reinvent the wheel before you understand how the wheel works. Once you have mastered a method and can reliably produce results, you can experiment with originality. But in the beginning, mimicry is not laziness; it is the fastest way to build competence and prove that you can show up consistently.

### Step 4: Pay Attention to the Small Details That Create Massive Differences

Kinsman emphasizes that sometimes the smallest things in a video — a specific visual cue, a phrase, a pacing choice — can be the difference between one million and ten million views. Most creators focus on big-picture elements like topic and lighting, but the micro-details that guide viewer behavior are what separate good content from great content. Study the content in your niche obsessively, catalog what works, and intentionally replicate those patterns in your own videos.

### Step 5: Protect Your Self-Belief Like It Is Your Most Valuable Asset

Before she was a viral content creator, Kinsman went bankrupt after signing a bad record deal following her time on The Voice. The turning point was deciding to become her own biggest cheerleader, even on days when she did not feel like it. She calls this "delusional enough" confidence — the willingness to tell yourself you are going to make it when the evidence says otherwise. Your mindset is malleable, and the people and information you surround yourself with will shape it. If you consume content that tells you the game is rigged, you will believe it and stop trying.

### Step 6: Control Your Inputs Ruthlessly

Kinsman is extremely selective about what she listens to, who she spends time with, and what conversations she participates in. She knows her mindset can be influenced easily, so she curates her environment to ensure only edifying, growth-oriented information enters her consciousness. The same principle applies to your content business: if you consume content that makes you feel small, fearful, or defeated, you will produce content that reflects that energy. Audit your inputs with the same rigor you apply to your outputs.

## Examples

### Example 1: From Bankrupt Musician to 1.3 Billion Monthly Views

Kinsman's journey began with a failed music career, a bad record deal, and bankruptcy. Rather than accepting that she had been defeated, she rebuilt her identity around content creation — a space where she felt she had a natural gift for entertaining and connecting with people. She protected her self-belief through the bleakest days, eventually building an audience of hundreds of millions and a content empire that generates significant revenue. The lesson: your lowest point is not your permanent address if you are willing to rebuild your mindset.

### Example 2: Engineering the Comment Section, Not Just the Video

Kinsman's team designs videos around specific desired outcomes in the comment section. If they want controversy, they seed a polarizing idea. If they want community, they ask a question that invites shared experience. If they want shares, they create a moment people want to tag their friends in. The video is just the vehicle — the comment section is the product. Most creators think about what they want to say; Kinsman's team thinks about what they want people to say back.

### Example 3: The Lie That Keeps People Small

Kinsman identifies the "shadow ban" myth as one of the most destructive lies in the creator economy. When creators blame the algorithm for poor performance, they absolve themselves of the responsibility to improve. This creates a victim identity that prevents growth. The reality is that the algorithm is designed to reward content that keeps users on the platform — if your content is not performing, it is because it is not compelling enough, not because the system is rigged against you.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Blaming the algorithm, shadow banning, or bad timing for poor content performance
  **Solution:** Run a brutal audit of your last 10 videos. What patterns do the high performers share? What are the low performers missing? Fix the inputs, not the excuses.

- **Problem:** Trying to be original before mastering the basics of the platform
  **Solution:** Reverse-engineer 50 videos in your niche. Identify the common structures, hooks, and pacing patterns. Replicate them until you can execute them flawlessly, then experiment.

- **Problem:** Losing self-belief after a streak of underperforming content
  **Solution:** Build a "proof file" — a document with every positive comment, DM, and metric that shows you are making progress. Read it when you feel like quitting.

- **Problem:** Consuming toxic or fear-based content that erodes your confidence
  **Solution:** Unfollow or mute anyone who makes you feel like the game is rigged. Follow only people who are winning at the level you want to reach.

- **Problem:** Focusing on vanity metrics (likes, followers) instead of business outcomes (revenue, leads, retention)
  **Solution:** Define your real business metric before you post. If the video does not move that number, it does not matter how many views it gets.
