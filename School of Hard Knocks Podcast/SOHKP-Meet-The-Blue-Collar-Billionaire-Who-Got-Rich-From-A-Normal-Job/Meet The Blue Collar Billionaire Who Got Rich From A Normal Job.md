# Meet The Blue Collar Billionaire Who Got Rich From A Normal Job

## Overview

This money teaching distills the key financial and business principles from Tommy Melo's journey into actionable steps you can apply to build wealth through a home service business. Melo built A1 Garage Door into a company generating over $300 million in annual revenue by specializing in a single trade, hiring for character over credentials, giving equity to every employee, and treating his business like a sport where discipline and preparation matter more than raw talent. His story demonstrates that you do not need a Silicon Valley idea or venture capital to build generational wealth — you need to dominate a niche, build systems, and take care of the people who show up every day.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When considering starting or scaling a home service business (garage doors, HVAC, plumbing, electrical, etc.)
- When you want to build wealth without venture capital or tech industry connections
- When deciding whether to specialize in one trade or diversify across multiple service lines
- When building a team culture based on integrity, competitive spirit, and personal accountability
- When evaluating how to structure employee compensation and equity incentives
- When the user asks about blue-collar entrepreneurship, home service scaling, garage door business, or building a nationwide franchise from a single trade

## Steps

### Step 1: Choose One Niche and Dominate It Completely

Melo's first major decision was to stop being a generalist and become the specialist in garage doors. He rejected the temptation to expand into other home services and instead focused entirely on becoming the best garage door company in the world. The logic is simple: if you can dominate one narrow category, you can do it in anything. People have built nine-figure businesses selling candy, mowing lawns, or installing HVAC — the common thread is obsessive specialization. Resist the urge to diversify until you have mastered your core trade and built systems that allow you to scale it.

### Step 2: Hire for Character, Not Credentials

Melo's hiring framework has four simple tests: (1) would I work for them, (2) would I buy from them, (3) would my grandma buy from them, and (4) would I go have a beer with them and laugh the whole time? He looks for people who smile, tell great stories, show up for practice (not just the game), and recruit on their days off. He does not hire salespeople — he hires good human beings who happen to sell. Technical skills can be taught; character, competitive spirit, and work ethic cannot. When you find people who love the practice as much as the result, you have found your core team.

### Step 3: Fire Top Performers Who Are Toxic to the Culture

One of Melo's hardest lessons was firing a top-producing employee who was disrespectful to peers and managers. Even though the person was generating significant revenue, the damage to team morale and culture was unacceptable. He draws a parallel to football: a talented receiver who does not run the right route will not get the ball, no matter how good he is. No individual contributor is more important than the health of the team. Protect your culture aggressively — if you let a high performer get away with bad behavior, you are telling the rest of the team that results matter more than how you achieve them.

### Step 4: Give Equity to Everyone, Not Just Executives

Melo believes every employee should think and act like an owner. His company gives equity incentives all the way down to technicians and installers — not just managers and executives. When people have ownership stakes, they pick up trash in the parking lot, they recruit neighbors, they ask for Google and Yelp reviews, and they treat clients like the company is their own. The result was explosive growth: the company went from $12 million to $27.5 million in EBITDA in 19 months after implementing an equity incentive program. Capital gains tax (around 23%) is far better than ordinary income tax, so aligning everyone's financial interests creates wealth for the whole team, not just the founders.

### Step 5: Read the Same Books Over and Over, Not Many Books Once

Melo's reading philosophy is counterintuitive: you do not need to read a lot of books. You need to read the same books many times, and in different chapters of your life they will mean different things. He gives every employee a copy of "Go for No" and returns repeatedly to classics like Dale Carnegie's "How to Win Friends and Influence People," Napoleon Hill's "Think and Grow Rich," and "The Ultimate Sales Machine." Take one or two ideas from each book, apply them, then read it again. Mastery comes from depth, not breadth.

### Step 6: Work on the Business, Not Just in the Business

Melo emphasizes that there are 168 hours in a week — sleep 50, work 60, work out 5, and you still have 55 hours left. The problem is most entrepreneurs spend all their time on minimum-wage tasks: answering emails, fixing small problems, putting out fires. The solution is time-blocking, journaling for two weeks to identify where you are wasting time, and hiring an executive assistant to handle everything that does not require your unique value. Your job as the owner is to extract the best out of people, run high-level meetings, do acquisitions, and think strategically — not to answer support tickets.

### Step 7: Build Your "Dream Manager" System

Melo's company has a full-time "dream manager" whose job is to figure out what each employee wants out of life and help them achieve it faster. The philosophy is simple: people do not work for companies, they work for leaders who care about them as human beings. When you know your employees' wives' names, their kids' names, and their personal goals, you can motivate them in ways that no paycheck ever could. Melo sends personalized video messages to employees who are struggling, telling them he sees their potential and their family is counting on them. This is the unscalable work that pays the highest ROI.

### Step 8: Bring in an Integrator to Execute Your Vision

Melo credits his COO and project manager with keeping him grounded and turning his big ideas into actionable plans. The Walt Disney model applies: Disney was the visionary dreamer, but his brother Roy was the integrator who made it all happen. Most entrepreneurs have ADHD — their focus changes constantly — so they need someone who will pull them back to reality and ask, "Is that in your top 10?" Find an integrator who can take your vision, break it into small actions, and keep you relentlessly prioritized on the objectives that matter most.

## Examples

### Example 1: From Predental Student to $300 Million Garage Door Mogul

Melo was on track to be a dentist until an internship with a practicing dentist revealed that the business owner was still heavily in debt at 47 and would require eight more years of school to reach his position. Melo realized the better path was to learn business itself — not a trade — and he pivoted into entrepreneurship. He started doing garage door jobs himself, then hired people, then built systems. Within two decades, A1 Garage Door was generating over $300 million in revenue with more than 700 employees. The decision to skip professional school and learn business directly was the highest-return choice he ever made.

### Example 2: The $24 Million Deal That Made Him Realize Money Is Not the Point

Melo's first major deal generated $24 million, and he expected it to change his life. It did not — he was still the same person from Sterling Heights, Michigan, and the money did not make him happier. What did change was watching his team's lives transform: employees who could finally buy houses, send their kids to college, and retire with dignity. This shifted his motivation from personal accumulation to collective success. The money became a scoreboard for how much value he had created, not a source of personal identity.

### Example 3: Firing a Top Sales Producer for Cultural Toxicity

Melo had to fire a top-performing salesperson who was disrespectful to managers, rolled his eyes in meetings, and undermined the team. Even though the person was generating significant revenue, Melo knew that allowing the behavior to continue would destroy the culture and cause better people to leave. He drew a hard line: no one is above the standards of the team. The decision earned him respect from the rest of the organization and reinforced that character matters more than short-term results.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Choose one niche and dominate it before expanding into adjacent markets
- ✅ Hire for character, competitive spirit, and energy — not just skills and credentials
- ✅ Fire top performers who are toxic to the culture, no matter how much revenue they generate
- ✅ Give equity to every employee, from technicians to executives, so everyone thinks like an owner
- ✅ Read the same foundational books repeatedly and apply one or two lessons at a time
- ✅ Work on the business, not in it — time-block, delegate, and eliminate minimum-wage tasks from your calendar
- ✅ Build a "dream manager" system to help employees achieve their personal goals
- ✅ Bring in an integrator (COO, project manager) to execute your vision and keep you focused
- ❌ Do not start as a generalist — specialize in one trade and become the best in the world at it
- ❌ Do not hire people who are exactly like you — hire complementary personalities who love what you hate
- ❌ Do not keep toxic high performers — culture decay is contagious and expensive
- ❌ Do not skip the personal connection with your team — people leave managers, not companies

## Keep In Mind

- There are no participation trophies in business — the market rewards winners, not effort
- You can build a nine-figure business in home services, but you cannot build it while treating it like a lifestyle business from day one
- The hustler has to die for the leader to be born — you must transition from doing the work to building the system that does the work
- Wealth without purpose is hollow — define what you are chasing beyond the money
- Your team is watching everything you do — model the discipline and character you expect from them

## Security & Safety Notes

- Maintain proper insurance coverage for all field operations — general liability, workers' compensation, and vehicle coverage are non-negotiable
- Document all equity incentive plans with a qualified attorney to avoid disputes
- Separate personal and business finances from day one to protect your assets
- Conduct thorough background checks on all hires, especially those who will enter clients' homes

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Trying to be a generalist and offer every home service under one roof
  **Solution:** Dominate one niche first. Once you have systems, capital, and brand recognition in one trade, expansion becomes optional — not necessary.

- **Problem:** Keeping a toxic top producer because they bring in revenue
  **Solution:** Calculate the hidden cost: how many good employees leave because of their behavior? How many clients are lost because of bad attitudes? Fire them publicly and quickly.

- **Problem:** Getting stuck doing technician work instead of owner work
  **Solution:** Journal every hour for two weeks. Any task a minimum-wage worker could do should be delegated within 90 days.

- **Problem:** Hiring people with the right resume but the wrong character
  **Solution:** Use Melo's four-question test: would I work for them, buy from them, would my grandma buy from them, and would I have a beer with them?

- **Problem:** Giving equity only to executives and expecting technicians to act like owners
  **Solution:** If you want everyone to think like an owner, give everyone a piece of the upside. Align incentives from the bottom up.
