# He Left India With $0… Now He's Building a $200M Company ｜ RJ Jain

## Overview

This teaching distills the financial and business principles from RJ Jain, who immigrated from India to the United States at 18 with no family entrepreneurial background, no network, and no safety net — and eventually sold a company to Google for nine figures while building Price.com to a $200 million valuation. The story demonstrates that being the first person in your family or community to pursue entrepreneurship is not a disadvantage — it can be a catalyst for independent thinking, risk tolerance, and creative problem-solving that inherited privilege often suppresses. Early exposure to problem-solving and building things, even small things, compounds into a powerful entrepreneurial identity over time.

## Steps

### Step 1: Leverage the Advantage of Having Nothing to Lose

Recognize that starting from zero is not a disadvantage — it is a source of risk tolerance that people with safety nets do not have. RJ moved to the U.S. from India at 18 with no family business background, no entrepreneurial network, and the weight of a traditional family expecting a stable career. That pressure created urgency. When you have no safety net, failure is not an option — but it also means you will take the risks that safer players will not, which is where the outsized returns live.

### Step 2: Build Technical Skills That Are Universally Valuable

Invest in hard skills that are in demand regardless of geography, industry, or economic cycle. RJ studied computer science at the University of Michigan — a field that provides portable, compounding career value. Technical skills are especially powerful for immigrants because they do not require social capital, local networks, or cultural fluency to deploy. Code is the same everywhere, and companies will pay for it regardless of your accent or background.

### Step 3: Start Building and Problem-Solving as Early as Possible

Begin creating things and solving problems before you feel qualified to do so. RJ was building flash games at age 12 — long before he had any formal technical training or business context. The habit of building things early creates an entrepreneurial identity that persists into adulthood. The person who spent their childhood creating small things does not hesitate to create big things later.

### Step 4: Make the Conscious Choice to Break From Tradition

Accept that being the first entrepreneur in a traditional family means making choices your community will not understand. RJ's parents expected a stable job — he chose a startup path that felt extremely risky by their standards. Entrepreneurship is inherently a rebellion against the conventional path. If your family and community have never done it, you will have to make the decision alone. That isolation is the price of the potential upside.

### Step 5: Use Early Wins to Fund the Next, Bigger Opportunity

Treat every successful exit or milestone as fuel for the next, more ambitious project. RJ sold his first company to Google for nine figures, then immediately used that capital, experience, and network to build Price.com to a $200 million valuation. Each exit is not an endpoint — it is a platform for the next, larger opportunity. The entrepreneurs who compound fastest are those who redeploy their wins immediately rather than pausing to enjoy them.

## Examples

**Example 1: From India to Michigan at 18**
RJ moved from Kanpur, India to Michigan at 18 to study computer science at the University of Michigan. He was the first person in his family to start a company. His parents expected a stable career, not entrepreneurship. That tension between tradition and ambition created the urgency that drove him.

**Example 2: Building Flash Games at Age 12**
RJ was building flash games for his sister when he was 12 years old — a decade before he had any formal business or technical credentials. Early exposure to building and problem-solving created the foundation for his later technical and entrepreneurial career.

**Example 3: Selling to Google and Building Price.com**
RJ sold his previous company to Google for nine figures. He then built Price.com, which hit a $200 million valuation. Each win funded the next, and each exit provided the capital and credibility to pursue a more ambitious target.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Use the "nothing to lose" mindset as a source of risk tolerance and urgency
- ✅ Build hard technical skills that are portable and universally valued
- ✅ Start creating and problem-solving as early as possible — the habit compounds over decades
- ✅ Accept that being the first entrepreneur in your family means making decisions in isolation
- ✅ Redeploy every win into the next opportunity rather than pausing to celebrate
- ❌ Do not let your family's risk aversion become your own
- ❌ Do not wait until you feel "ready" or "qualified" to start building
- ❌ Do not treat an exit as an endpoint — it is a platform for the next opportunity

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Letting family expectations dictate your career path
  **Solution:** Make the choice yourself — entrepreneurship is a decision that requires personal conviction, not community consensus

- **Problem:** Waiting until you feel "qualified" to start building
  **Solution:** The qualifications you think you need are often not the ones the market cares about — start building now and acquire credentials through results

- **Problem:** Treating an acquisition as a finish line
  **Solution:** Use the capital, network, and credibility from every exit to immediately pursue a bigger opportunity — momentum is easier to maintain than to restart

- **Problem:** Underestimating the advantage of being underestimated
  **Solution:** Being from an unconventional background means people will not have expectations of you — use that freedom to pursue paths others would not consider
