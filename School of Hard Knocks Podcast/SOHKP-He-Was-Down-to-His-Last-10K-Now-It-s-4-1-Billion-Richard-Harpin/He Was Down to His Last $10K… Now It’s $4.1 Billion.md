# He Was Down to His Last $10K… Now It’s $4.1 Billion | Richard Harpin

## Overview

This money teaching captures Richard Harpin's nine-step framework for building a billion-pound business, from his early rabbit-breeding and magic businesses to building HomeServe into a company sold for £4.1 billion. The core principles are to copy and improve proven models, bootstrap before raising capital, and build a team that can operate without you. Use these teachings when starting a service business, planning an exit, or scaling from one country to many.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When starting a service-based or recurring-revenue business
- When deciding whether to bootstrap or raise venture capital
- When building a leadership team that can run without you
- When expanding into international markets
- When planning an exit or acquisition strategy
- When the user asks about building billion-dollar companies, bootstrapping, or global expansion

## Steps

### Step 1: Copy and Pivot Instead of Reinventing

Find a business model that already works and execute it slightly better. Do not let originality paralysis stop you; if someone else is making money in a model, it is validated. Study their process, copy it, and add your own improvements. Copying is the first step to building something massive.

### Step 2: Bootstrap Until Your Model Is Proven

Do not raise capital until you have proven your business model works. Taking money too early forces you to give away too much equity. Bootstrap first, validate with real customers, and only raise when you know exactly how to deploy capital for growth.

### Step 3: Get Coaches and Mentors With Gray Hair

Find people who have already made the mistakes you are about to make. A paid business coach, a free mentor, or an experienced advisor can compress years of trial and error into a few conversations. The cost of ignorance is always higher than the cost of coaching.

### Step 4: Combine Bricks, Clicks, and Paper

Do not rely solely on digital marketing. Direct mail still works because fewer people use it, so your message stands out. Combine online marketing with physical presence—pop-up stands, retail partnerships, or local events. A multi-channel approach reduces risk and increases brand durability.

### Step 5: Hire Your Replacement and Work On the Business

Entrepreneurs have too many ideas and not enough focus. Hire a proven chief executive to run the company day to day, then step up to work on the strategy, vision, and growth instead of being trapped in operational details. Your job is to build the business, not to be the business.

### Step 6: Go Global With Local Leaders

Expand internationally once your home model is proven, but hire locals to run each country. Americans buy from Americans, British buy from British. Do not export your management style; export your standards and let local leaders adapt execution to their culture.

### Step 7: Evolve, Do Not Revolve

Incremental evolution beats disruptive revolution. Blockbuster died because it refused to evolve; BlackBerry died for the same reason. Constantly improve your model, test new channels, and adapt to customer behavior before the market forces you to change.

### Step 8: Maintain a Not-To-Do List

A to-do list is not enough; entrepreneurs need a not-to-do list to stay focused. Ideas are abundant, but execution is rare. Say no to good opportunities so you can say yes to the great ones. Focus on one thing and execute it brilliantly rather than chasing every shiny object.

### Step 9: Hone Your Character and Hire for Weaknesses

Know what you are good at, what you are bad at, and hire people to cover your gaps. Character—integrity, resilience, work ethic—is the foundation of long-term success. Perform honest self-assessments and surround yourself with people who complement your skills.

## Examples

### Example 1: From £50,000 Loss to £4.1 Billion Exit

A founder put £50,000 into an emergency plumbing business that failed because the Yellow Pages cost was too high and there was no repeat business. They pivoted to a plumbing insurance model, copied a competitor's affinity branding approach, and improved it. A last-ditch direct mail shot to 1,000 customers produced 38 sales and saved the company. That pivot became HomeServe, which eventually sold for £4.1 billion.

### Example 2: The Rabbit That Became a Business Empire

A 12-year-old received a rabbit as a gift and bred it, then offered rabbit-sitting services to friends. That led to performing as a children's magician, tying fishing flies, and eventually setting up a mail-order fly-tying business. Each small venture taught market research, customer development, and the art of pivoting based on feedback.

### Example 3: Vendor Finance vs. Equity

When a business was days from collapse, a friend offered either a 20% interest rate loan or 10% equity. The founder chose the high-interest loan, paid it back a year later, and kept 100% of the company. Years later, the company sold for £4.1 billion. That 10% equity would have been worth £400 million. The lesson: equity is far more valuable than interest when the business succeeds.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Copy proven models and improve them by 10 percent
- ✅ Bootstrap until your model is proven; raise capital only to scale
- ✅ Hire mentors and coaches who have already built what you want
- ✅ Combine direct mail, digital, and physical presence
- ✅ Hire a CEO to run day-to-day operations so you can focus on strategy
- ✅ Expand globally with local leadership
- ✅ Maintain a not-to-do list to protect your focus
- ❌ Do not raise venture capital before you know exactly how to spend it
- ❌ Do not try to do everything yourself; delegate operations
- ❌ Do not ignore direct mail just because it is old-fashioned

## Keep In Mind

- It is easier to buy revenue than to create it; consider acquiring profitable businesses instead of starting from scratch
- 97% of UK businesses fail within five years; buying an existing business dramatically reduces risk
- Your not-to-do list is more important than your to-do list
- Character flaws will surface under pressure; fix them before they become crises

## Security & Safety Notes

- Vendor finance and seller financing can be powerful tools, but structure them legally
- When expanding globally, keep your business model within 10% of the original to avoid complexity
- Do not over-diversify before your core model is stable
- Consult a corporate lawyer before negotiating exits or large financing deals

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Raising money before proving the model and giving away too much equity
  **Solution:** Bootstrap until you have repeatable, profitable unit economics

- **Problem:** Getting distracted by every new idea instead of executing one thing well
  **Solution:** Write a not-to-do list and protect your focus ruthlessly

- **Problem:** Expanding globally with the same management team instead of hiring locals
  **Solution:** Hire country managers who understand the culture, buying habits, and regulations

- **Problem:** Trying to reinvent a working model instead of copying and improving it
  **Solution:** Find what works, replicate it, and make it 10 percent better
