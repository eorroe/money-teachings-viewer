# They're Lying To You About Market Crashes... Here's How To See One Coming ｜ John Devaney

## Overview

This teaching distills the core market and risk principles shared by John Devaney, a bond trader who made and lost nearly $200 million in the 2008 financial crisis, into actionable steps. Devaney's central insight is that market crashes are not random — they are caused by excessive leverage, mispriced risk, and the illusion of safety in assets that have never been tested. The people who survive crashes are not the ones who predict them perfectly — they are the ones who maintain optionality, diversify across uncorrelated assets, and keep enough dry powder to deploy when others are liquidating at fire-sale prices. Opportunity and crisis are the same thing viewed from opposite sides.

## Steps

### Step 1: Understand That Leverage Is the Primary Crash Driver

Every major market crash in modern history has been caused by excessive leverage — borrowed money magnifying the downside of a mispriced asset. In 2008, banks held AAA-rated subprime mortgage bonds that were leveraged at 1% down. When the underlying mortgages defaulted, the bonds went from 95 cents to 40 cents, wiping out trillions in equity. Before any market can crash, ask: what is the leverage embedded in the system? If it is high, the downside is not linear — it is exponential.

### Step 2: Identify Illiquidity as a Source of Opportunity

When markets are liquid, everyone is confident and prices are efficient. When markets become illiquid — when buyers disappear and sellers cannot find a bid — that is where the opportunity lives. Devaney made his early fortune by finding bonds that no one else wanted to touch and becoming the only buyer. If you can identify an area of the market where liquidity has dried up but the underlying collateral is sound, you have found a potential multi-bagger.

### Step 3: Never Act as an Agent — Only Invest Your Own Money

Devaney's core trading philosophy was simple: "I won't act as agent because agents will say anything to make a commission. But if I buy a bond with my own money, I know it is good because I have skin in the game." When you invest your own capital, you evaluate deals differently than when you are managing other people's money. You ask harder questions. You walk away from deals that do not feel right. This discipline is what keeps you alive when markets turn.

### Step 4: Keep Dry Powder for the Dislocation

Devaney's biggest asset during the 2008 crisis was not his trading skill — it was the fact that he had sold his art collection for $55 million in late 2007, six months before the crash. That liquidity allowed him to buy distressed assets at prices no one else could touch. If you are fully invested at the top of the market, you cannot take advantage of the bottom. Always maintain optionality. Keep enough cash or available credit to deploy when fear is at its peak.

### Step 5: Reinvest Gains Into Your Core Competency

Devaney's trajectory was steep because he reinvested every dollar of profit back into the bond market he understood. Year one: $5 million. Year two: $25 million. Year three: $45 million. Year five: $110 million net income on $130 million revenue. He did not diversify into areas he did not understand. He went deeper into the one area where he had an information edge. Compounding works fastest when you stay in your circle of competence.

### Step 6: Build Relationships Before You Need Them

Devaney's broker dealer, United Capital Markets, grew not because of advertising but because of relationships built at industry conferences. He sponsored opening-night cocktail receptions, hired live bands, and made himself the center of the community. Clients came to him because they knew him, trusted him, and enjoyed being around him. In markets driven by trust and reputation, your network is your most valuable asset. Build it before you need to raise capital or raise your profile.

### Step 7: Accept That Some Crashes Are Unavoidable

Devaney did not see the 2008 crash coming in time to exit completely. He described it as a "one in a thousand-year storm" that wiped out the equity in the entire banking system. Even the smartest, best-connected operators were caught. The lesson is not that you should try to time every crash — you cannot. The lesson is that you should build your portfolio and your business so that a single catastrophic event does not destroy you. Diversify. Keep reserves. Do not over-leverage.

## Examples

### Example 1: The College Options Trade

Devaney was a hungover college student when he bought out-of-the-money Motorola call options with $3,000 he had just won in a scholarship. The options expired worthless the first time. He bought again. And again. Eight consecutive winning trades turned $3,000 into $14,000, then $20,000, then $50,000, and eventually $100,000. The lesson: early wins compound confidence, and confidence is the fuel for bigger risk-taking. But confidence must be paired with skill — Devaney studied options before he traded them.

### Example 2: The $200 Million Loss

In 2008, Devaney lost close to $200 million. His broker dealer, United Capital Markets, was heavily positioned in structured finance bonds. When the subprime mortgage market collapsed, the AAA-rated bonds he held went from 95 cents to 40 cents, then the subordinate tranches went to five cents. He was forced to liquidate his personal art collection and sell real estate to cover the losses. His net worth dropped by more than half. The recovery took years. The lesson: no one is immune to systemic risk, and leverage is the fastest way to turn a mistake into a catastrophe.

### Example 3: Building United Capital Markets

Devaney started United Capital Markets in 1999 at age 29. He became one of the youngest people ever approved by FINRA to own a broker dealer. His edge was specialization: while every major firm was trading investment-grade bonds, Devaney focused on the high-risk, high-reward subordinate tranches of asset-backed securities. He became the go-to market maker for bonds no one else wanted to touch. By year five, the firm was generating $130 million in revenue and $110 million in profit on a 20-person team. The lesson: niche specialization creates margins that competition cannot touch.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Identify leverage in any market before you invest — it is the primary driver of crashes
- ✅ Buy when markets are illiquid and others are selling — that is where the margin of safety lives
- ✅ Invest only with your own money or with structures that give you full transparency
- ✅ Maintain dry powder so you can deploy when others are forced to liquidate
- ✅ Reinvest gains into your core competency rather than diversifying into unknown areas
- ✅ Build a network through generosity and presence before you need it
- ✅ Accept that some crashes are unavoidable and build your portfolio to survive them
- ❌ Do not act as an agent for others when you could be principal for yourself
- ❌ Do not be fully invested at the top of a cycle — maintain optionality
- ❌ Do not leverage your portfolio to the point where a 20% move wipes you out
- ❌ Do not confuse past performance with future safety — untested innovations in finance are usually mispriced

## Keep In Mind

- "I won't act as agent because agents will say anything to make a commission. But if I buy a bond with my own money, it's good because I have skin in the game." Always evaluate who benefits from your investment decision.
- Market crashes are not the end of the world — they are the beginning of the next cycle. The people who buy when others are selling become the wealthy of the next generation.
- The best trades are not found in the headlines. They are found in the places where liquidity has dried up and no one is looking.
- Your net worth will fluctuate. Your skill and your network will not. Invest in those first.

## Security & Safety Notes

- This is educational content based on a publicly available podcast episode.
- Structured finance products, including asset-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations, carry significant risk and are not suitable for all investors.
- Options trading can result in total loss of capital. Never trade with money you cannot afford to lose.
- The 2008 financial crisis was a systemic event. No individual investor can fully insulate themselves from macro-level dislocations. Maintain adequate reserves.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Chasing performance in a hot market without understanding the underlying leverage.
  **Solution:** Before you buy any asset, ask what leverage is embedded in the system. If the answer is "a lot," the downside risk is not linear.

- **Problem:** Being fully invested when a dislocation hits.
  **Solution:** Keep 10-20% of your portfolio in cash or highly liquid instruments. Dry powder is more valuable than a full portfolio when prices collapse.

- **Problem:** Investing in areas you do not understand because everyone else is making money.
  **Solution:** Stay in your circle of competence. Devaney made his fortune in structured finance because he studied it obsessively. He did not jump into tech stocks or real estate because they were trendy.

- **Problem:** Using other people's money in ways that expose you to losses you cannot absorb.
  **Solution:** Only lever up when the downside is quantifiable and the potential upside justifies the risk. Never bet the firm on a single trade.
