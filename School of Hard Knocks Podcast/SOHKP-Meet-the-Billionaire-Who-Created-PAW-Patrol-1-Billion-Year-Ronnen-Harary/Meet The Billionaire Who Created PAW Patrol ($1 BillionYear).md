# Meet The Billionaire Who Created PAW Patrol ($1 Billion/Year) ｜ Ronnen Harary

## Overview

This money teaching distills the key financial and business principles from Ronnen Harary's journey into actionable steps you can apply to build a consumer products or entertainment brand. Harary co-founded Spin Master and helped create PAW Patrol, Bakugan, and other billion-dollar toy properties by starting with humble products, taking ideas from anywhere in the world, and building the entertainment property before building the product — the opposite of how most toy companies operate. His story demonstrates that you do not need a grand vision or a massive budget to build an empire; you need to ship products one at a time, stay open to unexpected opportunities, and let the screenplay of your life unfold without over-planning.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When building a toy company, consumer products brand, or entertainment property
- When deciding whether to build the product first or the media property first
- When sourcing ideas from international markets and adapting them for domestic audiences
- When scaling a company from humble beginnings to a publicly traded billion-dollar empire
- When the user asks about toy business strategy, PAW Patrol, Spin Master, building IP, or entertainment-driven product launches

## Steps

### Step 1: Start With Humble Products and Build One at a Time

Harary's first product was Earth Buddies — a Chia Pet-like toy made of grass seed and sawdust that sold a million pieces in the first year. His second product was Devil Sticks, a juggling set for kids. He did not start with a billion-dollar vision; he started with one product, learned from it, and moved to the next. The most important principle he identifies is taking things in stages and meeting yourself where you are at that moment in time. Do not try to build an empire before you have proven you can ship one product profitably.

### Step 2: Be Open to Ideas From Anywhere in the World

Spin Master's success came from looking globally for product ideas, not just looking in their own backyard. Earth Buddies came from an Israeli newspaper article about six manufacturers who were already making them. Harary's mother sat him down to translate the article, he did the math, and realized no one was making them in Canada or the United States. The best ideas are often already working somewhere else — your job is to find them, adapt them, and execute better than anyone else in your market.

### Step 3: Build the Show Before the Product

With PAW Patrol, Harary flipped the standard toy industry formula. Most companies build the product first, prove product-market fit, and then license a TV show. Harary did the opposite: he invested in building a high-quality television property first, knowing that a beloved show would drive massive demand for the toys. The result was a franchise where 26% of toy industry revenue comes from TV and movie properties — and PAW Patrol became one of the most successful examples of that model. If you are building a brand with entertainment potential, consider building the audience before you build the product line.

### Step 4: Test With Kids, Not Just Data

In the toy business, there is really no testing — you have to feel whether a product will resonate. Harary's most vivid memory of market testing was watching a kid watch a commercial for a product that ultimately failed. The kid's face was deadpan, with no reaction, and Harary knew immediately it would flop. He passed on Beyblade because spinning tops "aren't cool" — and six months later, Beyblade went on to do billions in sales for Hasbro. The lesson: your intuition about how children (or any audience) will respond to a product is a more reliable signal than focus groups or spreadsheets.

### Step 5: Never Put Numbers on Your Mission — Focus on the Next Product

When Spin Master started, the founders made three mission statements: be open to ideas from anywhere, partner with different people, and build the company as big as possible. They never put revenue or valuation targets on the mission. Instead, they focused obsessively on the next product, then the next one, then the next. The compound effect of shipping one successful product after another is what built a $2 billion-a-year company. Big visions can be motivating, but they can also be paralyzing — ship the next thing and let the vision emerge from execution.

### Step 6: Recognize That You Cannot Predict the Future, So Stay Flexible

Harary never dreamed that Spin Master would become a billion-dollar company. When asked whether he had any idea the toy company would turn into an empire, he said no — and that was the point. You cannot think too far in advance because you do not know what the screenplay of your life is going to be. The only thing you can control is how well you execute in the present. Stay flexible, stay curious, and let the opportunities that emerge from your work guide you forward rather than clinging to a fixed plan.

## Examples

### Example 1: Passing on Beyblade and Learning From the Mistake

Harary looked at Beyblade when Takara Tomy asked Spin Master to sell it in North America. His team's reaction was immediate: "Spinning tops? Ah, spinning tops aren't so cool." They passed. Six months later, Beyblade was a global phenomenon generating billions in toy sales for Hasbro. Harary tells this story not to glorify the miss, but to illustrate that even the most experienced product people cannot predict what kids will love. The market is the only real test, and you have to be willing to ship, observe, and adapt.

### Example 2: From Earth Buddies to PAW Patrol

Spin Master's arc from Earth Buddies to PAW Patrol is a masterclass in compound iteration. Earth Buddies taught them about manufacturing, retail distribution, and what makes a toy physically satisfying. Devil Sticks taught them about active play and repeat purchase behavior. Each product built the operational muscle and industry relationships that made PAW Patrol possible. There was no master plan to build the world's most successful preschool franchise — there was only a series of shipped products that created the capability to execute when the opportunity arose.

### Example 3: Building the Show First, Then the Toys

Most toy companies license a movie or TV show after it has already proven itself with audiences. Spin Master invested in creating the PAW Patrol property before there was a toy line, betting that a high-quality show about rescue dogs would create demand for figures, vehicles, and playsets. The show became a cultural phenomenon, and the toys followed. The franchise now generates billions in annual revenue and has become one of the most recognized children's brands in the world.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Start with one humble product and master shipping it before building the next
- ✅ Look globally for product ideas — the best opportunities are often already working somewhere else
- ✅ Build the entertainment property before the product line if you are creating a children's brand
- ✅ Test your intuition with real kids and real market feedback, not just spreadsheets
- ✅ Make mission statements about principles, not revenue targets
- ✅ Stay flexible and let your business evolve based on what you learn from each product
- ✅ Build operational muscle through iteration, not planning
- ❌ Do not try to build a billion-dollar vision before you have shipped one profitable product
- ❌ Do not rely only on domestic market research — some of the best ideas come from other countries
- ❌ Do not build the product first if you are creating a brand that depends on entertainment or media
- ❌ Do not over-plan — you cannot predict the screenplay of your life, so focus on executing the next scene well

## Keep In Mind

- The compound effect of shipping one successful product after another is more powerful than any single breakthrough
- Your first product will not be your best product — but it will teach you everything you need to know to build the next one
- Intuition about audience reaction is a more reliable signal than data in creative industries
- The best ideas are already working somewhere else — your job is to find them and adapt them
- Building a billion-dollar company takes as long as it takes — there are no shortcuts through the early, unglamorous stages

## Security & Safety Notes

- Consult intellectual property attorneys before licensing or adapting products from international markets
- Protect your brand trademarks and copyrights in every territory where you plan to sell
- Maintain clear contracts with all co-founders, investors, and licensing partners from day one
- Do not assume that a product concept is original just because you have not seen it in your home market

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Trying to skip the humble early products and build a blockbuster brand immediately
  **Solution:** Every empire is built one product at a time. Ship something small, learn from it, then ship something better.

- **Problem:** Passing on international ideas because they seem weird or unproven
  **Solution:** If a product is already working in another country, the concept has been validated. Your job is execution, not invention.

- **Problem:** Building the product first and then scrambling to create media to support it
  **Solution:** If your brand depends on entertainment, invest in the show or content property before you invest in manufacturing.

- **Problem:** Over-planning and waiting for the perfect moment to launch
  **Solution:** You cannot predict the future, so focus on executing well in the present. Let opportunities emerge from execution, not the other way around.

- **Problem:** Assuming your first product will be your breakthrough
  **Solution:** Your first product is a learning vehicle, not your legacy. Build it, ship it, learn from it, and move to the next one.
