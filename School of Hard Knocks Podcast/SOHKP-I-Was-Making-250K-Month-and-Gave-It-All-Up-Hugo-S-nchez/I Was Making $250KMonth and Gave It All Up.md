# I Was Making $250K/Month and Gave It All Up ｜ Hugo Sánchez

## Overview

This money teaching distills Hugo Sánchez's decision to walk away from a highly profitable dry cleaning business making $250,000 per month in order to launch a technology startup. The lessons cover how to recognize when a vision supersedes current income, how systemic barriers in venture capital affect founders from underrepresented backgrounds, why authenticity can redirect your trajectory, and how to persist through investor rejection without losing confidence.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are considering leaving a profitable business to pursue a new vision
- When navigating venture capital fundraising as an underrepresented founder
- When you need motivation to persist through repeated investor rejection
- When identifying authentic opportunities from unexpected observations in daily life
- When the user asks about tech startups, leaving a successful business, or Latino entrepreneurship

## Steps

### Step 1: Walk Away From Profit When a Clearer Vision Appears

Sánchez was printing money in dry cleaning when he saw a new opportunity in FaceTime-based communication. The existing business was not failing; it was thriving. But thriving is not the same as aligned. If a new idea genuinely excites you more than the business currently funding your lifestyle, that excitement is a signal worth taking seriously. Walk-away decisions are easier when you are already winning, because you have leverage and optionality.

### Step 2: Validate the Vision Before Abandoning Revenue

Before selling the dry cleaning locations, Sánchez spoke to trusted partners about the idea, observed the product working in prototype, and got strong signals from industry insiders. Do not abandon a stable income stream on a vague hunch. Talk to people who have done what you want to do, build a small working version, and get external validation before you make the irreversible move.

### Step 3: Expect Systemic Barriers and Prepare to Work Around Them

Sánchez discovered that only 2 percent of Latino-founded companies receive venture funding. That is a structural disadvantage, not a personal failure. Research the specific barriers in your market before you enter the room, so rejection is not surprising. Know the numbers, understand the bias, and decide in advance that it will not stop you.

### Step 4: Persist Through Rejection Without Internalizing It

Sánchez received a couple hundred investor rejections. The hardest ones came from other millionaires and billionaires, who asked the same philosophical question repeatedly to test founders. Rejection from investors is almost never about you personally; it is about portfolio fit, timing, or their own constraints. Keep track of the no's, but do not let them reshape your self-assessment.

### Step 5: Stand on Your Principles Even When It Costs You

Sánchez stopped an interview in progress when he saw a host disrespect a guest. That moment of authenticity changed the trajectory of his brand and his app. People remember how you showed up when it was inconvenient. Protecting your integrity in small, visible moments builds trust that compounds over time.

## Examples

### Example 1: Leaving a Profitable Business for a New Vision

A restaurant owner who runs two profitable locations decides to close one and invest the capital into a ghost kitchen and delivery-focused brand. The existing restaurant is still profitable, but the founder sees that delivery is reshaping the industry and wants to be ahead of the curve rather than defending the old model.

### Example 2: Persisting Through Venture Rejection

A founder from an underrepresented background applies to 150 accelerator programs and receives 147 rejections. The three acceptances include one that provides enough capital and network access to prove the model. The founder later says the rejections were the most useful data points because they forced refinement of the pitch and product.

### Example 3: Authenticity Redirecting Trajectory

A fashion brand founder speaks up when a podcast host makes a dismissive comment about their industry. The host respects the correction, amplifies the brand afterward, and the resulting interview becomes the brand's most-shared marketing asset.

## Best Practices

- Walk away from profit only after validating the new vision with real data and trusted advisors
- Research systemic barriers in your target market so rejection does not surprise you
- Track rejections as data points, not as verdicts on your worth
- Protect your principles in small, public moments

## Keep In Mind

- Profit and alignment are not the same thing
- Systemic bias is real; plan for it, do not ignore it
- Authenticity is a long-term asset, not a short-term risk

## Security & Safety Notes

- All advice is drawn from a publicly available podcast interview
- Do your own due diligence before making any business or investment decisions
- Consult qualified legal and financial advisors when exiting a business or raising capital

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Leaving a profitable business without validating the new opportunity
  **Solution:** Build a prototype and get external validation before making the full transition

- **Problem:** Taking investor rejection personally
  **Solution:** Treat every no as data about fit and timing, not as a judgment on your ability

- **Problem:** Ignoring systemic barriers until they surprise you
  **Solution:** Research the funding landscape for your demographic before entering the room

- **Problem:** Sacrificing authenticity to close a deal or impress an audience
  **Solution:** Stand on your principles even when it costs you in the short term
