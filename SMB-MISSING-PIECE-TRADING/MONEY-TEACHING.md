# SMB - The Missing Piece That Made Trading Better Through Collaboration

## Overview

This money teaching explains why trading as part of a collaborative team or "pod" outperforms the lone wolf model, based on SMB Capital's experience. The core insight is that no single trader can be elite at all the cognitive strengths required for trading, including high-bandwidth thinking, quick thinking, deep analytical thinking, idea generation, trade and risk management, execution, and narrative understanding, but a team with complementary strengths and a shared playbook can produce results no individual could replicate alone, as demonstrated by specific trade examples. The teaching walks through how to form or join a trading team, what roles to look for, how to communicate effectively, and what infrastructure to build.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are a solo retail trader looking to improve your trading results
- When you want to form a trading pod or join an existing team
- When your current collaboration is underperforming due to misaligned playbooks or poor communication
- When you want to build shared infrastructure to track setups and trades
- When you are unsure how to find and vet the right trading partners
- When you want to understand why group trade reviews can outperform solo analysis

## Steps

### Step 1: Adopt a Team-First Mindset

Accept that the lone wolf model limits your potential. Even if you are a successful solo trader, collaboration improves your trading results. You do not need to be the best at every aspect of trading — you only need to be the best at what you are the best at, and find others who cover your weaknesses. Look at sports teams: an elite rebounder like Dennis Rodman did not need to be the best scorer. Similarly, in trading, you do not need to excel at idea generation, execution, risk management, and narrative reading all at once.

### Step 2: Identify Your Cognitive Strengths

Take a clear inventory of where you add the most value. Common trading strengths include:
- High-bandwidth thinking: managing many positions simultaneously
- Quick thinking: processing information rapidly
- Deep analytical thinking: drilling into the details
- Idea generation: spotting unique setups
- Trade and risk management: structuring positions and exits
- Execution: timing entries and exits precisely
- Narrative understanding: reading the broader market environment

### Step 3: Find Complementary Partners

Seek traders whose strengths fill the weaknesses in your own trading. The ideal team has members who watch similar playbooks (so they are evaluating the same setups) but bring different strengths to the table. Avoid filling the pod with people who have the same strengths as you. If you are a quant-focused data scientist, look for a discretionary trader. If you are a scalper, look for someone with patience for the fat pitch.

### Step 4: Network to Build Your Pod

Trading pods do not form without effort. You must put in effort to find teammates:
- Join mentorship programs, trading summits, or in-house trader communities like SMB's Inside Access
- Engage on FinTwit (Twitter/X) by commenting and replying to traders with similar playbooks
- Post in YouTube, Spotify, or podcast comment sections looking for pod members
- Attend in-person events (summits, meetups) where traders who treat trading as a business congregate
- Use Discord communities associated with trading education programs to connect with like-minded traders

### Step 5: Vet Playbook Alignment and Communication Style

Before committing to a joint account or small pod, ensure alignment on:
- Playbook overlap: you need shared setups to evaluate together
- Trade frequency and selectivity: are you both waiting for A+ setups or are you scalping 5-6 low R:R trades per day?
- Grading system: how do you both rate a setup? A trade one person calls an A+ might be a C to the other. Define a common grading rubric.
- Communication style: are you both comfortable speaking up, disagreeing, and being transparent about conviction levels?

### Step 6: Establish Communication Protocols

Communication is the biggest factor in long-term pod success. Implement these rules:
- Always explain why a trade is relevant, not just what you see. State your conviction level and the narrative behind it.
- Disagree openly. Say "this is a C trade to me" rather than defaulting to groupthink.
- Do not let excitement about a partner's ideas override your own selectivity.
- Communicate urgency and context. Instead of saying "relative strength is strong," say "this stock should be on everyone's watchlist right now because the breakout could be the biggest trade of the month."
- Set up daily or weekly check-ins, even if it is just a Discord text thread in the morning.

### Step 7: Build Shared Infrastructure

Create a common workspace where the team logs playbooks, charts, and trade reviews. SMB uses Notion to build a shared playbook library. Benefits include:
- Everyone can see write-ups on gold trades, IPO setups, or oil trades
- The team builds a larger library of examples than any individual could compile alone
- Catalyst and setup tracking becomes centralized
- Post-trade reviews become easier because all context is in one place

### Step 8: Run Regular Group Trade Reviews

Review trades together rather than in isolation. Group reviews expose blind spots and reveal alternative approaches. Ask: "What did we miss?" "How would you have handled this differently?" "What pattern are we repeating?" Group review prevents the isolation effect where you repeat the same mistakes alone.

### Step 9: Define Natural Roles (Do Not Over-Engineer)

Roles should develop naturally, but aim for these three core functions:
- Technology/Quant: backtests strategies, codes scripts, uses AI, automates execution
- Market Feel / Narrative: understands what to trade, when to step on the gas, and when to wait for the fat pitch
- Execution / Timing: knows when price, volume, and time converge for maximum expected value, and can pound the table at the critical moment

Everyone should understand the playbook and execution, but one person typically leads each area. Do not assign strict role definitions too early — allow overlap to develop.

### Step 10: Avoid Common Pitfalls

- Misaligned playbooks: if one person thinks the account is for scalping and the other thinks it is for A+ breakout trades, you will trade too frequently with low R:R trades or miss high-selectivity setups
- Groupthink: saying "yes" to every trade idea prevents the team from benefiting from different perspectives
- Making unilateral trades without communicating: one person making unilateral decisions breaks trust
- Cloning teammates: do not fill the pod with traders who trade with the same playbook and the same strengths
- Giving up too early: forming a pod takes work and multiple conversations before the relationship becomes productive.

## Examples

### Example 1: BLSH IPO Day One Trade — The Power of Four Traders as One Machine

On August 13th, BLSH opened at 85. The SMB team identified it as a potential A or A+ day-one IPO. One trader placed the opening order. Another had back-tested IPO day-one moves over the past three to four years and estimated the initial price target. A third determined a 5% stop was appropriate given the lack of prior price levels. A fourth was ready to add aggressively on dips and set stops at the low of day.

The trade opened, they entered immediately, added to the position, hit their offers, and the stock faded to close around 67. Garret noted: "I would never have done that by myself. Not in a million years." The team combined bandwidth, back-testing, execution speed, and risk management in a way no individual could replicate alone.

### Example 2: Dr. S and K Fits — Complementary Strengths Over Decades

Dr. S is one of the best traders at SMB but barely knows how to set a price alert. He cannot code and does not use much technology. K Fits and the rest of the team built technology for him. In return, Dr. S provides call-outs and market intuition based on that technology. For example, K Fits built a low-float scanner for Dr. S. Now every pre-market, Dr. S calls out the runners of the day from that scanner — insights the creators of the scanner were not even seeing themselves. The advantage grows because each person operates in their strength zone.

### Example 3: The Failed Two-Person Pod — Misaligned Playbooks Harm Teams

Early in his career, Tim joined a two-person joint account with a partner who was passionate about active scalping (5-6 trades per day, 1.2:1 to 1.5:1 risk-reward). Tim preferred high-selectivity, wait-for-the-fat-pitch trading. Because there were only two people, the more active partner's conviction overwhelmed the decisions. They became a scalping account by default, even though both thought they were waiting for bigger trades. The account was death by a thousand small losses — overtrading it to the end. The lesson: a common playbook and a process for grading setups are non-negotiable, even (especially) in a two-person pod.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Trade with similar playbooks but complementary strengths. Shared setups + different cognitive roles = benefit
- ✅ Communicate with full context: state your conviction, the narrative, and why it matters — not just the raw observation
- ✅ Allow yourself and others to say "no" to a trade without damaging the relationship. Disagreement is healthy.
- ✅ Build a shared workspace (Notion, Google Docs, etc.) to centralize playbooks, charts, and trade reviews
- ✅ Network with the intent to learn and add value, not just to recruit. The best collaborations form naturally
- ✅ Let roles develop naturally rather than assigning strict titles from day one
- ✅ Run regular group trade reviews to avoid the isolation of solo decision-making
- ✅ Join or create communities (summits, Discord, mentorship programs) where traders who treat trading as a business congregate
- ✅ Look for teammates who are decisive and want the ball in their hands
- ✅ Be the facilitator/organizer who brings out others' strengths, not a dictator

## Keep In Mind

- Roles overlap significantly. One person may lead in technology but still contribute ideas. Do not expect clearly defined roles.
- Forming a pod takes time and effort. It will not happen from a single email. Expect multiple conversations and failed attempts before the relationship becomes productive.
- You do not need a joint account to benefit from a pod. Communication alone — even if everyone takes their own trades — helps you catch mistakes and find better setups through shared insights.
- Communication quality matters more than communication quantity. Saying "relative strength is strong" is not as useful as saying "this stock should be on everyone's watchlist because the breakout could be the biggest trade of the month."
- Most SMB traders do not trade alone. If they can do it on a trading desk, you can replicate it remotely.
- Your pod does not need to be large. Two to four people is enough to cover the key strengths.
- It is okay to be a lone wolf if that is how you operate best and you operate well alone. This teaching is for traders who are naturally social and thrive on collaboration.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Be cautious with joint accounts. Ensure you trust your partners and have clear legal and financial agreements in place.
- If trading a joint account, define position sizing, risk parameters, and loss allocation before you start.
- Do not share account credentials or sensitive financial data with untested partners.
- When networking publicly (Twitter/X, YouTube comments), avoid sharing specific positions, entry prices, or account sizes.
- Be aware that giving orders or taking direction from unverified individuals can violate broker terms or regulations depending on your jurisdiction.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Misaligned playbooks leading to trading too frequently with low R:R trades or missing high-selectivity setups.
  **Solution:** Before forming a pod, explicitly discuss and document your selectivity, trade frequency, and setup criteria. Create a grading rubric (A+, A, B, C) and agree that C trades will not be taken jointly.

- **Problem:** Groupthink — saying yes to every trade idea because you do not want to conflict with the team.
  **Solution:** Make it a standard practice that every member can veto a trade or call it a C. Your job is to bring your selectivity and conviction to the table, not to rubber-stamp others.

- **Problem:** One person overwhelms decisions because of high conviction, overriding the other's strengths.
  **Solution:** Establish that each person's voice is weighted by their role and expertise, not by how loudly or passionately they argue. In a two-person pod, this requires explicit decision rules.

- **Problem:** Making unilateral trades without communicating, which damages the team's working relationship and defeats the purpose of the team.
  **Solution:** Even if you take the trade independently, tell the team what you are doing and why. Transparency keeps everyone informed.

- **Problem:** Cloning teammates — filling the pod with people who trade with the same playbook and the same strengths.
  **Solution:** Vet for complementary strengths, not identical playbooks. Ask: "What are you best at?" and "What do you struggle with?" instead of "What ticker are you watching?"

- **Problem:** Giving up too early because the first attempt at collaboration did not work out.
  **Solution:** Pods take time to build trust and a productive working relationship. Treat early attempts as learning experiences. If it does not work, analyze why (playbook misalignment? communication failure?) and apply the lesson to the next attempt.
