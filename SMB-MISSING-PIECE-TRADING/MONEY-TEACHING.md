# SMB - The Missing Piece That Made Trading Better Through Collaboration

## Overview

This money teaching explains why trading as part of a collaborative team or "pod" produces better results than the solo-trader model, based on SMB Capital's experience. The core insight is that it is difficult for single traders to be elite at all of the following cognitive strengths simultaneously — including high-bandwidth thinking, quick thinking, deep analytical thinking, idea generation, trade and risk management, execution, and narrative understanding — but a team with complementary strengths and a shared playbook can produce results that would be challenging for any individual to replicate alone, as demonstrated by specific trade examples. The teaching walks through how to form or join a trading team, what roles to look for, how to communicate effectively, and what infrastructure to build.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are a solo retail trader looking to improve your trading results (higher returns, lower drawdowns, or more consistent performance)
- When you want to form a trading pod or join an existing team
- When your current collaboration is not meeting its potential to identify high-quality setups and manage risk due to misaligned playbooks, inconsistent communication, or poor vetting of setups — indicators include overtrading, agreeing with the group despite personal reservations, or unresolved conflicts over trade selection
- When you want to build shared infrastructure to track setups and trades
- When you are unsure how to find and vet compatible trading partners
- When you want to understand why group trade reviews can outperform solo analysis

## Steps

### Step 1: Adopt a Team-First Mindset

Accept that the solo-trader model limits the variety of strategies and cognitive perspectives you can apply to your trading. Even if you are a successful solo trader, collaboration increases the likelihood of identifying higher-quality setups and managing risk more effectively. You do not need to be the best at every aspect of trading — you only need to be the best at what you are the best at, and find others who cover your weaknesses. Look at sports teams: an elite rebounder like Dennis Rodman did not need to be the best scorer. The same specialization principle applies in trading: you do not need to excel at idea generation, execution, risk management, and narrative reading all at once.

### Step 2: Identify Your Cognitive Strengths

Take a documented inventory of where you add the most value to the pod based on your demonstrated strengths and recent performance. Common trading strengths include:
- High-bandwidth thinking: managing many positions simultaneously
- Quick thinking: processing information rapidly
- Deep analytical thinking: drilling into the details
- Idea generation: spotting unique setups
- Trade and risk management: structuring positions and exits
- Execution: timing entries and exits precisely
- Narrative understanding: reading the broader market environment

### Step 3: Find Complementary Partners

Seek traders whose strengths address weaknesses in your own trading. A well-functioning team has members who trade the same core setups (so they are evaluating the same opportunities) but bring different strengths to the table. Avoid filling the pod with people who have the same strengths as you. If you are a quant-focused data scientist, look for a discretionary trader. If you are a scalper, look for someone with patience for the highest-graded setup.

### Step 4: Network to Build Your Pod

Trading pods do not form without consistent effort over weeks or months. You must put in consistent effort to find teammates:
- Join mentorship programs, trading summits, or in-house trader communities like SMB's Inside Access
- Engage on FinTwit (Twitter/X) by commenting and replying to traders with compatible playbooks
- Post in YouTube, Spotify, or podcast comment sections looking for pod members
- Attend in-person events (summits, meetups) where traders who treat trading as a business congregate
- Use Discord communities associated with trading education programs to connect with like-minded traders

### Step 5: Vet Playbook Alignment and Communication Style

Before committing to a joint account or a pod of two to four people, ensure alignment on:
- Playbook overlap: you need to trade the same types of setups so you can evaluate them together
  - Trade frequency and selectivity: are all pod members waiting for A+ setups or are you scalping five to six low risk-reward (R:R) trades per day, meaning risk-reward ratios between 1.2:1 and 1.5:1?
- Grading system: how do all pod members rate a setup? A trade one person calls an A+ might be a C to another. Define a common grading rubric.
- Grading labels: define what each label means so all pod members agree. For example: A+ = a highest-conviction, highest-probability setup where risk-reward is exceptional; A = a strong setup with clear edge; B = a viable setup with moderate edge; C = a low-conviction setup that does not meet the pod's joint-trade threshold.
- Communication style: are all pod members comfortable speaking up, disagreeing, and being transparent about conviction levels?

### Step 6: Establish Communication Protocols

Communication is a critical factor in long-term pod success. Implement these rules:
  - In every trade communication, explain why a trade is relevant, not just what you see. State your conviction level on the pod's grading rubric (A+, A, B, or C) and the narrative behind it.
- Disagree openly. Say "this is a low-conviction trade to me" rather than defaulting to agreeing with the group despite personal reservations.
- Do not let a pod member's enthusiasm for an idea override your personal selectivity criteria.
- Communicate why a setup matters and how time-sensitive it is, along with the full context. Instead of saying "relative strength is strong," say "this stock should be on everyone's watchlist right now because the breakout could generate a top R-multiple trade for this month."
- Set up daily or weekly check-ins, even if it is just a Discord text thread in the morning.

### Step 7: Build Shared Infrastructure

Create a shared workspace accessible to all pod members where the team logs playbooks, charts, and trade reviews. SMB uses Notion to build a shared playbook library. Benefits include:
- All pod members can see write-ups on gold trades, IPO setups, or oil trades
  - The team builds a collective library of examples that grows as each member contributes, that any single individual could compile alone within a calendar year
  - The team centralizes catalyst and setup tracking
- Post-trade reviews become more efficient because all context is in one place

### Step 8: Run Regular Group Trade Reviews

Review trades together rather than in isolation. Group reviews expose unrecognized errors or missed considerations and reveal alternative approaches. Ask: "What did we miss?" "How would you have handled this differently?" "What pattern are we repeating?" Group review prevents traders from repeating the same mistakes alone without external feedback.

### Step 9: Define Natural Roles (Do Not Over-Engineer)

Roles should emerge from the team's actual trading patterns, but aim for these three core functions:
- Technology/Quant: backtests strategies, codes scripts, develops automated trading models and scripts, automates execution
- Market Feel / Narrative: consistently identifies which tickers and setups are most actionable, understands when to increase position size or market exposure, and when to wait for the highest-graded setup
- Execution / Timing: knows when price, volume, and time converge for the highest expected value within the pod's risk parameters, and can emphatically advocate for entering the trade at that moment

Every pod member should understand the shared playbook and execution standards, but one person leads each of the three core function areas with defined accountability with specific metrics for that domain. Do not assign strict role definitions during the first 30 days of the pod's formation — allow overlap to develop, and clarify leadership as the team gains shared trading history together.

### Step 10: Avoid Common Pitfalls

- Misaligned playbooks: if one person thinks the account is for scalping and the other thinks it is for A+ breakout trades, the pod will trade more frequently than the pod's agreed threshold with low risk-reward (R:R) trades or miss high-selectivity setups
- Group agreement despite personal reservations: agreeing with the group and saying "yes" to trade ideas without independent evaluation because you do not want to conflict with the team.
- Making unilateral trades without communicating: one person making unilateral decisions erodes team cohesion and can lead to partnership dissolution
- Cloning teammates: do not fill the pod with traders who trade using identical strategies and have identical strengths
- Giving up too early: forming a pod requires consistent effort over weeks or months and a series of structured conversations over 3-12 weeks or months before the relationship becomes productive. Expect that early collaboration attempts will not work out before finding a productive match.

## Examples

### Example 1: BLSH IPO Day One Trade — The Power of Four Traders as One Coordinated Team

On August 13th, BLSH opened at 85. The SMB team identified it as a potential A or A+ day-one IPO. One trader placed the opening order. Another had back-tested IPO day-one moves over the past three to four years and estimated the initial price target. A third determined a 5% stop was appropriate given the lack of prior price levels. A fourth was ready to add aggressively on dips and set stops at the low of day.

The trade opened, they entered immediately, added to the position, hit their offers, and the stock faded to close at 67. Garret noted: "I would never have done that by myself. Not in a million years." The team combined the range of cognitive tasks covered, back-testing, execution speed, and risk management in a way that would be challenging for any single individual to replicate alone.

### Example 2: Dr. S and K Fits — Complementary Strengths Over Decades

Dr. S is, in Garret's assessment, one of SMB's top traders but barely knows how to set a price alert. He cannot code and does not use much technology. K Fits and the rest of the team built technology for him. In return, Dr. S provides call-outs and market intuition based on that technology. For example, K Fits built a low-float scanner for Dr. S. Now every pre-market, Dr. S calls out the runners of the day from that scanner — insights the creators of the scanner were not all seeing themselves. The advantage grows because each person operates in their area of demonstrated strength.

### Example 3: The Failed Two-Person Pod — Misaligned Playbooks Harm Teams

Early in his career, Tim joined a two-person joint account with a partner who was passionate about active day-trading with five to six trades per day and risk-reward ratios between 1.2:1 and 1.5:1. Tim preferred high-selectivity, waiting-for-the-highest-graded-setup trading. Because there were only two people, the more active partner's conviction overwhelmed the decisions. They became a day-trading account by default, even though both thought they were waiting for bigger trades. The account was depleted by a series of small losses — overtrading it to the end. The lesson: a common playbook and a process for grading setups are non-negotiable, even (especially) in a two-person pod.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Trade with aligned playbooks but complementary strengths. Shared setups + different cognitive roles = trade ideas with better risk-reward profiles and better risk management
- ✅ Communicate with complete context including conviction level on the pod's grading rubric (A+, A, B, or C), the narrative, and why it matters — not just the raw observation
- ✅ Allow yourself and others to say "no" to a trade without damaging the relationship. Disagreement that improves trade selection is healthy.
- ✅ Build a shared workspace (Notion, Google Docs, or similar tools) to centralize playbooks, charts, and trade reviews
- ✅ Network with the intent to learn and add value, not just to recruit. Successful collaborations develop through shared interests over time
- ✅ Let roles emerge from the team's actual trading patterns rather than assigning strict titles from day one
- ✅ Run weekly or bi-weekly group trade reviews to avoid the isolation of solo decision-making
- ✅ Join or create trading communities such as summits, Discord servers, and mentorship programs where traders who treat trading as a business congregate
- ✅ Look for teammates who make timely decisions based on the pod's grading rubric and are willing to take initiative on trades that meet the pod's criteria
- ✅ Be the facilitator/organizer who brings out others' strengths, not a dictator

## Keep In Mind

- Roles overlap. One person may lead the technology function but still contribute ideas. Do not expect clearly defined roles.
- Forming a pod requires consistent effort over weeks or months and a series of structured conversations over 3-12 weeks or months. Expect that early collaboration attempts will not work out before finding a productive match.
- You do not need a joint account to benefit from a pod. Communication alone — even if everyone takes their own trades — helps you catch mistakes and find better setups through shared insights.
- Communication quality matters more than communication quantity. Saying "relative strength is strong" is not as useful as saying "this stock should be on everyone's watchlist because the breakout could generate a top R-multiple trade for this month."
- At SMB, most traders collaborate with at least one other person rather than trading entirely alone. If they can collaborate on a trading desk, you can replicate that collaboration remotely.
- Your pod does not need to have more than four people. Two to four people is enough to cover the core cognitive roles (technology/quant, market feel/narrative, and execution/timing).
- It is okay to be a solo trader if that is how you operate most effectively according to your own performance metrics and you operate profitably and consistently alone with rigorous self-accountability with documented trade reviews. This teaching is for traders who are naturally social and thrive on collaboration.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Be cautious with joint accounts (verify identity, start with small positions, and document agreements). Ensure you trust your partners (verified through consistent behavior and documented agreements) and have clear legal and financial agreements in place.
- If trading a joint account, define position sizing, risk parameters, and loss allocation before you start.
- Do not share account credentials or sensitive financial data with untested partners (no documented trading history together).
- When networking on public platforms such as Twitter/X, YouTube comments, avoid sharing specific positions, entry prices, or account sizes.
- Be aware that giving orders or taking direction from unverified individuals can violate broker terms or regulations depending on your country, state, or local regulations.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Misaligned playbooks leading to trading more frequently than the pod's agreed threshold with low risk-reward (R:R) trades or missing high-selectivity setups (those meeting the pod's A+ or A grading criteria).
  **Solution:** Before forming a pod, explicitly discuss and document your selectivity, trade frequency, and setup criteria. Create a grading rubric (A+, A, B, C) and agree that C trades will not be taken jointly.

- **Problem:** Group agreement despite personal reservations: agreeing with the group and saying "yes" to trade ideas without independent evaluation because you do not want to conflict with the team.
  **Solution:** Make it a standard practice that every member can veto a trade or call it a low-conviction trade. Your job is to bring your selectivity and conviction to the table, not to rubber-stamp others.

- **Problem:** One person overwhelms decisions because of high conviction (A+ on the pod's grading rubric), overriding the other's strengths.
  **Solution:** Establish that each person's voice is weighted by their role and expertise, not by how loudly or passionately they argue. In a two-person pod, this requires explicit decision rules.

- **Problem:** Making unilateral trades without communicating, which damages the team's working relationship and defeats the purpose of the team.
  **Solution:** Even if you take the trade independently, tell the team what you are doing and why. Transparency keeps everyone informed.

- **Problem:** Cloning teammates — filling the pod with people who trade using identical strategies and have identical strengths.
  **Solution:** Vet for complementary strengths, not identical playbooks. Ask: "What are you best at?" and "What do you struggle with?" instead of "What ticker are you watching?"

- **Problem:** Giving up too early because the first attempt at collaboration did not work out.
  **Solution:** Pods take time to build trust and a productive working relationship. Treat early attempts as learning experiences. If it does not produce better results than trading alone, analyze why (playbook misalignment? communication failure?) and apply the lesson to the next attempt.
