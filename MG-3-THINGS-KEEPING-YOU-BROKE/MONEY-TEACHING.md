# 3 Things That Are Keeping You Broke That You Must Stop Today

## Overview

This money teaching identifies three behaviors that keep people trapped in financial struggle and provides actionable steps to break free from them: stop buying visible status symbols (luxury vehicles, expensive clothing, large homes) before you have income-producing assets that fully fund your lifestyle, stop acquiring depreciating liabilities before building a reliable stream of asset-based income, and stop spending money on short-term gratification instead of consistently investing in wealth-building assets. The core principle is that building wealth is not primarily about working harder or earning more income, but about allocating your money in the correct sequence: first build income-producing assets, then use the returns from those assets to fund your lifestyle. "Right things" means directing money into assets that generate income rather than into liabilities that drain money. "Right order" means building the asset base before upgrading your lifestyle.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are earning money but still feel broke or unable to accumulate real wealth because all your income goes to bills, debt payments, and lifestyle expenses
- When you are considering a major purchase (defined as any single purchase or combined purchases totaling more than one month of your after-tax income) such as a car, house, or wedding, and you have not yet confirmed that your income-producing assets generate enough consistent monthly income to cover the ongoing costs of that purchase plus all your existing expenses
- When you feel pressure to impress others through material possessions (such as luxury watches, designer clothing, high-end cars, or expensive home renovations) that you are funding with earned income rather than with returns from your assets

## Steps

### Step 1: Stop Buying Status Symbols That You Are Funding With Earned Income

Postpone any purchase whose primary purpose is to signal wealth to others (including luxury cars, designer clothing, expensive watches, large homes in prestigious neighborhoods, and lavish parties) until your income-producing assets generate enough monthly income to pay for those items and all your other living expenses. Every dollar spent to appear successful is a dollar that is not invested in building real wealth.

To clarify the correct mindset: a person who is building wealth directs money into assets that generate income, then uses only the returns from those assets to fund lifestyle purchases. A person who appears rich but is actually accumulating no wealth spends earned income on visible status symbols. Until your assets generate enough income to pay for your car, your housing, your clothing, and all your monthly bills, do not buy items primarily to project an image of success. The purpose of money is not to pay bills or to impress other people; its purpose is to build a base of income-producing assets that eventually generates enough to cover all of your expenses without requiring you to trade time for money.

### Step 2: Direct Your Money Into Income-Producing Assets Instead of Liabilities

Redirect your money away from purchases that lose value over time (such as new cars, electronics, and luxury goods) or that create ongoing monthly costs (such as expensive subscriptions, recurring clothing box or rental services, or oversized homes) and toward assets that generate income for you (such as dividend-paying stocks, rental real estate, intellectual property, businesses, or investment accounts).

Here is how to think about your money flow in concrete terms: every dollar you earn enters your finances through the income window. That dollar then has two possible destinations: it either flows out through the outgo window (to pay for bills, debt payments, rent, food, and lifestyle purchases), or it flows into the income-producing asset window (into investments, savings accounts earmarked for investments, or business expansion). People who struggle financially send nearly all income out the outgo window. People who build wealth direct at least 20 percent of every dollar that comes in the income window into the asset window, before paying any other bills. People who build wealth channel money into the asset window even in the early stages when the asset generates no visible returns. Continue directing money into the asset window consistently for months and years, even when the returns are not yet visible, because eventually the asset window will grow large enough that its monthly returns pay all your bills, pay off all your debt, and fund your entire lifestyle. Do not reduce or stop contributions to the asset window when you face doubt or when you cannot see immediate results.

To make this actionable: before you pay any bill or make any purchase, calculate the total dollar amount that came in through your income window this month, and transfer a fixed percentage of that amount (at least 20 percent is recommended, but the specific percentage should be set by you based on your income and expenses) into a dedicated account or investment vehicle for income-producing assets. This transfer must happen before you pay rent, buy groceries, or make any other payment.

### Step 3: Delay Major Purchases Until Your Asset Income Covers Them, and Always Produce Before You Play

Before any single purchase that costs more than one month of your after-tax income, complete this decision process: first, identify whether the purchase is a liability (such as a primary residence upgrade, a luxury vehicle, a wedding, or a degree or certification that does not directly increase your monthly income) or an asset (such as a rental property, a business investment, or an education that directly increases your monthly income). Second, if the purchase is a liability, confirm that your income-producing assets currently generate enough monthly income to pay for the purchase's ongoing costs (such as loan payments, maintenance, insurance, and property taxes) plus all your existing monthly expenses without requiring any of your earned income. If the purchase is an asset, evaluate whether its expected monthly return exceeds its monthly cost by at least 20 percent within the first 24 months.

Major purchases made before your asset base is secure act as walls between you and wealth. Specific premature purchases to avoid include: upgrading to a larger or more expensive house before your rental properties or investments generate enough income to cover the new mortgage and all related costs; buying a vehicle with monthly payments that exceed 15 percent of your current earned income; pursuing a college degree in a field where technology is changing so rapidly that the skills will likely be obsolete by the time you graduate (as measured by current industry employment projections and employer requirements); and spending more than six months of your current after-tax income on a wedding before you have invested at least the same amount into your marriage relationship and your asset base.

Delay gratification by producing before you play: do not take vacations funded by borrowed money or by withdrawing money from your asset-building account. Do not spend money on parties, celebrations, or entertainment purchases (such as concert tickets, expensive meals, or luxury goods) whose primary purpose is to distract you from dissatisfaction with your current situation. Do not reward yourself with purchases when you have not completed your planned asset-building contribution for that month. If you feel that you need a vacation or a break from your daily routine, the root cause is that you have not yet structured your finances so that your assets generate enough income to fund rest and recreation without requiring you to withdraw from your principal or go into debt. Getting rich requires doing the foundational steps first: you cannot regularly spend money on recreation and simultaneously build the asset base needed to fund that recreation sustainably.

### Step 4: Commit to Structured, Time-Bound Learning to Keep Up With Market Changes

Technology and market conditions change faster than most formal education programs update their curricula, which means that many people hold credentials and specialized knowledge that employers no longer require or that industries have replaced with new tools. The historical pattern where deep specialization in one narrow field guaranteed lifetime wealth is no longer reliable in most industries. Today, people who build wealth fastest are those who can identify emerging skills that the market values, learn those skills in weeks or months rather than years, and apply them to generate income quickly.

To implement this: every quarter, spend at least five hours researching which skills are currently in demand in your industry and in adjacent industries (using sources such as job posting sites, industry reports, and conversations with people who are earning the income you want to earn). Select one skill to learn that directly increases your ability to generate income, allocate at least three hours per week to studying and practicing that skill, and apply it within 30 days to a real project, client, or income opportunity. Repeat this cycle every quarter. Do not invest years of time and tens of thousands of dollars into a specialized degree or credential without first confirming that the credential will still be required by employers or clients at the time you finish the program. Update your skills and knowledge continuously so your earning ability evolves with the market rather than becoming obsolete.

### Step 5: Make Financial Decisions Based on Your Own Goals, Not on What Other People Think

Separate your financial choices from the opinions of people who are not responsible for your financial outcomes. Before making any spending or investment decision, ask: does this purchase directly build my asset base or reduce my liabilities, based on my own written financial plan? If the answer is no, do not make the purchase regardless of what friends, family, or social media contacts might think. Do not spend money on items or experiences primarily to signal success to people who do not bear the financial consequences of your choices. Your measure of success should be a specific set of metrics that you define: for example, the total monthly income generated by your assets, the total value of your assets, or the percentage of your living expenses that your assets cover. Do not use lifestyle appearance, social media reactions, or the opinions of people around you as measures of your financial progress. The people who truly influence your financial trajectory are those who evaluate you based on your results and your asset base, not on the image you project. Focus on building real, measurable wealth rather than on performing wealth for an audience that has no stake in your financial future.

## Examples

### Example 1: Buying a Vehicle With Monthly Payments That Exceed 15 Percent of Earned Income

A person earning $100,000 per month (after taxes) buys a luxury vehicle with $3,300 in monthly loan or lease payments, believing the payment is small relative to their monthly income. When an economic downturn reduces their monthly income to $40,000, the $3,300 monthly vehicle payment becomes a significant burden because it is now 8.25 percent of their reduced income, and the vehicle continues to depreciate while the loan balance remains. The correct behavior is: first, calculate whether your monthly vehicle payment will remain below 15 percent of your earned income even if your income drops by 60 percent. Second, wait until your income-producing assets generate enough monthly income to pay the full vehicle cost (including insurance, maintenance, and fuel) without requiring any earned income. Third, treat the vehicle as a tool for transportation or business use, not as a status symbol. Only after your assets consistently cover all your expenses should you consider upgrading your vehicle, and even then the purchase should be a reward for building the asset base rather than a display for others.

### Example 2: Investing Time and Money Into an Asset That Takes Months or Years to Pay Off

An aspiring author spends nine months writing, editing, and designing a book, and invests money in professional editors and designers, receiving no income during that period. The urge to stop the project and redirect that money to immediate needs builds. The correct behavior is: before starting the project, define the expected return (such as projected annual passive income from book sales) and the timeline (such as 12 to 24 months to break even). During the months when returns are zero, continue investing the same dollar amount you originally allocated to the asset each month, or reduce other expenses before reducing the asset contribution. In this example, the book eventually generates $81,000 per year in passive income from work that was completed once. A second book written years later generates $180,000 per year. This demonstrates that delaying gratification and persisting through periods of zero visible returns produces compounding asset income that far exceeds the initial time and money invested.

### Example 3: Upgrading Housing or Lifestyle Before Your Asset Base Can Absorb a Downturn

An entrepreneur who has been earning between $100,000 and $250,000 per month in earned income upgrades to a larger house, buys luxury vehicles, and increases monthly living expenses, feeling that their high income makes the lifestyle sustainable. When an economic shift causes clients to stop paying invoices, the reduced earned income cannot cover the increased fixed expenses. The entrepreneur must sell the house at a loss and endures seven years of financial recovery. The correct behavior is: before increasing your housing costs, calculate whether your income-producing assets generate enough monthly income to cover the new housing payment plus all other monthly expenses, even if your earned income drops to zero for six months. Maintain a modest lifestyle until your asset base is large enough that its monthly returns absorb economic shocks without requiring you to sell assets at a loss. A practical test: if losing all earned income for six months would force you to sell an asset or take on debt, your lifestyle is too expensive for your current asset base.

### Example 4: Pursuing a Degree in a Field Where Demand Is Declining Due to Technology

A person enrolls in a college program for a field where current industry data shows that automation, artificial intelligence, or outsourcing will reduce the number of available jobs by at least 30 percent within the next five years. The person accumulates tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars in student debt for a credential that, by the time they graduate, does not qualify them for employment that pays enough to service the debt and cover living expenses. The correct behavior is: before enrolling in any degree or certification program, research current job posting data for that field (such as the number of open positions, median starting salary, and five-year employment projections from official labor statistics). Calculate the expected total cost of the program (tuition plus living expenses for the duration) and compare it to the expected first-year salary. If the expected first-year salary is less than 1.5 times the total program cost, or if employment projections show a decline of more than 15 percent over the next five years, do not enroll. Many people who completed degrees in fields that later contracted now hold jobs that do not require their credentials, demonstrating that premature or uninformed acquisition of credentials can become a financial trap rather than an asset.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Before paying any bill or making any purchase, transfer at least 20 percent of every dollar of earned income into a dedicated income-producing asset account or investment vehicle, and only spend from the remaining amount on living expenses and liabilities. This 20 percent is a minimum; increase the percentage as your income grows and your essential expenses are covered.
- ✅ Before any single purchase that costs more than one month of your after-tax income, complete the following decision process: (1) identify whether the purchase is a liability or an asset, (2) if it is a liability, confirm that your assets generate enough monthly income to pay for it and all your existing expenses without using earned income, (3) if it is an asset, confirm that its expected monthly return exceeds its monthly cost by at least 20 percent within 24 months.
- ✅ Only spend money that your income-producing assets have already generated as returns. Do not withdraw the principal amount you originally invested in assets to fund your lifestyle; principal is for generating future returns, not for consumption.
- ✅ Focus on learning and implementing the foundational wealth-building practices first: consistently allocating a fixed percentage of income to assets, tracking your asset window growth monthly, and avoiding liabilities that drain your cash flow. Do not chase advanced strategies (such as speculative investments, complex derivatives, or get-rich-quick schemes) until you have maintained consistent asset contributions for at least 12 months and your assets generate enough income to cover at least 30 percent of your monthly expenses.
- ✅ Every quarter, spend at least five hours researching one high-demand skill that directly increases your income potential, allocate at least three hours per week to learning and practicing that skill, and apply it to a real income opportunity within 30 days of beginning. This ensures your earning ability evolves with the market rather than becoming obsolete.
- ✅ Track and measure your financial progress using these specific metrics: (1) total monthly income generated by your assets, (2) total value of your income-producing assets, (3) the percentage of your monthly living expenses that your assets cover, and (4) the ratio of your asset contributions to your total earned income. Do not use your earned income level, the car you drive, or the home you live in as measures of wealth-building progress.
- ✅ Delay gratification consistently by following the order of operations: build asset income first, then use asset returns to fund lifestyle upgrades. Track the gap between your asset income and your total expenses, and only upgrade your lifestyle when that gap closes completely and your asset income covers all expenses plus a 20 percent buffer.

## Keep In Mind

- How you think about money directly affects your financial outcomes. If you view money as something to spend on bills and status symbols, you will spend it that way. If you view money as seed capital for assets that generate income, you will allocate it that way. Changing your financial behavior starts with changing the decisions you make when you receive money.
- Working longer hours or earning a higher salary will not build wealth if you continue spending all earned income on liabilities and lifestyle. Wealth is created by the gap between what you earn and what you spend, directed consistently into income-producing assets over time.
- Earning more income without changing how you allocate money will only increase your spending if your habits and beliefs about money do not change. Higher income without a corresponding increase in asset contributions only raises your lifestyle costs.
- Your earned income and your asset income serve different purposes: earned income is for covering current living expenses and building your asset base; asset income is for funding your lifestyle without requiring you to trade time for money. Keep these two streams mentally and financially separate.
- Spending money on vacations, parties, or celebrations before your asset income covers your expenses delays wealth building because that money is taken from the pool that could be generating compounding returns. Plan recreation only with money that your assets have already generated, not with earned income that should be directed into assets.
- Specialized knowledge in a single field can become a financial liability if that field shrinks due to technological change. The financial advantage belongs to people who can identify emerging opportunities, learn the required skills quickly, and apply them to generate income.
- Wealth is a result of consistently providing value to others through assets, products, or services. Focus on building something that generates income for you, rather than focusing on wealth itself as the goal.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not take on consumer debt (such as auto loans, credit card debt, or personal loans) to fund lifestyle purchases that you cannot afford with your current earned income. Consumer debt increases your monthly outgo, reduces your ability to contribute to assets, and magnifies financial risk if your earned income decreases. The only exception is debt used to acquire an asset that generates monthly income exceeding the debt payment by at least 20 percent, where you can demonstrate that the income will begin within 12 months.
- Only borrow money when the expected monthly return from the asset you are purchasing exceeds the monthly cost of the debt (principal plus interest) by at least 20 percent, and when you can document the source of that return (such as a signed lease, a verified business contract, or audited historical returns). Do not borrow based on projected or speculative returns.
- Do not carry a balance on credit cards for consumer purchases. Credit card interest (often exceeding 20 percent annually) turns consumption into compounding loss. Pay credit card balances in full every month. If you cannot pay the balance in full, do not make the purchase until you can.
- Economic recessions, layoffs, and industry disruptions can reduce earned income by 50 percent or more within months. Before upgrading your housing, vehicle, or lifestyle, confirm that your income-producing assets generate enough monthly income to cover all your expenses (including the new expense) for at least six months without any earned income.
- Before enrolling in any degree, certification, or training program, verify three data points: (1) the current number of job openings in the field, (2) the median starting salary for entry-level positions, and (3) the five-year employment projection from official labor statistics. Only proceed if the total program cost is less than 1.5 times the expected first-year salary and employment projections show growth or stability, not decline.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Making purchases primarily to signal wealth to others when you are funding those purchases with earned income rather than with asset returns. This includes luxury vehicles, designer clothing, expensive watches, home upgrades in prestigious neighborhoods, and lavish events. **Solution:** Before any purchase that costs more than one month of your after-tax income, confirm that your income-producing assets generate enough monthly income to pay for it and all your other expenses without requiring any of your earned income. Track your metric: the percentage of your lifestyle expenses covered by asset income. The target is 100 percent. Until you reach that target, limit lifestyle purchases to essentials funded by earned income only after your asset contribution is complete.
- **Problem:** Spending all or most of your earned income on bills, rent, debt payments, and consumer purchases, leaving nothing to direct into income-producing assets. This keeps you in a cycle where you must continue working indefinitely because you have no assets generating passive income. **Solution:** Before you pay any bill or make any purchase each month, calculate your total earned income for that month and transfer a fixed percentage (minimum 20 percent) into a dedicated asset-building account. Treat this transfer as a non-negotiable payment to yourself, like a bill you must pay before any other obligation. Set up an automatic transfer on payday so the decision is made before you have a chance to spend the money.
- **Problem:** Stopping contributions to assets or withdrawing money from assets when returns are not immediately visible. This happens during the early months of building a business, writing a book, or investing in real estate, where months or years pass before the asset generates income. **Solution:** Before starting any asset-building project, define the expected timeline to first return (such as 12 months) and the expected annual return (such as $10,000 per year). During the period before returns begin, maintain your planned monthly contribution to the asset. If you need to reduce your contribution, reduce your lifestyle expenses first before touching the asset account. Track your progress against the timeline you set, and only reassess the project after the timeline has passed and actual returns differ from projections.
- **Problem:** Buying a house, vehicle, or funding a wedding before your income-producing assets generate enough income to cover the ongoing costs of those purchases plus all your existing expenses. This creates fixed monthly obligations that reduce your ability to build assets and can force you to sell assets at a loss if your earned income drops. **Solution:** Before any major purchase, run this test: if your earned income dropped to zero for six months, could you pay for the new purchase's ongoing costs (mortgage or loan payment, insurance, maintenance, property taxes, utilities) plus all your existing expenses without selling assets or taking on new debt? If the answer is no, do not make the purchase until your asset income reaches the required level. For a home purchase, your monthly mortgage, insurance, property tax, and maintenance costs must be covered by asset income. For a vehicle, the monthly payment plus insurance and maintenance must be covered by asset income. For a wedding, the total cost must be covered by asset income or must be less than 20 percent of your current liquid assets.
- **Problem:** Believing that earning more income will automatically make you wealthy. Many people increase their income by 50 percent or more and then increase their spending by the same amount, leaving their savings rate unchanged and their asset base unchanged. **Solution:** When your income increases, keep your lifestyle expenses the same for at least 12 months and direct 100 percent of the additional income into income-producing assets. Track your savings rate (the percentage of income directed to assets) before and after each income increase. If your savings rate does not increase when your income increases, you are spending the additional income rather than building your asset base.
- **Problem:** Relying on specialized knowledge or credentials in a field where technology or market demand is declining, making your earning ability obsolete before you have built a sufficient asset base. **Solution:** Every quarter, assess whether your current skills are in growing or declining demand by reviewing job posting volumes, salary trends, and industry reports. Allocate at least three hours per week to learning one skill that is in growing demand and that you can apply within 30 days to increase your income. Do not commit to a degree or certification program longer than 12 months without verifying that employment in that field is projected to grow, not shrink.
- **Problem:** Taking vacations, throwing parties, or funding entertainment with borrowed money or with money that was allocated for asset building. This creates debt or reduces your asset base while providing no lasting financial benefit. **Solution:** Only take vacations or fund celebrations with money that your income-producing assets have already generated as returns. Do not use earned income that was earmarked for asset contributions, and do not use borrowed money. A practical rule: if your assets do not yet cover 100 percent of your monthly expenses, your recreation must be low-cost or free until that threshold is reached.
