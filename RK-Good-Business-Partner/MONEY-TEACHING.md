# How to Find a GOOD Business Partner

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills Robert Kiyosaki's principles for selecting, evaluating, and maintaining the right business partners. It explains why most people choose partners poorly—confusing good character with good partnership fit—and provides a concrete framework for assessing whether someone truly adds value to your deal. It also covers the three non-negotiable pillars of any successful business venture: good partners, good financing, and good management or employees.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When someone approaches you to become a business partner
- When evaluating whether an existing partnership is still working
- When entering a real estate deal or any business venture
- When you have previously chosen a partner based on personality rather than skills or value
- When deciding whether to remove a partner who no longer fits the business

## Steps

### Step 1: Ask the One Question Every Potential Partner Must Answer

Before agreeing to any partnership, ask: "What value do you bring that I don't already have?" Evaluate the answer honestly. If the person cannot articulate a specific, unique contribution—whether property management expertise, financing access, operational skills, or an investor mindset—they are not the right partner. This single question filters out people who want the rewards of partnership without earning their seat at the table.

### Step 2: Judge by Values and Mental Models, Not Just Personality

Assess a person's values by observing what they already have in life. Someone still renting, with expenses rising and wages falling, is living a different financial reality than you—and that difference reveals their underlying values. A person's current circumstances are the clearest indicator of whether their financial mental model aligns with yours. Do not partner with someone whose daily habits and choices contradict the wealth-building outcomes you seek.

### Step 3: Reject the "Negative Midas Touch" and "Loser's Mentality"

Avoid anyone with a negative Midas touch—meaning everything they touch turns bad regardless of the opportunity. This pattern reveals a loser's mentality, not bad character. Such people will ruin a good property, a good stock, or a good business because their internal framework drives failure no matter the external conditions. Being a hardworking, good-hearted person does not make someone a good partner. A laborer mindset and an investor mindset are not interchangeable—do not confuse the two.

### Step 4: Accept That Good People Can Still Be Bad Partners

Understand the distinction: a bad partner is not necessarily a bad person. People change, circumstances shift, and skills that once fit a business may no longer serve it. If a partner no longer fits the business, you must make a change—for the health of the deal and for both parties. Do not let loyalty to a good person blind you to the damage a bad partnership causes.

### Step 5: Secure the Three Pillars Before Committing to Any Deal

Every successful deal requires three elements: good partners, good financing, and good employees or management. If any one of these pillars is missing, the deal is at risk. Evaluate each pillar independently before moving forward. Strong partners can help you find financing and build a team—but none of these can compensate for the absence of another.

### Step 6: Choose Cash Flow Over Capital Gains as Your Investment Framework

Commit to investing based on cash flow, not on the expectation that property values or stock prices will rise. Cash-flow-based investing is fundamental: you ask whether the asset pays back your loan regardless of market conditions. Capital-gains investing is speculative and relies on market momentum. When the market shifts—as it always eventually does—capital-gains investors lose everything. Cash-flow investors keep their assets and their income.

### Step 7: Evaluate Financing in the Commercial Arena on Cash Flow

Commercial lenders value real estate based on the income it produces, not on its market appreciation. This means you can secure good financing even when residential markets are collapsing—provided the property generates positive cash flow. Understand this distinction and use it. Properties with strong cash flow remain bankable even in downturns, while equity-dependent deals fail.

### Step 8: Look for Properties with Bad Management as Investment Opportunities

Actively seek out properties suffering from poor management. These are often undervalued precisely because other investors see low or no cash flow in year one and walk away. If you can acquire a property with bad management at a fair price and then turn the management around—increasing occupancy, raising rents, cutting waste—you create value that most investors miss. Accept that turning around management can take 18 to 24 months, and plan your investment accordingly.

### Step 9: Trade Up—Do Not Stay Small

Once you have established a working partnership and positive cash flow, move to larger assets. A 30-unit apartment building may be manageable, but it is not economically efficient for a serious investor. Scale up to bigger properties that generate meaningful cash flow and justify professional management. Your partner should share this growth mindset and push you toward the next level rather than comfort in small deals.

### Step 10: Recognize That Bad Deals Lead to Good Partners

Do not fear bad deals—fear the wrong lesson from them. Every bad deal Kiyosaki entered was with a good person, and those experiences were the source of valuable connections, including the partnership with Kenny. Stay open to the relationships that emerge from failed ventures. At the same time, remove yourself from bad partnerships as quickly as possible so you become available for good ones. You cannot attract the right partner while still holding onto the wrong one.

## Examples

### Example 1: Evaluating a Prospective Partner Who Has No Track Record

A person approaches you about investing in rental property together. They are enthusiastic and hardworking but have no portfolio, no property management experience, and no access to financing. Using the "What value do you bring that I don't already have?" test, you discover they have nothing unique to contribute. Partnering with them would cost you time and opportunity without adding leverage. The correct decision is to decline and continue looking for someone whose skills fill your gaps.

### Example 2: Buying a 24-Unit Property with Poor Management

You find a 24-unit apartment building where the current management has allowed occupancy to drop to 60 percent and rents to fall below market. Other investors passed because the property shows no cash flow in year one. You recognize that replacing management, raising rents to market, and pushing occupancy to 90 percent would generate strong monthly cash flow within two years. You purchase the property, execute the turnaround plan, and within 22 months the asset is producing positive cash flow that covers the loan and delivers monthly income.

## Best Practices

- Ask "What value do you bring that I don't already have?" before accepting any partnership
- Evaluate a person's current financial situation as a reflection of their values and decision-making
- Reject partners with a negative Midas touch, regardless of how likable they are
- Separate character from partnership fit—good people can still be wrong partners
- Confirm all three pillars exist before signing into a deal: partners, financing, and management
- Base every investment decision on whether the asset covers its loan from cash flow
- Actively look for properties with bad management as undervalued opportunities
- Plan 18 to 24 months to turn around poorly managed properties before expecting positive cash flow
- Trade up to larger assets once your partnership and cash flow are stable
- End bad partnerships quickly so you can make room for good ones
- Do not invest based on market appreciation predictions—cash flow is the only reliable metric
- Treat every bad deal as a potential source of good relationships and lessons

## Keep In Mind

- Being a good, hardworking person does not qualify someone as a business partner
- A laborer mindset and an investor mindset are fundamentally incompatible in a partnership
- Poor people tend to focus on capital gains; rich people focus on cash flow—know which camp your potential partner is in
- The economy does not change the principles of good management, good financing, and good partners—only the context in which they operate
- Middle-class investors who bought bigger homes during boom times suffered when the market turned; do not repeat their mistake
- Property values decline when momentum shifts, but cash-flowing assets retain their value to investors and lenders

## Security & Safety Notes

- Conduct full due diligence on any potential partner's background, legal history, and existing business relationships before entering a deal
- Do not rely on personal impressions or charm as a substitute for verified track record and documented skills
- If a prospective partner cannot articulate specific value they bring, decline the partnership
- Formalize all partnership agreements in writing with clear roles, expectations, and exit terms
- Never partner with someone whose financial circumstances suggest they are seeking a rescue rather than offering leverage

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Partnering with someone because they are a good person rather than because they add unique value
  **Solution:** Always run the "What value do you bring that I don't already have?" test and require a concrete answer
- **Problem:** Investing based on market appreciation or the belief that property values will keep rising
  **Solution:** Base every investment decision on whether the asset generates positive cash flow that covers its debt
- **Problem:** Holding onto a bad partner out of loyalty or discomfort with confrontation
  **Solution:** Remove bad partners decisively—you cannot attract a good partner while a bad one remains in the deal
- **Problem:** Skipping the management pillar because a deal has strong partners and financing
  **Solution:** Evaluate all three pillars independently; a deal fails if any one pillar is missing
- **Problem:** Passing on properties with weak cash flow in year one without a turnaround plan
  **Solution:** Identify the management problems, model a realistic turnaround timeline of 18 to 24 months, and act if the upside justifies the effort
