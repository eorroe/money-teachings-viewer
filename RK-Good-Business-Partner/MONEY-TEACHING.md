# How to Find a GOOD Business Partner

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills Robert Kiyosaki's principles for selecting, evaluating, and maintaining the right business partners. It explains why people choose partners poorly—confusing character with partnership fit—and provides a framework based on the question "What value do you bring that I don't already have?" for assessing whether someone adds value to your deal. It also covers the three non-negotiable pillars of any successful business venture: partners who bring unique value, financing that covers the loan from cash flow, and management that maintains occupancy and collects rents at or above market rates.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When someone approaches you to become a partner
- When evaluating whether an existing partner can still contribute property management expertise, financing access, operational skills, or an investor mindset
- When entering a real estate deal or any business venture
- When you have previously chosen a partner based on personality rather than skills
- When deciding whether to remove a partner who is no longer a partner whose skills and financial mental model align with the deal requirements

## Steps

### Step 1: Ask the One Question Every Potential Partner Must Answer

Before agreeing to any partnership, ask: "What value do you bring that I don't already have?" Evaluate the answer honestly. If the person cannot articulate a specific skill, resource, or perspective that the other partner does not already provide—whether property management expertise, financing access, operational skills, or an investor mindset—they are not a partner with an investor mindset. This single question filters out people who want the rewards of partnership without earning their seat at the table.

### Step 2: Judge by Values and Mental Models, Not Just Personality

Assess a person's values by observing what they already have in life. Someone still renting, with expenses increasing while earned income declines, is living a different financial reality than you—and that difference reveals their underlying values. A person's current circumstances indicate whether their financial mental model aligns with yours. Do not partner with someone whose daily habits and choices contradict the outcomes you seek.

### Step 3: Reject Partners with a Laborer Mindset

Avoid anyone with a pattern of poor outcomes across investments regardless of opportunity quality—meaning a laborer mindset focused on wages and expenses increasing over time rather than cash flow. This pattern reveals a financial mental model that drives poor outcomes no matter the external conditions. Being a hardworking, good-hearted person does not make someone a good partner. A laborer mindset focused on wages and an investor mindset focused on cash flow are not interchangeable—do not confuse the two.

### Step 4: Accept That Partners with Different Financial Mental Models May Not Fit

Understand the distinction: a partner with a laborer mindset is not necessarily a bad person. People change, circumstances shift, and skills such as property management or financing access that once fit a business may no longer serve it. If a partner is no longer a partner whose expertise matches the needs of the specific deal, you must make a change—for the deal's ability to generate cash flow and operate efficiently and for both parties. Do not let loyalty to a good person blind you to the damage a partnership with a laborer mindset causes.

### Step 5: Secure the Three Pillars Before Committing to Any Deal

In Kiyosaki's framework, every successful deal requires three elements: partners who bring unique value, financing that covers the loan from cash flow, and management that maintains occupancy and collects rents at or above market rates. If any one of these pillars is missing, the deal faces significant risk of failure. Evaluate each pillar independently before moving forward. Strong partners can help you find financing and build a team—but Kiyosaki teaches that none of these can fully compensate for the absence of another.

### Step 6: Choose Cash Flow Over Capital Gains as Your Investment Framework

Commit to investing based on cash flow, not on the expectation that property values or stock prices will rise. Cash-flow-based investing is the principle that you ask whether the asset pays back your loan; a partner with an investor mindset can remain bankable even in downturns, according to Kiyosaki. Capital-gains investing is dependent on price appreciation rather than cash flow and relies on market momentum. When the market shifts—as market cycles eventually shift—capital-gains investors suffer losses. Cash-flow investors keep their assets and their income.

### Step 7: Evaluate Financing in the Commercial Arena on Cash Flow

Commercial lenders value real estate based on the income it produces, not on its market appreciation. This means you can secure financing even when residential markets are collapsing—provided the property generates positive cash flow. Understand this distinction and use it. Properties with positive cash flow can remain bankable even in downturns, per Kiyosaki's framework, while deals that depend on rising equity rather than cash flow tend to fail when markets decline.

### Step 8: Look for Properties with Poor Management as Investment Opportunities

Actively seek out properties suffering from poor management—management that allows occupancy to drop and rents to fall below market. Kiyosaki teaches that these properties are often undervalued because other investors see low or no cash flow in year one and walk away. If you can acquire a property with poor management at a price that allows the property to generate positive cash flow after management improvements and then turn the management around—increasing occupancy, raising rents, cutting waste—you create value that other investors miss. Accept that turning around management can take 18 to 24 months, and plan your investment accordingly.

### Step 9: Trade Up—Do Not Stay Small

Once you have established a working partnership and positive cash flow, move to properties that generate cash flow and justify management. Your partner should share the willingness to scale from one house to 18 houses and push you toward the next level rather than comfort in single-family houses.

### Step 10: Recognize That Deals with Partners with a Laborer Mindset Can Lead to Partners with an Investor Mindset

Do not fear deals with partners who have a laborer mindset. Kiyosaki describes entering deals with partners who had a laborer mindset alongside partners with an investor mindset, and those experiences were the source of connections with partners like Kenny. Stay open to the relationships that emerge from deals with partners who have a laborer mindset. At the same time, remove yourself from partnerships with people who have a laborer mindset promptly so you become available for partnerships with people who have an investor mindset. Kiyosaki teaches that you cannot attract a partner with an investor mindset while still holding onto a partner with a laborer mindset.

## Examples

### Example 1: Evaluating a Prospective Partner Who Has No Track Record

A person approaches you about investing in rental property together. They are enthusiastic and hardworking but have no portfolio, no property management experience, and no access to financing. Using the "What value do you bring that I don't already have?" test, you discover they cannot articulate property management expertise, financing access, operational skills, or an investor mindset. Partnering with them would cost you time and opportunity without adding leverage. The correct decision is to decline and continue looking for someone whose skills such as property management expertise, financing access, operational skills, or an investor mindset fill your gaps.

### Example 2: Buying a 24-Unit Property with Poor Management

You find a 24-unit apartment building where the current management has allowed occupancy to drop to 60 percent and rents to fall below market. The property shows no cash flow in year one. You recognize that replacing management, raising rents to market, and pushing occupancy to 90 percent would generate positive monthly cash flow within two years. You purchase the property, execute the turnaround plan, and within 22 months the asset is producing positive cash flow that covers the loan and delivers monthly income.

## Best Practices

- Ask "What value do you bring that I don't already have?" before accepting any partnership
- Evaluate a person's current circumstances—such as renting with expenses increasing while earned income declines—as a reflection of their values and decision-making
- Reject partners with a laborer mindset—focused on wages and expenses increasing over time rather than cash flow—regardless of how likable they are
- Separate character from partnership fit—a person with an investor mindset can still be the wrong partner if they lack property management expertise, financing access, or operational skills
- Confirm all three pillars exist before signing into a deal: partners, financing, and management
- Base every investment decision on whether the asset covers its loan from cash flow
- Actively look for properties with poor management—management that allows occupancy to drop and rents to fall below market—as undervalued opportunities
- Plan 18 to 24 months to turn around poorly managed properties before expecting positive cash flow
- Trade up to properties with at least 18 units once your partnership and cash flow are stable
- End partnerships with people who have a laborer mindset quickly so you can make room for partnerships with people who have an investor mindset
- Do not invest based on market appreciation predictions—cash flow is the only reliable metric
- Treat every deal with a partner who has a laborer mindset as a potential source of connections with experienced partners and lessons

## Keep In Mind

- Being a hardworking person with a laborer mindset does not qualify someone as a business partner
- A laborer mindset and an investor mindset are fundamentally incompatible in Kiyosaki's framework in a partnership
- In Kiyosaki's framework, people with a laborer mindset tend to focus on capital gains while people with an investor mindset focus on cash flow—know which camp your potential partner is in
- The economy does not change the principles of partners who bring unique value, financing that covers the loan from cash flow, and management that maintains occupancy and collects rents at or above market rates—only the context in which they operate
- Investors who bought bigger homes as investments often lost money when the market turned, according to Kiyosaki; do not repeat their mistake
- Property values decline when momentum shifts, but cash-flowing assets with at least 18 units tend to retain their value to investors and lenders, per Kiyosaki

## Security & Safety Notes

- Conduct due diligence on any potential partner's track record in property management, financing history, and operational experience before entering a deal
- Do not rely on personal impressions or charm as a substitute for a portfolio of properties they have managed and cash flow they have generated
- If a prospective partner cannot articulate property management expertise, financing access, operational skills, or an investor mindset they bring, decline the partnership
- Formalize all partnership agreements in writing with written roles, expectations, and exit terms
- Never partner with someone whose rental situation, expenses increasing while earned income declines, suggest they are seeking a rescue rather than bringing property management expertise, financing access, operational skills, or an investor mindset

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Partnering with someone because they are a hardworking, good-hearted person rather than because they add property management expertise, financing access, operational skills, or an investor mindset
  **Solution:** Always run the "What value do you bring that I don't already have?" test and require an answer of property management expertise, financing access, operational skills, or an investor mindset
- **Problem:** Investing based on market appreciation or the belief that property values will keep rising
  **Solution:** Base every investment decision on whether the asset generates positive cash flow that covers its debt
- **Problem:** Holding onto a partner with a laborer mindset out of loyalty or discomfort with confrontation
  **Solution:** Remove partners with a laborer mindset decisively—Kiyosaki teaches that you cannot attract a partner with an investor mindset while a partner with a laborer mindset remains in the deal
- **Problem:** Skipping the management pillar because a deal has partners who bring unique value and financing that covers the loan from cash flow
  **Solution:** Evaluate all three pillars independently; if any one pillar is missing, the deal faces significant risk of failure
- **Problem:** Passing on properties with no cash flow in year one without a turnaround plan
  **Solution:** Identify the management problems, model a turnaround timeline of 18 to 24 months, and act if the positive cash flow that covers the loan and delivers monthly income justifies the effort
