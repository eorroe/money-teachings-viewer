# The Rich Don't Work for Money: Reject the Paycheck Path

## Overview

Robert Kiyosaki teaches that the traditional path of going to school, getting a job, and working for a paycheck can keep people trapped in a cycle of dependency. Financial education enables you to stop working for money: instead of working for money, you must structure your life so that money and cash-flowing assets work for you. This Money Teaching guides you to build wealth through the four asset classes of businesses, real estate, commodities, and paper assets such as savings accounts, stocks, bonds, mutual funds, and ETFs (exchange-traded funds) while avoiding the trap of earned income dependence.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are tired of spending your entire earned income on bills and living expenses with nothing left to invest
- When you are evaluating whether to accept a traditional full-time job after graduation or a career change
- When you want to understand how the rich legally avoid paying the highest marginal tax rates on income
- When you are deciding which asset classes to focus on building first with your available capital
- When you want to understand why relying on a 401K (employer-sponsored retirement plan), traditional savings account, or government pension as your sole retirement plan creates financial risk
- When you want to learn how to use borrowed money strategically to acquire assets rather than consumer goods

## Steps

### Step 1: Reject the Traditional Path

Stop accepting the default belief that going to school and getting a job is the only path to success. Most schools do not teach financial literacy. The standard path creates dependence on an employer or the government for income, exposes you to the highest tax rates, and offers no protection against job loss. Your first action is to consciously decide that your goal is not to get a job, but to achieve a state where you never need a paycheck again.

### Step 2: Define Your Goal as Financial Independence

Write down a clear, measurable goal: "I will never need a paycheck again, from any employer or the government." This means you are not dependent on an employer, social security, Medicare, or a government pension for survival. This goal changes every subsequent decision you make about education, work, and investing. Write this goal in a place you see it daily, such as a note on your desk or a recurring reminder on your phone. Use it to evaluate every opportunity that comes your way.

### Step 3: Learn to Read a Financial Statement

Understand the difference between an asset and a liability. An asset puts money into your pocket each month. A liability takes money out of your pocket each month. Most people think their house is an asset, but if the mortgage, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and transaction costs total more than any rental income or appreciation it generates, it is a liability. Learn to read the income statement and balance sheet so you can identify what is truly building your wealth versus what is draining it. Study the personal financial statement format used in the Rich Dad books as a starting point. Kiyosaki describes FICO scores as a measure of how well you pay your bills rather than a measure of wealth-building ability, and he emphasizes that bankers want to see financial statements, not FICO scores.

### Step 4: Choose Your Asset Classes to Build

Focus your education and capital on these four asset classes instead of earned income:

- **Businesses**: Start or acquire businesses that generate positive monthly cash flow. If your business has employees, the government provides tax breaks that W-2 (employee) income does not receive. Aim to own a business where you are not required to trade time for money. Kiyosaki states he has multiple businesses and does not work for a paycheck from them.
- **Real Estate**: Buy real estate using borrowed money (mortgage debt). Because you use debt to acquire the property and the asset generates rental cash flow that exceeds expenses, you receive tax advantages including depreciation deductions. This is the opposite of consumer debt, which has no tax benefits and drains monthly cash flow. Kiyosaki identifies debt as the primary reason real estate is valuable to him.
- **Commodities**: Invest in physical commodities such as gold, silver, oil, land, water, and food. Physical ownership of commodities carries tax advantages that paper commodity investments such as commodity ETFs do not. Start with monthly purchases of gold or silver if you do not have enough capital for larger commodity positions. Kiyosaki began buying gold in 1972 and later invested in oil well partnerships.
- **Paper Assets (Selectively)**: Savings accounts, stocks, bonds, mutual funds, ETFs, and 401Ks are labeled "paper" assets. The rich generally avoid relying on these as their primary wealth vehicle because they offer minimal tax advantages compared to the other three asset classes and ordinary income from wages is taxed at the highest marginal rates. Only use paper assets after you have built cash-flowing assets in the other three categories. Kiyosaki states he does not rely on these assets as his primary wealth vehicle.

### Step 5: Use Debt as a Tool, Not a Trap

Consumer debt such as credit card debt keeps average people trapped because it has no tax benefits, carries high interest rates, and drains monthly cash flow. Strategic debt, such as mortgage debt on income-producing real estate, allows you to control a large asset with a small amount of your own capital while receiving tax deductions for interest and depreciation. Learn the difference between bad debt and good debt. Bad debt finances consumption: cars, clothes, vacations, and consumer goods. Good debt finances assets that put money in your pocket each month after all expenses are paid. Use only good debt to acquire assets. Kiyosaki states he is in debt approximately $600 million and uses debt strategically to acquire real estate while paying little in taxes.

### Step 6: Start Investing As Soon As You Have Capital Available

Begin building commodity positions as soon as you have capital available after covering your required living expenses. For example, start buying gold or silver on a monthly schedule, or invest in a partnership that owns physical oil wells. If you cannot afford real estate yet because you lack the down payment, start with commodities. The key is to begin moving your money out of earned income and into assets that generate passive cash flow. Do not wait until you have accumulated a large amount of money to start. Start now with the amount you can invest consistently each month. Kiyosaki began buying gold in 1972 and later invested in oil well partnerships as his first wealth-building activities after leaving school.

### Step 7: Build Your Team Around the Asset Classes

Surround yourself with people who can help you acquire and manage assets in your chosen categories. For example, a licensed real estate agent who is also an investor can help you find investment properties that meet your cash flow criteria. Accountants who understand real estate and business tax law can help you structure your holdings correctly. Mentors who have already achieved financial independence through your chosen asset classes can guide you around common mistakes. Alexandra Gonzalez describes both of her parents as part of her team: her mother is a real estate agent and investor, and her father also supports her investing goals. Your team should include at least one professional for each asset class you are actively building.

### Step 8: Accept Mistakes and Keep Learning

Mistakes are part of the learning process. In a traditional job, mistakes are punished publicly and can get you fired. In wealth building, mistakes teach you what does not work. Admit your mistakes openly to yourself and your team, learn from them, and keep moving forward. Alexandra's employer explicitly encourages admitting mistakes rather than covering them up. Her supervisor told her that covering up a mistake is grounds for termination, so the company's stated policy is to learn from mistakes openly.

## Examples

### Example 1: Alexandra's Experience Working at a Bank

Alexandra Gonzalez worked at a bank and lived paycheck to paycheck without financial education. She could not afford the things she wanted and was stuck in a cycle of paying bills and going to work. After learning the money teachings from Kiyosaki, she now owns properties and says that if she were fired tomorrow, she would have her properties to live off of. She notes that her biggest fear used to be being fired or losing a pension, like her parents experienced before they adopted this philosophy.

### Example 2: Kiyosaki's Early Career After Graduation

Kiyosaki graduated in 1969. He and his classmates were the highest-paid graduates in the world that year, earning approximately $120,000 to $150,000 per year. As young people often do, they spent every penny. Kiyosaki had his rich dad's lesson—"the rich don't work for money"—in his head, so he asked himself what was beyond the secure job. His goal was to never need a paycheck again, from any employer or the government, including Medicare, Social Security, or any government pension. He turned down a military pilot pension because accepting it would mean he needed a paycheck. When he left school, he focused first on commodities, starting with gold in 1972, when it was illegal for Americans to own gold. He then went into real estate to use debt strategically, and started his first real business: a nylon and velcro surfer wallet business that went worldwide before failing. He states that businesses and real estate are what kept him alive when the wallet business failed.

### Example 3: Building Wealth Through Oil Well Partnerships and Commodities

Kiyosaki states that he and his wife own oil wells, not shares of Standard Oil, Chevron, or Exxon. He explains that owning physical oil provides huge tax breaks that owning paper oil (such as oil company stock) does not. When governments seek to attract major employers, they offer incentives because employees pay taxes. Kiyosaki mentions Tesla receiving what he recalls as $50 billion in incentives from the state of Nevada to locate operations there, as an example of how governments compete for employers that bring in employees who pay taxes.

## Best Practices

- Define your goal as never needing a paycheck again, not getting a promotion or raise
- Learn to read a financial statement and distinguish monthly cash-flowing assets from cash-draining liabilities
- Focus your primary wealth-building efforts on businesses, real estate, and commodities before relying on paper assets
- Use good debt (debt used to acquire cash-flowing assets with tax advantages) strategically and intentionally
- Start investing as soon as you have capital available after covering required living expenses
- Build a team of mentors and professionals who understand the specific tax and legal requirements of your chosen asset classes
- Admit mistakes openly to yourself and your team, and document what you learned from each mistake

## Keep In Mind

- Schools do not teach financial literacy. Do not expect the school system to teach you what you need to know about money. Alexandra's fourth grade teacher told her the school does not teach about money, which Alexandra found unreasonable given that school is supposed to prepare students for life.
- Governments write tax rules in the United States and other countries to incentivize business ownership, real estate investment, and commodity production. If you rely solely on W-2 (employee) earned income, you pay the highest taxes. If you own businesses, real estate, or commodities, you receive tax advantages such as depreciation deductions, interest expense deductions, and capital gains treatment.
- A paycheck creates dependence on an employer or the government. Cash flow from assets you control creates freedom and optionality.
- Your peers who took the traditional path may appear successful, but some are living paycheck to paycheck with high consumer debt and no real security if they lose their job. Kiyosaki observes that people who appear successful by conventional standards may lack genuine financial security.
- Financial education is a lifelong process. This video teaches one core concept. You must study the specifics of taxes, legal structures, and each asset class to implement this successfully.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not put all of your capital into a single asset class or a single investment. Diversify your capital across at least two of the three primary asset classes: businesses, real estate, and commodities.
- Understand the specific tax rules, legal requirements, and licensing requirements for each asset class in your country and state before investing. Consult a qualified tax professional before making major investment decisions.
- When using debt for real estate, ensure the property generates positive cash flow after all expenses, including the mortgage payment, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and property management fees. Negative cash flow drains your wealth every month.
- Student loan debt in the United States cannot be discharged in bankruptcy in most cases. Avoid taking on more student loan debt than you can repay from income from a job you actually want to hold. Kiyosaki states this directly in the transcript.
- Commodity investments such as oil wells carry specific risks including commodity price fluctuations, environmental liability, and regulatory risk. Research thoroughly and consult professionals before investing directly in physical commodities.
- Never invest in something you cannot explain clearly to a friend or family member. If someone pitches you an investment and you cannot explain how it generates cash flow, what the tax treatment is, and what the specific risks are, do not invest.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You get a high-paying job and spend every penny on lifestyle upgrades, believing your salary is the path to wealth.
  **Solution:** Treat your salary as a temporary tool, not your identity or long-term plan. Invest at least 20 percent of every paycheck into cash-flowing assets before spending on lifestyle, housing, or discretionary purchases.

- **Problem:** You believe your primary residence is your largest asset by market value.
  **Solution:** Calculate whether your house puts money into your pocket or takes it out each month after mortgage, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and transaction costs. If it drains cash every month, it is a liability, not an asset.

- **Problem:** You rely on a 401K (employer-sponsored retirement plan), IRA (Individual Retirement Account), or other tax-advantaged retirement account as your primary source of retirement income.
  **Solution:** Build cash-flowing assets outside of government-controlled retirement accounts first. Use 401Ks and IRAs only as a supplement to your primary wealth-building efforts, not as your main source of retirement income. Money in retirement accounts is often locked up until age 59.5 and subject to penalties for early withdrawal.

- **Problem:** You fear all debt because you have been told to get out of debt as fast as possible.
  **Solution:** Understand the difference between bad debt (consumer debt on credit cards, car loans, and personal loans with no tax benefits) and good debt (borrowed money used to acquire cash-flowing real estate or businesses that generate monthly income and provide tax deductions). Use good debt intentionally and calculate the return on your borrowed capital before taking on the debt.

- **Problem:** You wait until you have saved a large amount of money to start investing in assets.
  **Solution:** Start with the amount of capital you can invest consistently each month. Small, consistent investments in commodities or a single real estate investment compound over time. Waiting for a large lump sum delays your progress by years.

- **Problem:** You believe getting a good job with benefits is the definition of success.
  **Solution:** A job can be a useful short-term tool for gaining experience and generating startup capital, but your long-term goal is to make the job optional. Evaluate every job offer against your stated goal of never needing a paycheck again.

## Glossary

**Asset** — Anything that puts money into your pocket on a regular basis. An asset generates cash flow whether you work or not.

**Balance Sheet** — A financial statement that shows what you own (assets) and what you owe (liabilities) at a specific point in time.

**Cash Flow** — The movement of money into or out of your pocket over a given period. Cash flow direction — in or out — determines whether something is an asset or a liability.

**Commodities** — Physical raw materials or agricultural products such as gold, silver, oil, land, water, and food that can be bought and sold.

**ETF (Exchange-Traded Fund)** — An investment fund traded on stock exchanges, similar to mutual funds but bought and sold throughout the trading day like individual stocks.

**FICO Score** — A credit score used by lenders to evaluate creditworthiness. Kiyosaki contrasts reliance on a high FICO score with the ability to manage cash flow, arguing that cash flow management is more critical to financial intelligence than a credit score.

**Financial Statement** — A record (typically an income statement or balance sheet) that maps every item in your financial life into income, expenses, assets, liabilities, and cash flow.

**Good Debt** — Debt used to borrow money for an asset where the monthly income generated by that asset covers the monthly debt payment, covers all monthly expenses, and still puts additional money in your pocket each month.

**Income Statement** — A financial statement that tracks your income and expenses over a period of time, revealing whether you have positive or negative cash flow.

**Liability** — Anything that takes money out of your pocket on a regular basis. A liability drains cash flow regardless of your employment status.

**Paper Assets** — Financial assets such as savings accounts, stocks, bonds, mutual funds, ETFs, and retirement accounts that exist as paper or digital records rather than physical assets.

**Phantom Income** — Money kept by using tax breaks from the government — taxes that you do not have to pay.

**W-2 Income** — Earned income from employment, reported on a W-2 tax form, taxed at the highest marginal rates.
