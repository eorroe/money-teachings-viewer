# How To Start A Business With AI (10 Steps From An AI Billionaire)

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills the step-by-step blueprint that Anton Oska, founder of Lovable, used to build the fastest-growing company in history from zero to $100 million in annual recurring revenue in just 243 days. It covers how to generate a business idea from a pain point, validate demand with a waitlist, raise money, build with AI, price your product, and stay customer-obsessed as you scale. Follow these teachings when starting an AI-powered or software business from scratch with minimal budget.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you want to start a software or AI-powered business without being technical
- When you have a business idea but no money, no marketing experience, and no team
- When you need a proven framework to go from idea to revenue in record time
- When you are building with AI tools and want to know how to price, launch, and grow

## Steps

### Step 1: Start With a Pain Point, Not Just an Idea

Identify a real problem you or others experience in daily life or work. The strongest business ideas come from solving a pain point you personally understand. Write down exactly what frustrates you, what is broken, or what is missing. If you do not have an idea yet, examine your daily routines, work tasks, and common frustrations to identify inefficiencies and ask yourself what you would pay to fix.

### Step 2: Build a Minimal Proof of Concept With AI

Use AI coding tools such as Lovable, Cursor, or similar platforms to build the simplest possible version of your solution fast. You do not need to know how to code. Describe what you want to build to an AI tool and have it generate a working prototype. The goal is not perfection — it is proof that the concept works. Share it publicly on a platform such as Twitter/X or Hacker News to gauge real audience reaction and collect initial feedback.

### Step 3: Launch Early and Solicit Honest Feedback

Put your product in front of real users as soon as it has the core features needed to solve the main problem you identified. Do not wait until it is perfect. Early negative feedback is more valuable than delayed praise because it shows you what to fix before you scale. Treat criticism as data, not as a personal attack on your brand.

### Step 4: Build a Waitlist of Passionate Early Users

Create a waiting list before you launch publicly. A waitlist gives you proof of demand, creates urgency, and provides a pool of early adopters who will help you refine the product. Promote the waitlist through social media, communities, and your personal network. A waitlist of 10,000 people is a powerful asset when approaching investors.

### Step 5: Raise Money by Showing Traction, Not Just a Pitch Deck

The best way to raise early funding is to demonstrate that people want what you are building. Investors will come to you if you have a working product, viral attention on social media or a cultural trend driving awareness, and a waitlist showing demand. Focus first on building something valuable enough that investors notice you, rather than spending months preparing a pitch before you have proof.

### Step 6: Choose Your Co-Founder Based on Complementary Skills and Shared Values

Your co-founder should have the opposite skill set to yours so that you cover more ground together, but you must share the same moral code and long-term vision. Write down in detail what you love doing, what you hate doing, and what kind of person you want to work with. Trust and respect are non-negotiable — if you would not want to be stuck with this person on a desert island, do not partner with them.

### Step 7: Decide Your Market: Niche First, Then Scale

Start by serving a specific, underserved niche before trying to mass-market. Early success in a niche gives you case studies, testimonials, and revenue to fund expansion. Once you have proven product-market fit in a niche, you can broaden your marketing. For a mass-market product, you must reach a much higher quality bar before launching publicly.

### Step 8: Price From Day One to Validate Real Demand

Charge money from the beginning, even if the initial price is low. People paying is the strongest signal that your product is valuable enough to keep. If no one will pay, you need to create more value, not lower the price further. Iterate on pricing as your product and AI costs evolve — do not lock into one model forever.

### Step 9: Identify and Serve Your Non-Price-Sensitive Customers

Not all users are equal. Identify the customers who get the most value from your product and are least sensitive to price — these are your primary revenue drivers. Be generous with free tiers for everyone else, but make sure your paid tier is structured so that your most valuable users happily cover the cost of serving the rest.

### Step 10: Stay Customer-Obsessed, Not Competitor-Focused

Do not spend your time tracking what competitors are doing. Instead, obsess over your own customers: what are they building, what problems do they still have, and how can you make them more successful? If you make your customers successful, they will tell others to use your product. Word-of-mouth from happy users is the strongest and cheapest growth engine.

## Examples

### Example 1: Building a No-Code SaaS Tool for Non-Technical Founders

Anton Oska built Lovable by first creating a simple AI tool that generated a Snake game, posting it on Twitter, and watching it go viral. He then turned that proof of concept into a full product aimed at non-technical founders who wanted to build software without hiring developers. He used a waitlist to gather 10,000+ early signups, raised funding once investors came to him, and launched publicly with a paid tier while keeping a free version available.

### Example 2: Launching a Personal Brand or Content Business With AI

If you want to build a content or education business, start by recording your knowledge or experience and use AI to turn it into a workshop, course outline, or script in minutes. Post clips publicly, collect feedback, and refine your offer based on what resonates. Price your first offer at a level that filters out tire-kickers but is accessible to your target audience. Scale only after you have paying customers who recommend you to others.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Start with a pain point you personally understand — empathy beats market research
- ✅ Build the smallest version that proves the concept, then improve based on real feedback
- ✅ Launch early; perfectionism is the enemy of progress
- ✅ Create a waitlist before you publicly launch to validate demand and build leverage
- ✅ Charge from day one to confirm real product-market fit
- ✅ Pick a co-founder who complements your skills and shares your moral code
- ✅ Identify the customers who are least price-sensitive and design your pricing around them
- ✅ Focus on your customers, not your competitors
- ✅ Reinvest early profits into the product rather than taking a large salary
- ✅ Use AI to compress the time between idea and launch dramatically

## Keep In Mind

- A great idea with no execution is worthless — executing and testing beats spending months planning without building
- Your first version will be rough and unpolished; that is expected and fixable
- Investors prefer founders who have already shown they can build and attract attention
- A niche audience of passionate users is more valuable than a large audience of indifferent ones
- Pricing is a signal, not just a revenue tool — the wrong price can mislead you about product-market fit
- The goal of business is freedom, not endless revenue; know what "enough" looks like for you

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not share sensitive API keys, credentials, or infrastructure details when seeking feedback publicly
- Validate user feedback carefully — early users may have unique needs that do not represent the broader market
- When using AI tools to build software, review the generated code for security vulnerabilities before deploying
- Keep customer data separate from development environments and follow data protection regulations
- Be cautious when sharing financial metrics or revenue numbers publicly, especially before fundraising

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Waiting until the product is perfect before launching.
  **Solution:** Launch the minimum viable version immediately and improve based on real user feedback. Perfection is a moving target and delays are costly.
- **Problem:** Chasing investors before proving that customers will pay.
  **Solution:** Build a waitlist, get early paying users, and let investors come to you. Traction beats pitch decks every time.
- **Problem:** Pricing too low and losing money on every user.
  **Solution:** Set prices based on the value delivered to your most committed users, not on what you think the market will bear. Raise prices as you add value.
- **Problem:** Partnering with someone based only on skills, ignoring shared values.
  **Solution:** Test your co-founder's moral compass with hypothetical scenarios before formalizing the partnership. Misaligned values destroy businesses.
- **Problem:** Spreading attention across too many markets or user types at once.
  **Solution:** Dominate one niche first. Broad markets require higher quality and more resources than you have in the early days.
- **Problem:** Obsessing over competitors instead of customers.
  **Solution:** Track what your most successful customers are building and what they still need. If you solve their next problem, you will win regardless of what competitors do.
