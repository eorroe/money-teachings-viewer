# How to Get Rich In The Next Market Crash

## Overview

This Money Teaching shows how to prepare for and profit from a market crash by adopting a contrarian investor mindset, separating personal housing from real estate investment, and focusing on cash flow rather than short-term price appreciation. It explains why people lose money during downturns—they mistake their home for an asset, chase capital gains, or lack the financial education to act when opportunities arise. The goal is to train you to recognize that a crash is an entry point, not a disaster, and to position yourself with income-producing assets while others panic.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When the stock market or real estate market is declining and others are selling assets due to fear
- When you are evaluating whether your home counts as part of your net worth
- When deciding between flipping properties for capital gains versus holding for cash flow
- When you want to understand macro trends—population growth and currency devaluation—that affect long-term real estate values
- When you need a reminder that no external authority will save your finances; you must act yourself

## Steps

### Step 1: Adopt a Contrarian Mindset

Buy when others are selling. As Warren Buffett states, purchase assets when bears are selling and continue buying until bears finally give up. Do not wait for the bottom—start entering the market during the pessimism phase. During the 1987 crash, the author dropped to his knees in gratitude when he learned the market had crashed because he recognized it as an opportunity, not a loss.

### Step 2: Distinguish Your Home From a Real Estate Investment

Your primary residence is a liability, not an asset. It costs money every month in mortgage payments, maintenance, and taxes. The author's own house costs approximately $7,000 per month in mortgage payments, maintenance, property taxes, and insurance. A house is housing—a necessity like food or clothing. Real estate investment is an asset bought specifically to generate cash flow. Do not confuse the two. If your home price declines, you never had net worth from it; you only had a liability losing value.

### Step 3: Focus on Cash Flow, Not Capital Gains

Invest for monthly cash flow, not for the property's resale value. Flipping—buying low and selling high—is capital gains, not investing. The author's company is Cash Flow because that is the objective. If you must sell, use a 1031 tax-deferred exchange to move into a larger, more efficient property rather than cashing out. At 150 units and above, economies of scale in management make larger properties more profitable.

### Step 4: Study Macro Trends That Drive Values

Two major trends favor real estate investors over the long term. First, the U.S. population continues to grow—from 150 million in 1950 to 300 million in 2006 to a projected 350 million in coming years—and population growth creates sustained housing demand. Second, the dollar is declining because of government deficit spending. When the dollar falls, real estate prices in dollar terms appear to rise. Rising rents and replacement costs benefit cash-flow investors, not people simply watching their personal home's market value fluctuate.

### Step 5: Stop Relying on Others and Educate Yourself

The author states that people who lack financial education lose money in stocks, bonds, mutual funds, and real estate alike. You must learn the difference between an asset and a liability, between trading and investing, and between housing and real estate before you commit capital.

### Step 6: Act During the Depth of Pessimism

The greatest wealth is built when sentiment is most negative. In 1989, at the height of pessimism following the 1987 crash, the author decided to get rich. Everyone around him was panicking about the Savings and Loan collapse, the tax reform act, and falling prices. He entered the market while others sold assets in fear. Prepare financially and mentally so that when the next crash arrives, you have the courage and capital to act while others freeze.

## Examples

### Example 1: The 1987 Crash Entry Point

In 1987, while flying from Sydney to Honolulu, the author learned the market had crashed. He thanked God for the crash and began buying when everyone else was selling. The crash wiped out the entire Savings and Loan industry and created a banking-system collapse, but it also presented the entry point he had been waiting for. By acting when sentiment is most negative, he positioned himself to build wealth while others lost everything.

### Example 2: Your House Is a Liability, Not an Asset

When the author said on television in 1997 that your house is not an asset, Wall Street, financial planners, and real estate salespeople attacked him. He clarified: your house is a necessity, similar to a car or food—you need it, but it puts money out of your pocket every month. His own house costs $7,000 monthly. Real estate that puts money into your pocket is an investment asset. When people cry about their home's falling value, they are grieving a liability they never should have counted as net worth.

### Example 3: Macro Trends Favor Cash-Flow Investors

The author points to a USA Today article showing U.S. population growth from 150 million in 1950 to a projected 350 million in coming years. More people means more housing demand. At the same time, the Federal Reserve and successive administrations have printed money and run up deficits, causing the dollar to fall. When the dollar declines, real estate prices in dollar terms rise and replacement costs increase. A cash-flow investor benefits from rising rents; a homeowner merely watches a dollar-denominated number on a Zillow estimate change.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Buy assets that put money in your pocket every month regardless of market direction
- ✅ Separate your personal residence (liability) from your investment portfolio (asset)
- ✅ Enter or increase positions when sentiment is most negative and prices are at their lowest prices
- ✅ Use 1031 exchanges to defer taxes and scale into larger cash-flow properties
- ✅ Study macro trends—population growth, debt levels, and currency strength—before investing
- ✅ Read Rich Dad Poor Dad and continue financial education before committing capital

## Keep In Mind

- Real estate investment means buying for cash flow; your personal home is housing, not an investment
- When property values rise, the dollar is usually falling—do not confuse nominal price increases with real gains
- Hope is for hopeless people; take physical and financial action instead of waiting for rescue
- In Kiyosaki's framework, if you cannot make a deal work at current rates, the problem is your education, not the market

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never invest based on television advice, media excitement or social trends, without understanding the difference between an asset and a liability
- Do not trust anyone who tells you your primary residence is an investment; verify cash flow yourself
- Beware of financial advisors who sell mutual funds as diversification—mutual funds place your money with a manager rather than teaching you to evaluate investments yourself.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Counting your primary residence as an asset and including it in net worth calculations
  **Solution:** Remove your home from net worth calculations immediately. If it costs you money monthly, it is a liability. Track only income-producing assets.
- **Problem:** Buying real estate expecting appreciation instead of monthly cash flow
  **Solution:** Run the numbers before buying. If the property does not produce positive cash flow after all expenses, do not buy it.
- **Problem:** Waiting until the market bottom to buy, or avoiding purchases entirely during a crash
  **Solution:** Start buying during the pessimism phase. Warren Buffett buys when bears are selling, not when they have given up.
- **Problem:** Flipping properties for capital gains instead of holding for cash flow
  **Solution:** Flip only to move up into a larger, more efficient cash-flow property via a 1031 exchange. Short-term trading is speculation, not investing.
- **Problem:** Believing government officials who claim there is no inflation while the dollar falls and prices rise
  **Solution:** Watch the dollar's value against assets that historically hold value when fiat currencies decline, such as gold or stable foreign currencies. If the dollar buys less, your purchasing power is shrinking regardless of official inflation reports.
