# SMB PROP-DESKS

## Overview

This teaching reveals how proprietary trading desks like SMB Capital classify every trade setup into distinct grades (A+, A, A-, B, C) and match risk allocation to each grade. The core insight is that most traders risk the same amount on every trade, which leaves money on the table for high-quality setups and exposes capital to unnecessary drawdowns on low-quality ones. By implementing a graded risk system tied to daily, weekly, and monthly limits, you can scale your account safely, protect capital during unfavorable conditions, and concentrate exposure where the edge is highest.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you have a defined playbook and can distinguish between setup qualities
- When you want to grow your account without changing your trading psychology
- When you are overtrading B and C setups that erode monthly P&L
- When you need guard rails to stop compounding losses during bad sessions
- When you are reviewing past trades to improve future grading accuracy

## Steps

### Step 1: Define Your Trade Grades and Corresponding Risk Percentages

Create a risk grading system that maps each trade quality level to a percentage of your maximum allowable daily risk. SMB's model uses A+ at 80%, A at 40%, A- at 20%, and collapses both B and C down to 5% each. You can adjust these percentages to fit your own playbook, but the key is having different risk levels for different trade qualities rather than risking the same amount on every setup.

### Step 2: Set Daily, Weekly, and Monthly Risk Limits

Establish a hard daily stop (for example, $1,000 maximum loss per day). This limit acts as a guard rail to prevent you from pounding your head against the wall during unfavorable market conditions. When you hit your daily loss limit, stop trading and return tomorrow. This limit also serves as the scaling instrument: when you are ready to risk more, simply raise the maximum daily risk amount while keeping the same percentage allocations.

### Step 3: Grade Each Setup Before Entering

Before entering any trade, assess its quality relative to your playbook criteria. Use mental cues such as "trade of the quarter" for A+, "trade of the month" for A, "core trade of the week" for A-, "situational filler" for B, and "experimental" for C. Consider opportunity profile, gut feel, checks in favor, catalyst strength, and market environment. Do not rely on a rigid formula; grading is a blend of quantified criteria and experienced intuition.

### Step 4: Adopt the Right Trader Persona for Each Grade

Once a trade is graded, shift into the corresponding trader mindset. The A+ trader is offensive, expressive, willing to take multiple shots, and comfortable with active resilience. The A- trader is selective but consistent. The B trader is defensive, patient, and picky, only taking the best B setups. The C trader is experimental and strictly limited. If you are assigned a B grade, you must behave like the B trader even if you have recently experienced success or increased your account size.

### Step 5: Execute With Grade-Appropriate Sizing and Tactics

Enter the trade using a position size that reflects its grade percentage of your max daily risk. For an A+ trade, you might build the position over multiple entries, buy dips to VWAP, use a TWAP program, and hold for a swing. For a B trade, you would wait for a very specific pattern, avoid pre-market or open entries, and keep the position small. Do not apply A+ tactics to a B-grade setup.

### Step 6: Track Running Stats Per Grade

Keep detailed records of win rate, misgrades, and running P&L for each grade. Review these stats monthly. If your B-trader stats show a consistently low win rate and no edge, respect that data and remain defensive. If you discover that B trades are leaking P&L despite profitable A and A+ months, impose a monthly cap on the number of B trades you take. Use the stats to continuously refine your grading accuracy.

### Step 7: Scale Without Changing Your Process

When you are ready to increase position sizes because of strong performance, do not change your trading approach or psychology. Instead, raise your maximum daily risk limit and continue risking the same percentage of that new limit on each grade. For example, if your daily stop moves from $1,000 to $2,000, an A+ trade is now 80% of $2,000 instead of 80% of $1,000, but mentally you are still risking the same 20% or 80% you always have. This prevents the psychological distortion that comes from thinking you are suddenly trading "bigger."

## Examples

### Example 1: Gold Overextension Short (A+ Trade)

In the episode, gold gapped up massively into a Thursday session with extreme overextension across multiple timeframes, well above moving averages, large ATR extensions, and unfilled gaps. Before the open, the team identified this as a potential A+ opportunity because all checks in favor aligned. As the A+ trader, Garrett used full expression: he bought puts on the open as insurance, built into the swing on dips to VWAP using a TWAP program, and remained willing to get stopped out because the risk-reward was fantastic. The position was sized up to the 80% max risk allocation for the day, and the trade ultimately moved 90%.

### Example 2: FSLY Earnings (A-Grade Trade)

FSLY earnings was graded as an A+ catalyst (huge inflection quarter, acceleration in edge computing theme) but was downgraded to A because the broader market was crashing, introducing extra uncertainty. The team bought on dips versus the low of the day and let the trade prove itself. Even though the catalyst was exceptional, the market environment prevented it from being treated as a pure A+ size, demonstrating how multiple variables shift the final grade.

### Example 3: B Trade Overtrading Warning

A trader who recently increased their account size might see excessive B-grade trades and feel compelled to trade them larger and more frequently because they "graduated." This is a pitfall: the B trader stats still show low win rate and no consistent edge. Taking too many B trades creates "leaks" that cut into profits from A+ and A trades. The fix is to cap B trades monthly and remind yourself that being a bigger trader does not change the fact that the B trader inside you does not have an edge.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Grade every setup before entry using your personalized rubric of opportunity profile, checks in favor, and gut feel
- ✅ Keep daily, weekly, and monthly risk limits as guard rails and scaling mechanisms
- ✅ Treat passing on a trade as a valid decision that risks zero allocation
- ✅ Maintain separate running P&L stats for each grade and review them monthly
- ✅ Keep your trading goals narrowly focused (for example, "nail my grading") rather than scattering attention across many objectives
- ✅ Scale by raising your max risk limit, not by changing your percentage allocation or psychology
- ✅ Play tight: only take A+, A, and A- trades; avoid B and C trades unless strictly selective
- ✅ Review past parabolic shorts and catalyst plays to calibrate your grading intuition

## Keep In Mind

- The market does not always give you the perfect entry you envision, especially on A+ scenarios. Be willing to express the trade in multiple ways rather than waiting for one ideal spot.
- Your emotional state and recent performance can distort grading. A hot streak may make B trades feel more appealing than they are.
- B trades appear every day; A+ trades are rare. Plan your frequency around this reality.
- Being "graduated" or trading larger capital does not change the statistical edge of each grade.
- If you cannot yet distinguish between grades, risk the same amount on every trade until your playbook and experience allow you to differentiate.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never exceed your daily, weekly, or monthly risk limits regardless of how compelling a setup appears.
- Do not use leverage or position sizing that could produce a loss beyond your predefined max risk.
- If you are using a prop desk or funded account, understand the specific drawdown rules and compliance requirements before implementing this grading system.
- Keep your trading journal and grade stats private; sharing them publicly can reveal strategy details that others could exploit.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Risking the same dollar amount on every trade regardless of grade.
  **Solution:** Implement the percentage-of-max-risk model so that A+ trades get 80% allocation while B and C trades each get only 5%.

- **Problem:** Changing trading psychology when scaling up, leading to overtrading or oversizing B setups.
  **Solution:** Keep the same percentage of max risk in your mind. If you were risking 20% before, tell yourself you are still risking 20% even though the dollar amount is larger.

- **Problem:** Paralysis from thinking you must find the perfect 80% entry for an A+ trade.
  **Solution:** Focus on bringing your A+ trader to the table. Let the trade develop, scale in as price action confirms, and avoid fixating on a single magic entry.

- **Problem:** Taking too many B trades because they feel familiar or "could be something."
  **Solution:** Impose a monthly cap on B trades. Trust your stats: B trades do not make up bulk P&L and are the primary source of leaks.

- **Problem:** Misgrading due to recency bias or emotional attachment to a catalyst.
  **Solution:** Compare the current setup against past examples with similar variables. Review your misgrades at the end of each month and adjust your criteria accordingly.

- **Problem:** Using a black-and-white formula that ignores nuance.
  **Solution:** Remember that grading is playbook-specific and requires feel. A trade might have an A+ catalyst but a B market environment, landing it at A-. Stay flexible.
