# SMB PROP-DESKS

## Overview

This teaching reveals how proprietary trading desks like SMB Capital classify every trade setup into distinct grades (A+, A, A-, B, C) and match risk allocation to each grade. The core insight is that traders who risk the same amount on every trade find that losses on B and C setups can offset gains from A+ and A setups. By implementing a graded risk system tied to daily, weekly, and monthly limits, you can scale your account by raising your maximum daily risk limit, guard against compounding losses when you are not seeing the market clearly, and concentrate risk exposure on A+ and A setups.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you have a defined playbook and can identify the variables that make one setup higher-quality than another
- When you want to grow your account without changing your decision-making approach
- When you are taking too many B and C setups that erode monthly P&L
- When you need guard rails to stop compounding losses during sessions where you are not seeing the market clearly
- When you are reviewing past trades to improve future grading accuracy

## Steps

### Step 1: Define Your Trade Grades and Corresponding Risk Percentages

Create a risk grading system that maps each trade quality level to a percentage of your maximum allowable daily risk. SMB's model uses A+ at 80%, A at 40%, A- at 20%, and collapses both B and C down to 5% each. You can adjust these percentages to fit your own playbook, but the key is having different risk levels for different trade qualities rather than risking the same amount on every setup.

### Step 2: Set Daily, Weekly, and Monthly Risk Limits

Establish a hard daily stop (for example, $1,000 maximum loss per day). This limit acts as a guard rail to prevent you from pounding your head against the wall during sessions where you are not seeing the market clearly. When you hit your daily loss limit, stop trading and return tomorrow. This limit also serves as the scaling instrument: when you are ready to risk more, simply raise the maximum daily risk amount while keeping the same percentage allocations.

### Step 3: Grade Each Setup Before Entering

Before entering any trade, assess its quality relative to your playbook criteria. Use mental cues such as "trade of the quarter" for A+, "trade of the month" for A, "high quality core trade of the week" for A-, "situational filler" for B, and "experimental" for C. Consider opportunity profile, gut feel, positive factors supporting the setup, catalyst strength, and market environment. Do not rely on a rigid formula; grading is a blend of quantified criteria and experienced intuition.

### Step 4: Adopt the Right Trader Persona for Each Grade

Once a trade is graded, shift into the corresponding trader mindset. The A+ trader is offensive, expressive, willing to take multiple shots, and comfortable with active resilience. The A trader is willing to get stopped out if all checks in favor are present, and is willing to swing the trade for a bigger move. A- trades have undisputable edge. They are not as special as A or A+ trades, but they still carry edge. The B trader is patient and picky, only taking the best B setups and protecting risk rather than going willy-nilly. The C trader is experimental and strictly limited, used for working on new plays. If you are assigned a B grade, you must behave like the B trader even if you have experienced success or increased your account size.

### Step 5: Execute With Grade-Appropriate Sizing and Tactics

Enter the trade using a position size that reflects its grade percentage of your max daily risk. For an A+ trade, express it freely—buy puts on the open as insurance, build into the swing trade on dips to VWAP, use a TWAP program, give yourself multiple shots, and be willing to get stopped out. For a B trade, wait for a very specific pattern, avoid pre-market or open entries, and keep the position small. Do not apply A+ tactics to a B-grade setup.

### Step 6: Track Running Stats Per Grade

Keep detailed records of win rate, misrades, and running P&L for each grade. Review these stats monthly. As Tim Beldin notes, each grade is like a different version of you—the A+ trader Tim, the A trader Tim, the A- trader Tim, the B trader Tim. If your B-trader stats show a super low win rate and no consistently profitable edge (the B trader would have been fired long ago), respect that data and remain defensive. If you discover that B trades are generating losses despite profitable A and A+ months, impose a monthly cap on the number of B trades you take. Use the stats to continuously refine your grading accuracy.

### Step 7: Scale Without Changing Your Process

When you are ready to increase position sizes because of strong performance, do not change your trading approach or psychology. Instead, raise your maximum daily risk limit and continue risking the same percentage of that new limit on each grade. For example, if your daily stop moves from $1,000 to $2,000, an A+ trade is now 80% of $2,000 instead of 80% of $1,000, but mentally you are still risking the same percentage of your maximum daily risk that you always have. This prevents the subtle shift in your thinking that comes from thinking you are suddenly trading "bigger."

## Examples

### Example 1: Gold Overextension Short (A+ Trade)

In episode 10 of the Trading Floor Podcast, gold gapped up massively into a Thursday session with extreme overextension across multiple timeframes—above the Kelners on all four timeframes, large ATR extensions from the five-day low, well above moving averages, and unfilled gaps. SMB's team identified this as an overextension short setup and a potential A+ opportunity because all positive factors supporting the setup aligned. As the A+ trader, Garrett Dryen used full expression: he bought puts on the open as insurance, built into the swing trade on dips to VWAP using a TWAP program, and remained willing to get stopped out because the risk-reward was fantastic.

### Example 2: FSLY Earnings (A-Grade Trade)

FSLY earnings was graded as an A+ catalyst (huge inflection quarter, acceleration in edge computing theme) but was downgraded to A because the broader market was crashing. SMB's team bought on dips to VWAP using a TWAP program and let price action confirm the thesis.

### Example 3: B Trade Overtrading Warning

A trader who increased their account size might see too many B-grade trades and feel compelled to trade them larger and more frequently because they believe they have "graduated." This is a pitfall: the B trader stats still show low win rate and no consistently profitable edge. Taking too many B trades cuts into gains from A+ and A trades. The fix is to cap B trades monthly and remind yourself that being a bigger trader does not change the fact that the B trader inside you does not have an edge.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Grade every setup before entry using your personalized rubric of opportunity profile, positive factors supporting the setup, and gut feel
- ✅ Keep daily, weekly, and monthly risk limits as guard rails and scaling mechanisms
- ✅ Treat passing on a trade as a valid decision that risks zero allocation
- ✅ Maintain separate running P&L stats for each grade and review them monthly
- ✅ Keep your trading goals narrowly focused (for example, "nail my grading") rather than scattering attention across many objectives
- ✅ Scale by raising your max risk limit, not by changing your percentage allocation or psychology
- ✅ Play tight: only take A+, A, and A- trades; avoid B and C trades unless they are your best setups
- ✅ Review past parabolic shorts and catalyst plays to calibrate your grading intuition
- ✅ Cap B trades monthly to avoid leaks—B trades don't make up monthly P&L but can cut into gains from A+ and A trades if overtraded
- ✅ Aim for a triangle-shaped trade frequency where A- trades form the center, A+ and A trades are less frequent but high-conviction, and B trades are the most selective

## Keep In Mind

- The market does not always give you the perfect entry you envision, especially on A+ scenarios. Be willing to express the trade in multiple ways rather than waiting for one ideal spot.
- Your emotional state can distort grading.
- B trades appear every day; A+ trades are rare. Plan your frequency around this reality.
- Being "graduated" or trading larger capital does not change the statistical edge of each grade.
- If you cannot yet distinguish between grades, risk the same amount on every trade until your playbook and experience allow you to differentiate.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never exceed your daily, weekly, or monthly risk limits regardless of the setup's apparent strength.
- Do not use leverage or position sizing that could produce a loss beyond your predefined max risk.
- If you are using a prop desk or funded account, understand the specific drawdown rules and compliance requirements before implementing this grading system.
- Keep your trading journal and grade stats private; sharing them publicly can reveal strategy details that others could exploit.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Risking the same dollar amount on every trade regardless of grade.
  **Solution:** Implement the percentage-of-max-risk model so that A+ trades get 80% allocation while B and C trades each get only 5%.

- **Problem:** Changing your decision-making approach when scaling up, leading to taking too many or oversizing B setups.
  **Solution:** Keep the same percentage of max risk in your mind. If you were risking 20% before, tell yourself you are still risking 20% even though the dollar amount is larger.

- **Problem:** Paralysis from thinking you must find the perfect 80% entry for an A+ trade.
  **Solution:** Trade with the mindset and tactics appropriate for an A+ grade. Let the trade develop, scale in as price action confirms, and avoid fixating on a single idealized entry point.

- **Problem:** Taking too many B trades because they feel familiar or "could be something."
  **Solution:** Impose a monthly cap on B trades. Trust your stats: B trades do not make up monthly P&L but can cut into gains from A+ and A trades.

- **Problem:** Misgrading due to recency bias or emotional attachment to a catalyst.
  **Solution:** Compare the current setup against past examples with similar variables. Review your misrades at the end of each month and adjust your criteria accordingly.

- **Problem:** Using a black-and-white formula that ignores nuance.
  **Solution:** Remember that grading depends on your specific playbook and involves judgment based on experience. A trade might have an A+ catalyst but a B market environment, landing it at A. Allow market conditions to influence your final grade.
