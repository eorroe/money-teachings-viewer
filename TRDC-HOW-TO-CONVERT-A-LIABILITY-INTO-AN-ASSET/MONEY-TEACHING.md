# How To Convert A Liability Into An Asset

## Overview

This money teaching teaches you to distinguish between assets and liabilities using the cash flow test rather than traditional accounting definitions. Robert Kiyosaki explains that anything putting money into your pocket qualifies as an asset, while anything taking money out qualifies as a liability — including your personal residence. By applying this framework, you can evaluate every purchase, property, and relationship on your financial statement, then take specific actions to convert liabilities into cash-flowing assets. The goal is to shift your focus from earning more income to building more assets so that money works for you whether you are employed or not.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are deciding whether to buy a house, car, or other major purchase and need to evaluate its actual cash flow impact.
- When you want to understand why your expenses remain high despite a growing income.
- When you are evaluating an existing property, investment, or business to determine if it is an asset or liability.
- When you are planning for long-term financial security and need to assess future obligations such as aging family members or healthcare costs.

## Steps

### Step 1: Learn The Key Terms Of Financial Literacy

Memorize and understand the key terms: income, expense, asset, liability, and cash flow. Kiyosaki also discusses financial statements, including income statements and balance sheets, as the framework for mapping every item in your financial life into these categories. Financial literacy begins with this vocabulary — without it, you cannot evaluate whether something helps or harms your financial position. He also describes FICO scores as a measure of how well you pay your bills rather than a measure of wealth-building ability, and he emphasizes that controlling cash flow is more critical to financial intelligence than having a high FICO score.

### Step 2: Apply The Cash Flow Test To Every Item On Your Financial Statement

For each item you own or are considering buying, ask one question: does cash flow into your pocket or out of your pocket? If cash flows in, it is an asset. If cash flows out, it is a liability. Ignore labels such as "investment property" or "retirement account" until you verify the cash flow direction. A rental property with monthly rent exceeding all expenses is an asset; your personal residence with mortgage, taxes, insurance, and maintenance is a liability.

### Step 3: Evaluate Your Primary Residence As A Liability First

Assume your personal home is a liability until proven otherwise. Calculate all monthly costs: mortgage payment, property taxes, homeowner insurance, maintenance, and repairs. If you have paid off the mortgage, you still have taxes, insurance, and upkeep. Only reclassify it as an asset if you rent out part or all of the property and the rental income exceeds all ownership expenses.

### Step 4: Convert The Liability Into An Asset Through Rental Income

If your personal residence has spare rooms, a basement, or an accessory dwelling unit (a separate self-contained living unit on the property), rent it out. Alternatively, rent the entire property and move to less expensive housing. Ensure rental income covers the mortgage, taxes, insurance, maintenance, and property management fees with positive cash flow remaining. If rental income does not cover all ownership costs, the property remains a liability.

### Step 5: Evaluate Other Common Liabilities Mistaken For Assets

Audit items commonly mislabeled as assets: cars, boats, luxury goods, and even retirement accounts such as 401(k)s and IRAs. Cars and boats cost money each month through payments, fuel, insurance, and maintenance. 401(k)s and IRAs require ongoing contributions and carry the risk of market loss or tax-law changes. If the item does not put money in your pocket every month, classify it as a liability.

### Step 6: Assess Human Liabilities In Your Financial Plan

Include people in your cash flow analysis. Children, aging parents, and family members with chronic health conditions can drain cash flow through childcare, education, and medical expenses. Long-term healthcare costs can potentially reach $20,000 per month. Plan for these obligations by calculating monthly and lifetime costs, then build assets that generate cash flow matching or exceeding those projected costs. Bad financial advisors or business partners who make poor decisions drain cash flow and deplete your financial resources.

### Step 7: Shift Your Focus From Income To Acquiring Assets

Stop measuring financial progress solely by salary increases. Instead, ask "What new assets am I building today?" Invest earned income into cash-flowing assets before raising your lifestyle. Kiyosaki discusses how wealthy individuals leverage their assets to minimize tax burdens compared to those who rely solely on employment income — illustrating that true financial intelligence lies in one's ability to manage cash flow effectively.

### Step 8: Adopt The "How Can I Afford It?" Mindset

When you want something — a bigger house, nicer car, or luxury item — do not say "I can't afford this." Instead, ask "How can I afford this?" Use that question to spark creative solutions: find an investment, start a side business, or acquire a cash-flowing asset that funds the purchase. Asking "How can I afford this?" instead of saying "I can't afford it" is the starting point for building the asset income needed to make the purchase.

## Examples

### Example 1: Converting A Personal Residence Into A Rental Asset

Alexa's mother owned a home that she considered her biggest asset. After applying the cash flow test, she realized the house cost her monthly in mortgage, taxes, insurance, and maintenance with zero income. She later rented out part of the property, and the rental income exceeded all ownership expenses, producing positive cash flow. The same house shifted from liability to asset.

## Best Practices

- Master the key financial literacy terms: income, expense, asset, liability, and cash flow.
- Run the cash flow test on every property, investment, and major purchase before committing money.
- Treat your personal residence as a liability by default; only reclassify it as an asset when rental income exceeds all monthly ownership costs.
- Acquire cash-flowing assets before upgrading your lifestyle.
- Ask "How can I afford this?" instead of "I can't afford this" when evaluating desired purchases.
- Plan for human liabilities — aging parents, healthcare costs, and children — by building asset cash flow that matches or exceeds those projected costs.
- Keep money in the asset column; do not drain assets to fund consumption.

## Keep In Mind

- Assets put money in your pocket whether you work or not; liabilities take money out.
- The same house can be either an asset or a liability depending on cash flow direction.
- A high salary does not equal financial freedom if expenses match or exceed income.
- Financial intelligence is measured by cash flow management ability, not by academic degrees or job titles.
- People can be assets or liabilities on your financial statement; plan accordingly.
- Retirement accounts such as 401(k)s and IRAs may not be true assets because they require ongoing contributions and carry market risk and tax-law change risk.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not rely on a single source of income; diversify across earned income, portfolio income, and passive income from assets.
- Evaluate any financial advisor or partner carefully before entrusting them with your money; bad advisors drain cash flow and deplete your financial resources.
- Understand the tax treatment of each asset class before investing; different assets carry different tax burdens and benefits.
- Be cautious with debt used to acquire liabilities (such as credit card debt for consumption) versus debt used to acquire assets (such as a mortgage on a cash-flowing rental property).
- Review insurance coverage, legal structure, and estate planning to protect your assets from loss.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** You call your personal residence an asset because real estate agents and conventional wisdom say so, even though it drains cash every month.
  **Solution:** Calculate all ownership costs and apply the cash flow test. Rent out unused space or convert the home to an investment property before classifying it as an asset.

- **Problem:** You receive a pay raise and immediately upgrade to a larger home or more expensive car, trapping yourself in higher expenses.
  **Solution:** Use the additional income to buy cash-flowing assets first. Only upgrade your lifestyle after asset income covers the higher costs.

- **Problem:** You assume retirement accounts like 401(k)s and IRAs are guaranteed assets without understanding market risk, contribution requirements, or tax-law changes.
  **Solution:** Research the actual structure and risk profile of each account. Diversify into assets with predictable cash flow, such as rental real estate or dividend-paying businesses, instead of relying solely on market-dependent accounts.

- **Problem:** You fail to plan for the financial cost of aging parents, healthcare, or family obligations, and deplete your savings unexpectedly.
  **Solution:** Calculate potential future monthly costs for family members now. Build an asset column with enough passive income to cover those costs before they arrive.

- **Problem:** You buy things that depreciate in value and require ongoing expenses, believing they are investments.
  **Solution:** Before any purchase, ask whether it generates income or incurs expenses. If it incurs expenses, it is a liability regardless of how it is marketed.

- **Problem:** You say "I can't afford it" when you want something, which stops you from finding creative solutions.
  **Solution:** Replace "I can't afford it" with "How can I afford it?" Use that question to drive asset-building strategies that fund the purchase.

## Glossary

**Accessory Dwelling Unit (ADU)** — A separate self-contained living unit on the same property as a primary residence, such as a converted basement, garage apartment, or backyard cottage, that can be rented out for income.

**Asset** — Anything that puts money into your pocket on a regular basis. An asset generates cash flow whether you work or not.

**Balance Sheet** — A financial statement that shows what you own (assets) and what you owe (liabilities) at a specific point in time.

**Cash Flow** — The movement of money into or out of your pocket over a given period. Cash flow direction — in or out — determines whether something is an asset or a liability.

**Cash Flow Test** — The method of classifying any item as an asset or liability by asking one question: does it put money into your pocket each month, or take money out?

**FICO Score** — A credit score used by lenders to evaluate creditworthiness. Kiyosaki contrasts reliance on a high FICO score with the ability to manage cash flow, arguing that cash flow management is more critical to financial intelligence than a credit score.

**Financial Statement** — A record (typically an income statement or balance sheet) that maps every item in your financial life into income, expenses, assets, liabilities, and cash flow.

**Income Statement** — A financial statement that tracks your income and expenses over a period of time, revealing whether you have positive or negative cash flow.

**Liability** — Anything that takes money out of your pocket on a regular basis. A liability drains cash flow regardless of your employment status.

**Passive Income** — Income earned from assets with little or no ongoing effort required to maintain it, such as rental income from real estate or royalties from intellectual property.

**Portfolio Income** — Income generated from investments such as dividends, interest, and capital gains, as distinct from earned income from employment.
