# They're Lying About Selling... Here's How She Made $1.2M in One Day ｜ Forbes Riley

## Overview

This teaching distills the core sales and communication principles shared by Forbes Riley, known as "The Pitch Queen," into actionable steps. Riley has generated over $2.5 billion in sales throughout her career by mastering the art of getting people to say "yes." The central insight is that most people overtalk, undertalk, or babble their way past the sale. The real skill is getting the other person to want what you already have — without explaining why they need it. Pitch is not about talking; it is about asking, listening, creating curiosity, and structuring an interaction so that the only logical next step is agreement. The same formula works whether you are selling a product, pitching a business, or asking for a favor.

## Steps

### Step 1: Nail the "What Do You Do?" Answer

The first test of your pitch is your answer to "What do you do?" If you cannot answer that in a way that makes the other person lean in and ask for more, you have already lost. A great answer is short, provocative, and focused on what you can do for the listener, not what you do for a living. "I run the largest online business media brand in the world" works because it is brief and raises a question. Practice your answer until it is automatic, confident, and impossible to ignore. Refine it until the listener's next words are "Tell me more."

### Step 2: Shift from "What I Do" to "What I Can Do for You"

Nobody cares what you do when they ask "What do you do?" They care what you can do for them. Reframe your answer around the listener's benefit, not your job title. Instead of describing your role, describe the access, result, or opportunity you offer. This single shift changes the energy of any interaction from a status exchange into a value proposition. It takes the same amount of time to say, but it changes whether the other person leans in or checks their phone.

### Step 3: Build Want Before You Reveal the Solution

Do not lead with the product. Lead with the problem. Make the listener feel the gap between where they are and where they want to be. Only once they feel the pain or the desire do you introduce what you have. Compare this to selling a stain remover: do not show the product first. Ask if they have ever ruined a shirt with spaghetti sauce. Let them say yes. Now they want the solution before you have even named it. Stop telling people what they need. Get them to want what you have.

### Step 4: Use Assumptions to Connect with Your Audience

Before you speak to any audience, make educated assumptions about who they are, what they are feeling, and what problem they are trying to solve. A pitch at 9 a.m. on a Tuesday reaches a very different person than a pitch at 2 a.m. on a Saturday. Assume the audience's context, speak directly to it, and watch engagement rise. Making no assumptions and pitching generically is the fastest way to lose a room. The best pitchers meet their audience exactly where they are.

### Step 5: Use Third-Party Validation

Never sell alone. Bring in a third party — a testimonial, a case study, a customer, or a co-host — to describe the result. The listener needs to hear someone other than you say that the product works. In an infomercial structure, Riley introduces a customer who says, "I used this product and my wife still loves me. She was going to throw out that carpet. $800 I saved." Now the listener trusts the stranger more than they trust the pitcher. Structure your pitch so that by the time you name the price, the listener has already heard someone just like them describe the result.

### Step 6: Create Urgency and Scarcity Without Being Pushy

Give the listener a reason to act now, not later. This is not about fake countdown timers. It is about making the cost of inaction feel real. Riley's formula always includes an offer that expires, a bonus that is only available for a limited window, or a price that goes up. Make the listener feel that walking away means losing something they now know they want. The urgency must feel like a gift to the listener, not pressure from the pitcher.

### Step 7: Ask for the Order and Then Stop Talking

Once the listener says yes, give them more than they expected and then stop explaining. Riley's pitch structure always ends with an overdelivered offer: two for the price of one, free bonuses, or added access. The goal is not just to close — it is to make the buyer feel like they won. When someone feels they got a better deal than they negotiated for, they become a repeat buyer and a referral source. The person who asks for the order gets it. Once you have the yes, stop talking. Overtalking kills more deals than bad pricing.

## Examples

### Example 1: The Pen Pitch That Launched a Career

Riley was 15 when she walked into an audition and was told to sell a pen to a camera. Instead of describing the pen, she told a story about her mother writing her handwritten notes every day in college, and how a pen could reach out and touch someone's heart. She put the pen down. The director said, "You're going to make me a lot of money." She had sold the emotion, not the object. That moment launched a career that would span 1,500 products and over $2.5 billion in sales. The lesson: when you understand the hook, the product almost sells itself.

### Example 2: The Stain Remover Infomercial Structure

Riley demonstrates her pitch formula in real time with a fictional stain remover. First, she sets up the problem: you have a white carpet and you just spilled spaghetti sauce. She does not show the product. She reminds the viewer of the stain and how nothing works to remove it. Then she builds curiosity: "What if I told you I could make it disappear?" Third-party comes in: someone the viewer does not know says the product worked and saved them $800. Then the deal: two for the price of one, and the offer ends at midnight. The viewer has their credit card out before the product is fully revealed. This formula works on any product, every time, because it follows the psychology of how people buy.

### Example 3: 64,000 Units in 24 Hours

Riley once sold 64,000 units in 24 hours — generating $1.2 million in a single day — using the same structure she has used for every product since the 1980s. The formula is repeatable: set up the problem, make them feel the want, validate with a third party, and close with urgency and overdelivery. The product did not matter. The structure did. The same arc works for a $10 fitness tool, a $1,000 program, or a $100,000 consulting engagement. Pitch is a formula, not a talent.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Answer "What do you do?" in 10 words or less — make them ask for more
- ✅ Reframe your answer around what you can do for the listener, not what your job title is
- ✅ Lead with the problem, not the product
- ✅ Make assumptions about your audience's context before you pitch — meet them where they are
- ✅ Use third-party stories and testimonials before you make your case
- ✅ Create real urgency — make inaction feel like a loss
- ✅ Ask for the order directly, then stop talking
- ✅ Overdeliver at the close so the buyer feels like they won
- ✅ Practice your pitch until it is automatic, then refine it based on results
- ❌ Do not overtalk — once they say yes, stop explaining
- ❌ Do not tell people what they need — make them want what you have
- ❌ Do not pitch without knowing your audience's pain first
- ❌ Do not forget that execution matters more than the product idea
- ❌ Do not pitch generically — assume something about your audience and speak directly to it

## Keep In Mind

- Pitch is a skill, not a talent. Riley was not born with it — she was thrown into 1,500 live product pitches without any coaching and learned by watching what worked.
- The person who asks for the order gets it. Riley's most powerful embedded command is simply asking a small, low-stakes question that leads to a sale.
- People buy emotion, not logic. If you can make someone feel the problem viscerally, the price becomes irrelevant.
- Every pitch should follow the same arc: problem → want → proof → urgency → overdeliver. If your pitch does not hit all five steps, you are leaving money on the table.
- People hate to be sold to, but they love to buy. Your job is not to push a product — it is to create a world where buying is the only logical next step.

## Security & Safety Notes

- This is educational content based on a publicly available podcast episode.
- These principles are intended for ethical persuasion, not manipulation. Always sell products or services that genuinely deliver on the promise made in the pitch.
- Do not use urgency or scarcity tactics that are fabricated or misleading. Legal requirements for truth in advertising vary by jurisdiction.
- If you are selling physical products, ensure you have the right to market them and that they meet all relevant safety standards.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Overtalking the product features before establishing the buyer's desire.
  **Solution:** Never mention the product until the listener has said yes to solving the problem you described. Let them ask for it.

- **Problem:** Pitching generically without considering the audience's context.
  **Solution:** Before every pitch, write down one assumption about who you are speaking to and what they care about right now.

- **Problem:** Failing to ask for the order directly.
  **Solution:** Use embedded commands — ask for small commitments that lead to the sale. The person who asks gets the yes.

- **Problem:** Believing you need to be a natural communicator to pitch well.
  **Solution:** Riley was shy, had braces for eight years, and was told she could not be understood for two years. Pitch is a formula you can learn and practice.

- **Problem:** Selling without a repeatable structure.
  **Solution:** Write down your pitch arc before you present. Test it, measure the conversion rate, and refine it. Every pitch should be improvable.
