# Building a Category-Defining Technology Product Through Personal Problem-Solving

## Overview

This guide distills the product and funding principles used by Renji Bijoy to build Immersed VR — now the most widely used VR office application in the world — from a personal frustration with remote work into a category-leading product. Bijoy's journey includes turning down medical school, earning $360,000 per year as a lead software architect by age 23, leaving a stable high-income job to pursue AI research, going through an intense accelerator program as a solo founder, and raising $8 million in crowdfunding in two weeks. The underlying themes are: build what you personally need before seeking market validation, choose business models with recurring revenue potential, use accelerators to fill specific skill gaps, build community before you need to raise money, and time your entry into markets that are going to be large regardless of when they arrive.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When building a technology product in an emerging category where mainstream adoption has not yet arrived
- When deciding whether to pursue a conventional high-income career path or build a startup
- When entering an accelerator program as a solo founder or first-time CEO
- When raising early-stage capital through equity crowdfunding rather than traditional VC
- When choosing between competing technologies or platforms (e.g., gaming-focused VR headsets vs. productivity-focused spatial computing)
- When the user asks about VR/AR startup strategy, solo founder best practices, crowdfunding, or building products in pre-market categories

## Steps

### Step 1: Build a Product for a Problem You Personally Experience

Bijoy started building Immersed in 2017 — three years before COVID-19 made remote work universal — because he personally experienced the isolation and productivity limitations of working from home. He had watched more of his coworkers shift to remote arrangements year over year and could see the trajectory. His guiding principle, borrowed from a Y Combinator talk: if you build a product you personally need, you will not waste time guessing what users want. Before starting any company, identify a specific problem in your own daily workflow or life that no existing product adequately solves.

### Step 2: Validate the Idea With the Cheapest Possible Prototype

Bijoy's first step was not writing code — it was buying a $20 Google Cardboard headset to experience VR at the lowest possible fidelity. The blurry, uncomfortable experience was sufficient to validate that the concept was worth pursuing before he had invested any meaningful time or money. When testing whether a new technology or platform is viable for your idea, invest the minimum possible to answer the core question: is this real? Only proceed to full-scale development after you have confirmed the fundamental premise with the cheapest possible prototype.

### Step 3: Choose Business Models With Recurring Revenue Potential

Bijoy's first job after dropping out of his PhD program was at a web services company he co-founded as a college freshman — a business with recurring monthly contracts, not one-time sales. He absorbed that lesson early: businesses where customers pay you continuously compound in ways one-time transaction businesses do not. When choosing what to build, prioritize models where the customer relationship is sticky by design — subscriptions, retainers, SaaS contracts — rather than models where you must find a new customer for every dollar of revenue.

### Step 4: Use Accelerator Programs to Fill Specific Skill Gaps, Not to Validate Your Idea

Bijoy chose Techstars Chicago over Y Combinator because he had a strong product background but no knowledge of legal, hiring, finance, fundraising, or marketing — the exact skills a business-focused accelerator teaches. He was the only solo founder in his program and one of only 10 companies selected from 10,000 applicants worldwide. The program required 5 a.m. to midnight commitment for 12 weeks, during which he had to run his company on weekends. Choose an accelerator based on the specific skills you lack, not its brand prestige. Be honest with yourself about your gaps before applying.

### Step 5: Build a Growth Squad Before You Need to Raise Money

The engine of Bijoy's crowdfunding success was not the WeFunder platform — it was a pre-built community of 40 committed early supporters for the first campaign (700 by the second campaign) who invested on day one and shared the opportunity across their networks. The day-one investment volume created social proof that triggered a chain reaction of additional investments. Before launching any public fundraising campaign, personally invest time in building a core community of supporters who are committed to your success. They are your first investors, your first customers, and your first evangelists.

### Step 6: Raise Money Through Crowdfunding to Gain Leverage in Traditional Fundraising

Bijoy raised $2 million in 2 days and $8 million in 2 weeks through equity crowdfunding — a pace that traditional VC fundraising almost never achieves. The strategic benefit extends beyond the capital raised: VCs and angels see the public momentum and experience FOMO, which gives the founder leverage in future negotiations. When VCs call, you are no longer the one asking for money — they are competing to invest in something that has already proven market demand. Crowdfunding also distributes ownership across a broad community rather than concentrating it in a small number of investors who may have conflicting incentives.

### Step 7: Time Inevitable Market Shifts — Do Not Wait for Them to Arrive

Bijoy started Immersed in 2017 because he could see that remote work was an inevitable trend, even though it was still unusual at the time. He was three years early — COVID-19 made the trend undeniable — but his early start meant Immersed was already a polished, working product when demand exploded. The same principle applies to spatial computing, AI, and other emerging technology markets. Identify trends that are already visible in early adoption data and start building before they become mainstream. The people who built products during the "trough of disillusionment" in VR (2016–2019) are the ones who won when the market finally matured.

### Step 8: Invest in Leadership Capability Before Product Scale

The best advice Bijoy received from Intel CEO Pat Gelsinger was that his primary job was no longer building the product — it was building the leadership team that would build the product. This shift from individual contributor to team-builder is the hardest and most important transition a founder makes. High-caliber leaders are never looking for jobs — they must be actively recruited. Plan to spend 40% of your senior-leadership time attracting the right people to your team. The product will not scale until the team around it does.

## Examples

### Example 1: The Google Cardboard Prototype That Launched a Category-Leading Company

Bijoy's first investment in VR was $20 for a Google Cardboard headset — the cheapest possible way to experience the medium. The blurry, uncomfortable experience was sufficient to confirm that VR as a concept was worth building for, even though the hardware was not ready for mass adoption. That $20 test preceded three years of building before the product was polished enough to launch on the Oculus store. The principle: test the biggest assumptions with the cheapest possible prototype before committing to full development.

### Example 2: Raising $8 Million in Two Weeks Through Crowdfunding

Bijoy's second crowdfunding campaign grew from a growth squad of 700 people who had invested in the first round. On day one of the campaign, 40 of those people posted comments on social media saying "I'm in," creating visible social proof that triggered a chain reaction. The campaign ultimately raised $8 million in two weeks — a pace that traditional VC fundraising almost never achieves. The tactical lesson: build your investor community before you need them. Every person who invests early becomes a channel for the next round.

### Example 3: The Solo Founder Advantage at Techstars

Bijoy was the only solo founder in a Techstars program of 10 companies, most of whom had co-founders, Ivy League backgrounds, or prior exits. His co-founder had dropped out rather than relocate for the program, leaving Bijoy to run the company alone through an intense 12-week boot camp. The experience forced him to develop judgment, decision-making speed, and operational discipline far faster than founders who could split responsibilities with a partner. Solo founder status is a disadvantage in fundraising psychology but an advantage in personal development velocity.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Build products you personally need — it eliminates the need to guess what users want
- ✅ Test the biggest assumption with the cheapest possible prototype before building at scale
- ✅ Choose business models with recurring revenue — one-time transactions require constant hunting
- ✅ Fill specific skill gaps with accelerator programs, not validate general ideas
- ✅ Build a community of 50 to 100 committed supporters before any public fundraising
- ✅ Use crowdfunding to gain leverage in future VC negotiations, not just to raise capital
- ✅ Time market shifts by identifying trends visible in early data — do not wait for mainstream confirmation
- ✅ Shift your focus from building product to building leadership teams before you think you need to
- ❌ Do not build a product for a market you do not personally inhabit
- ❌ Do not raise capital before proving that the fundamental business model works
- ❌ Do not choose an accelerator based on brand prestige alone — match it to your specific gaps
- ❌ Do not wait for hardware or platform maturity before starting to build — early movers win when the market arrives

## Keep In Mind

- Product-market fit feels like a boulder rolling downhill — the shift is unmistakable when it happens, but getting the boulder to the peak requires sustained effort with no visible momentum
- VR/AR adoption is held back by hardware, not by software quality — the right product at the wrong time is still the right product
- The iPhone displaced the Game Boy not because it was better at games but because it was useful for adults with money
- Every great technology company had a period where it looked like it would never work — the question is whether the founder lasts through that period
- Community capital is more durable than financial capital — investors come and go; a committed user base stays

## Security & Safety Notes

- Equity crowdfunding is subject to securities regulations — consult legal counsel before any public raise
- Do not disclose sensitive valuation, financial, or product roadmap information in public crowdfunding materials beyond what regulations require
- Protect intellectual property before approaching investors — file provisional patents or document trade secrets before any public demo
- Never share investor personal financial data or contact information with third parties without explicit consent

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Building a product for a market trend rather than for a personal problem
  **Solution:** Ask yourself: would I pay full price for this product today, even if no one else ever bought it? If no, you are building a pitch, not a product.

- **Problem:** Waiting for hardware or platform maturity before starting to build
  **Solution:** Start when the trend is visible, not when it is obvious. Early builders have a multi-year head start when the market arrives.

- **Problem:** Raising too much capital too early before proving unit economics
  **Solution:** Bootstrap until you have evidence that customers will pay for the product at a price that covers costs. Capital amplifies problems as much as progress.

- **Problem:** Treating an accelerator program as a finish line rather than a starting point
  **Solution:** Accelerators compress learning into months — they do not eliminate the need for years of execution. The hardest work begins after the program ends.

- **Problem:** Hiring for credentials rather than demonstrated obsession with your problem
  **Solution:** The best early hires are the ones who were already working on your problem before you hired them — find them through community, not job boards.
