# If I Had to Start From Scratch Again, I'd Do This ｜ Vic Keller

## Overview

This money teaching distills Vic Keller's blueprint for starting over, based on his track record of founding 17 companies and selling nine of them, including three to Berkshire Hathaway. The lessons cover how to build durable businesses rather than speculative flips, the power of partnering with people better than you, why boring businesses often create the most wealth, and how to anchor your work to something larger than profit.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When deciding what kind of business to build in a new market or economic environment
- When evaluating whether to build for long-term value or short-term exit
- When seeking a mentor or business partner who can accelerate your learning
- When you feel pressure to chase trendy business models instead of durable ones
- When the user asks about business exits, partnering with mentors, or building recession-proof companies

## Steps

### Step 1: Build for Value, Not for Exit

Keller explicitly states that he never built any of his early companies with the mindset of selling them. The strategy was always about delivering exceptional value to customers. Businesses built with exit as the primary goal tend to cut corners, optimize for optics, and fail to develop the customer loyalty that produces durable cash flow. If you build something genuinely valuable, the exit will take care of itself.

### Step 2: Choose Boring, Durable Businesses

The biggest opportunity is not building a trendy AI wrapper or chasing the next hot sector. It is building a business that customers cannot live without, regardless of the economic cycle. Look for businesses that solve persistent, repetitive problems and have pricing power. If a recession hits and your customers still need your product, you have built something real. If they can easily cancel, you have built a luxury, not a necessity.

### Step 3: Attach Yourself to People Better Than You

Keller's first business partner was a multi-billionaire, which he accessed by showing up, asking questions, and demonstrating seriousness. The fastest way to accelerate your learning is to surround yourself with people who have already solved the problems you are currently facing. You do not need to be the smartest person in the room; you need to be the person who listens the most and contributes the most effort.

### Step 4: Master One Thing Before Diversifying

Keller criticizes shiny object syndrome and the pressure to build multiple income streams before excelling at one. The people who build lasting wealth are usually the ones who go deep on a single problem, build real expertise, and extract meaningful profit before expanding. Diversification before mastery is a distraction that slows real wealth creation.

### Step 5: Anchor Your Business to Something Larger Than Profit

Keller cites Jesus Christ as the foundation of his marriage, family, and business resilience. Whether your anchor is faith, family, community, or a mission, having something non-negotiable at the center of your life makes the entrepreneurial loneliness survivable. The business will test you; if you have a center that does not move, you will stay standing when others fold.

## Examples

### Example 1: Building a Durable Business

A founder builds a commercial janitorial company serving apartment complexes. The contracts renew automatically, the margins are stable, and the service is non-negotiable for property managers. During every economic downturn, the business grows because landlords still need clean buildings. Twenty years later it is sold to a private equity firm for a significant premium.

### Example 2: Partnering With a Mentor

A 24-year-old entrepreneur attends industry events and consistently asks experienced operators thoughtful questions about their biggest operational challenges. One of them invites them to work on a deal as a junior partner. Within three years, the younger founder has learned more from the partnership than they could have learned in a decade alone.

### Example 3: Resisting Shiny Object Syndrome

A profitable e-commerce brand receives inbound interest from investors who want to pivot into crypto, AI, and Web3 simultaneously. The founder declines all of them and reinvests all profits back into the core business, doubling revenue in two years. The brand eventually sells for a much higher valuation because it is stronger, not trendier.

## Best Practices

- Build businesses that solve persistent customer problems, not temporary trends
- Partner with people who have already solved the problems you are facing
- Master one business before expanding into adjacent opportunities
- Anchor your life to something that does not depend on business outcomes
- Avoid shiny object syndrome by evaluating every new idea against your core mission

## Keep In Mind

- Durability beats trendiness in long-term wealth creation
- The right partner is worth more than any amount of capital
- Exit value is a byproduct of customer value

## Security & Safety Notes

- All advice is drawn from a publicly available podcast interview
- Do your own due diligence before making any business or investment decisions
- Consult qualified legal and financial advisors when structuring partnerships or exits

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Building with an exit mindset rather than a value mindset
  **Solution:** Optimize for customer outcomes; the exit will follow

- **Problem:** Chasing trendy sectors instead of durable problems
  **Solution:** Ask whether customers will still need your product in a recession

- **Problem:** Trying to learn everything alone instead of partnering up
  **Solution:** Find mentors and partners who are already where you want to go

- **Problem:** Diversifying before excelling
  **Solution:** Go deep on one business before adding streams of income
