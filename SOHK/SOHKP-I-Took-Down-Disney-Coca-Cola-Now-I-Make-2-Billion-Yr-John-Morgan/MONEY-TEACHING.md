# I Took Down Disney & Coca-Cola... Now I Make $2 Billion/Yr ｜ John Morgan

## Overview

This money teaching distills John Morgan's approach to building Morgan and Morgan into one of the largest personal injury law firms in North America, generating over $2 billion in annual fees. The lessons cover relentless competitiveness, understanding how large organizations actually make decisions, finding luck through intentional action, negotiating from a position of strength, and maintaining a personal philosophy that outlasts any single case or deal.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When competing against larger, better-resourced opponents
- When negotiating with powerful corporations or institutions
- When building a service business at scale
- When you need a philosophy of relentless persistence
- When the user asks about law firm growth, negotiation strategy, or competitive positioning

## Steps

### Step 1: Be Willing to Fight When Others Stop

Most people are satiable; they reach a level of success or comfort and stop pushing. Morgan explicitly rejects that instinct. The firms and opponents he defeated were often larger and more established, but they lacked the willingness to keep fighting. Adopt a mindset where the goal is not just to compete but to outlast. If you are the person still in the ring when everyone else has left, the odds of winning shift dramatically in your favor.

### Step 2: Understand That Large Organizations Respond to Money, Not Ethics

Morgan learned from negotiating with billion-dollar companies that the only language they reliably understand is financial pressure. Appeals to fairness, right and wrong, or public opinion have limited effect on entities optimized for shareholder value. Enter every negotiation knowing the leverage points are financial. Prepare your case, your data, and your alternatives before you sit at the table.

### Step 3: Bring Overwhelming Force to Every Engagement

Morgan's advice on negotiation is direct: do not bring a knife to a gunfight. Bring overwhelming, well-prepared force. That means investing in legal research, expert witnesses, media strategy, and public positioning before the other side even knows a fight is coming. Preparation compounds. The side that has already done the work will always have the advantage over the side that is reacting.

### Step 4: Create Your Own Luck Through Relentless Activity

Morgan argues that luck is not random; it is the byproduct of putting yourself in the way of opportunities. Never eat alone. Show up. Return calls. Take meetings that feel inconvenient. Every additional interaction is a lottery ticket. The more tickets you hold, the more likely you are to win. Humility about your own limitations keeps you open to the luck that others miss because they stopped looking.

### Step 5: Anchor Yourself to a Guiding Philosophy

Morgan's guiding principle is the Golden Rule: treat others as you would want to be treated. A clear, simple philosophy removes decision fatigue and keeps you grounded when success becomes loud. Define your own principle early and test every major decision against it. The businesses and leaders that last are usually the ones built on something non-negotiable.

## Examples

### Example 1: Outlasting a Larger Opponent

A small software company discovers a well-known platform is infringing on its patent. Larger competitors in similar situations often settle quietly. The small company files suit, gathers evidence over 18 months, and builds a public campaign. The larger company settles on favorable terms because the smaller firm was willing to sustain the fight longer than anyone expected.

### Example 2: Negotiating With a Powerful Vendor

A mid-size retailer is locked into a supply contract with price escalators that are destroying margins. Rather than complaining, the leadership team audits every line item, identifies overcharges, builds a legal and financial case, and presents it with a clear alternative supplier ready to step in. The vendor restructures the deal.

### Example 3: Creating Luck Through Activity

A consultant decides to attend one industry event per month, have lunch with one new contact per week, and respond to every inbound email within 24 hours. Within a year, the pipeline is full not because of a single lucky break but because the sheer volume of interactions created multiple unexpected opportunities.

## Best Practices

- Treat every competition as a marathon; stamina beats talent when talent quits
- Enter negotiations with full preparation and clear financial leverage points
- Invest in preparation before the conflict starts, not during it
- Put yourself in front of as many opportunities as possible
- Anchor decisions to a simple, non-negotiable philosophy

## Keep In Mind

- Satiation is the enemy of exceptional results
- Large institutions are not moral actors; they are economic actors
- Luck is manufactured through volume and consistency of effort

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Quitting when the opponent is bigger or better funded
  **Solution:** Outlast rather than outspend; persistence is a competitive advantage

- **Problem:** Appealing to fairness in negotiations with large corporations
  **Solution:** Focus on financial leverage and prepared alternatives

- **Problem:** Waiting for luck to arrive
  **Solution:** Manufacture luck through deliberate, consistent activity and openness to unexpected connections

- **Problem:** Losing philosophical grounding during growth
  **Solution:** Define and regularly revisit your core guiding principle
