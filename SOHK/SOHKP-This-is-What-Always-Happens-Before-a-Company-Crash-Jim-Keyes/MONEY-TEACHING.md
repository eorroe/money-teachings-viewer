# This is What "Always" Happens Before a Company Crash ｜ Jim Keyes

## Overview

This money teaching distills the core business and leadership principles shared by Jim Keyes, former CEO of 7-Eleven and Blockbuster, into actionable steps for building wealth and avoiding the patterns that lead to corporate failure. Keyes' central insight is that company crashes are rarely caused by external market forces alone — they are caused by internal fear, inertia, and a refusal to embrace change. The same principles that help a leader rise through a Fortune 500 company — cultural literacy, relentless positivity, and the courage to make hard people decisions — are the same principles that prevent a company from collapsing when disruption arrives.

## Steps

### Step 1: Embrace Change as Opportunity, Not Threat

Most people encounter change by putting their head down and resisting it. Keyes reversed this instinct. When 7-Eleven filed for bankruptcy in 1991, he did not see it as a failure — he saw it as the fastest path to the top. In a wildly successful company, he might have spent another 20 years without becoming CEO. In a challenged company, he rose to CFO and eventually CEO because he was willing to look at the problem as an opportunity. When your industry or company is disrupted, do not panic. Ask yourself: what opportunity does this create that would never have existed in stable times?

### Step 2: Develop Cultural Literacy Before You Go Global

Technology has shrunk the world, but most businesses still fail internationally because they export their home culture instead of adapting to the local one. Keyes learned this as a student in London, where he went from least popular to most popular by learning British culture and cricket. Decades later, that same skill allowed him to negotiate with Japanese investors and expand 7-Eleven into China. In markets like Saudi Arabia, where relationship-building precedes any business discussion, the American instinct to pitch immediately is a liability. Study the culture, respect the customs, and enter every new market as a student, not an expert.

### Step 3: Treat People as Your Primary Asset

CEOs often obsess over equipment, assets, and balance sheets while ignoring the human resource. Keyes argues that people are the only asset that can make or break a company. That means hiring the right people for the right roles — and having the courage to remove the wrong ones. If you keep an underperforming employee because they have been around for 20 years, you are penalizing the entire team. Cut the cancer out immediately. The hard conversation today prevents a toxic culture tomorrow.

### Step 4: Eliminate Fear by Replacing It with Knowledge

Organizations are destroyed by two fear-driven behaviors: fighters who resist everything and flighters who passively agree and then sabotage. Both behaviors stem from the same root — the amygdala-driven fight-or-flight response. The antidote is not motivation speeches or team-building exercises. It is knowledge. When an employee lashes out in a budget meeting, the cause is almost always that they do not understand the financials. Teach them. Replace the caveman brain with what Keyes calls the aviator brain: using data, logic, and shared understanding to make decisions instead of reacting from fear.

### Step 5: Cut Through Layers to Cast Vision

Vision gets distorted the more layers of management it passes through. Keyes compares management to a game of telephone — by the time strategy reaches the front line, it is unrecognizable. Instead of relying on cascading communication, he cut through 10 to 15 layers of management at 7-Eleven by hosting weekly teleconferences with the entire organization and repeating only three priorities. When your entire company cannot write down your strategy verbatim, you have a communication problem, not a people problem.

### Step 6: Differentiate Yourself with Change, Confidence, and Clarity

In a corporate environment where 90% of people are coasting, the ones who rise are not necessarily the smartest — they are the ones who differentiate. Keyes boiled his own career propulsion down to three words: change, confidence, and clarity. Embrace change when others resist it. Build confidence by learning skills you do not yet have. Maintain clarity about where you are going so that decisions become obvious. These three traits are more valuable than any degree or credential because they are rare.

### Step 7: Practice Radical Optimism as a Leader

A CEO's emotional state sets the ceiling for the entire organization. If you stand in front of your team and express uncertainty or anxiety about the situation, they will reflect that anxiety. If you face a crisis with radical optimism — "We are going to be all right, and here is how we are handling it" — the team will follow. This is not blind positivity. It is the belief that problems are solvable and that the organization has the capacity to solve them. Without it, startups die and divisions get shut down.

### Step 8: Understand That No Company Is Too Big to Fail

Blockbuster had the chance to beat Netflix. They had exclusive studio content, a Google distribution partnership, and a streaming product called Blockbuster on Demand. The 2008 financial collapse killed the deal — Google walked away, studios tightened credit terms, and Blockbuster was forced into restructuring. The lesson is that external shocks expose internal weaknesses. If your company lacks innovation, fear and inertia will finish what the crisis started. The companies that survive are the ones that were already changing before the market forced them to.

## Examples

### Example 1: Rising Through Bankruptcy at 7-Eleven

In 1991, 7-Eleven was bankrupt and nearly went away. Keyes joined the company in that chaos and rose from running the gasoline operation to CFO, COO, and eventually CEO. In a successful company, he likely would have remained in an operational role for decades. The bankruptcy created the vacuum that allowed him to demonstrate commercial intellect and leadership at the highest level. Ten years later, he sold 7-Eleven to the Japanese ITOCHU Corporation, which wanted the company to expand into China.

### Example 2: The Blockbuster Deal That Got Away

Keyes explains that the common narrative — that Blockbuster turned down Netflix for $50 million — is false. What actually happened was far more complex: Blockbuster had acquired a streaming company called Movie Link, secured exclusive streaming rights from Viacom for $100 million a year, and negotiated a distribution collaboration with Google on YouTube. The three elements — content, distribution, and exclusivity — would have created a checkmate against Netflix. When Lehman Brothers collapsed in 2008 and Blockbuster's billion-dollar debt load spooked the studios, Google killed the deal. The company was then forced into restructuring rather than transformation.

### Example 3: Learning Cultural Literacy in London

As a junior year student in London, Keyes was initially hated for being an American. He was a Yank who did not understand British culture. His Pakistani and Indian friends took him under their wing, taught him to play cricket, and helped him adapt. He went from least popular to most popular on campus. Decades later, that same cultural literacy allowed him to negotiate with Japanese investors and enter the Chinese market with a level of comfort that his American teammates lacked. The skill he learned as a student became a competitive advantage at the CEO level.

### Example 4: The Budget Meeting Outburst

During a 360-degree feedback exercise, Keyes asked his management team to write down the company strategy. He discovered that none of them could articulate it correctly — even though he had been communicating it every week for years. The problem was that he was the only one speaking the strategy. People can listen to a language for years without being able to speak it. The fix was simple: every leader in the organization had to start speaking the strategy, not just hearing it. That one change aligned the entire company around a shared objective.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Treat every crisis as an opportunity to differentiate yourself from the 90% who will panic or quit
- ✅ Study the culture of every country before you do business there — adapt your behavior, do not export your assumptions
- ✅ Hire for cultural fit and commercial intellect, not just credentials and resumes
- ✅ Remove toxic employees quickly — fear-driven behavior spreads faster than optimism
- ✅ Cut through management layers by communicating directly and repeating only a handful of priorities
- ✅ Replace the caveman brain with the aviator brain: use knowledge and data, not fear and perception
- ✅ Practice radical optimism — your team will feel exactly what you feel
- ✅ Understand that no company is too big to fail if it stops innovating

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Resisting change because it feels uncomfortable or threatens your current position.
  **Solution:** Reframe every disruption as an opportunity. The companies and careers that flourish are the ones that use change as a ladder, not as a cliff.

- **Problem:** Expanding globally without understanding local business culture.
  **Solution:** Spend time in the market before you try to sell in it. Build relationships before you pitch. In many cultures, trust is the prerequisite for every transaction.

- **Problem:** Keeping underperforming employees because they are loyal or have been around for a long time.
  **Solution:** Loyalty to a person who is dragging the team down is not kindness — it is negligence. Make the hard decision and help them find a role where they can actually succeed.

- **Problem:** Letting fear drive decisions instead of knowledge.
  **Solution:** When you feel the urge to fight or flee, pause. Ask yourself: what am I afraid of, and is it based on a fact or a perception? Replace fear with a learning plan.

- **Problem:** Believing your company is too big to fail.
  **Solution:** Blockbuster had three checkmate moves against Netflix and still lost because of a financial crisis they could not control. Build innovation into your culture before you need it, not after.
