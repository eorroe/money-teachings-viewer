# They're Lying To You About Buying Property... Here's How To Get Rich ｜ Ari Rastegar

## Overview

This teaching distills the core real estate and wealth principles shared by Ari Rastegar, founder of Rastegar Property Company, into actionable steps. Rastegar built a multibillion-dollar real estate portfolio starting from a family that immigrated to America with nothing. His central insight is that failure is not a bad thing — it is a required ingredient for growth. You should reframe every setback as a signal that you are still in the game. Wealth comes from setting a specific, written destination, mastering the language of contracts, and consistently taking actions that compound over years, not weeks. Emotional fitness — the ability to feel the pain of inadequacy and keep going anyway — is more important than financial literacy.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are building or scaling a real estate portfolio
- When you are navigating failure and need to reframe setbacks as progress
- When you want to understand how to use contracts and legal knowledge as a competitive advantage
- When you need to set long-term goals that survive short-term discouragement
- When you are considering law school, entrepreneurship, or a career transition into real estate
- When you want to understand how proximity to higher standards accelerates growth
- When the user asks about real estate development, commercial property, or wealth building at scale

## Steps

### Step 1: Write Down a Specific, Numeric Destination

A goal that is not written down is not a goal — it is a wish. Rastegar wrote himself a personal check for $1 billion when he was 23 years old and carried it in his wallet every day for almost 15 years. The check was not a wish — it was a destination. Without a written, specific goal, you cannot know when you have arrived. Your GPS cannot give you directions if you only say "I want to go eat." Write down the exact number, the exact asset, the exact outcome. Then revisit it daily until it becomes real. The difference between a goal and a dream is whether it is written down.

### Step 2: Reframe Failure as Fuel

Rastegar's grandfather told him, "You're a failure, Ari." He agreed. That agreement became the foundation of his resilience. Most people experience failure and conclude they should stop. Rastegar experienced failure and concluded he was still in the game. When you are insulted, rejected, or knocked down, do not argue with the label. Agree with it. Then let it drive you to prove the next version of yourself wrong. Failure is data. Each setback is teaching you what does not work. The person who fails the most and keeps going wins.

### Step 3: Set a Meaningful First Goal, Not a Grandiose One

Rastegar did not start by trying to build a multibillion-dollar company. He started by getting into law school — a goal he could see, name, and work toward. Grandiose goals are a form of procrastination. Pick a goal that is meaningful and achievable, master it, and let the next goal reveal itself. The person you become by hitting that first goal will be the person who can see the next one. You cannot connect the dots looking forward; you can only connect them looking back. Trust that the first goal is the right starting point.

### Step 4: Master the Language of Business: Contracts

All business is a contract. All relationships are covenants — which is another word for contracts. Rastegar used his legal training to understand order, structure, and consideration. When you understand how deals are structured, you can see opportunities others miss. Study the mechanics of a real estate transaction, a partnership agreement, or a lease before you sign anything. The person who understands the contract controls the outcome. Legal training is not just for lawyers — it is for anyone who wants to understand how value is exchanged, protected, and enforced.

### Step 5: Activate Compound Consistency

Rastegar cites the principle that we dramatically overestimate what we can do in a year and dramatically underestimate what we can do in a decade. Most people quit too early because the reward is not visible in 90 days. The real reward is invisible until year five or year ten. Show up consistently even when nothing seems to be happening. The compound effect is working even when you cannot see it. Boring is beautiful: show up, do the work, read consistently, and let time do what speed cannot.

### Step 6: Use Proximity as a Strategy

Rastegar grew up in Highland Park, the richest neighborhood in Texas, as one of the poorest kids there. Proximity to wealth made the gap feel enormous — but it also made the goal feel possible. Seek environments where the standard is higher than your current income or skill level. Exposure to what is possible is the first step toward building it. If everyone around you is broke, you will stay broke. If you are surrounded by people building at the level you want, your brain will start to map the path. You do not need to be invited. Read their books, watch their content, study their decisions.

### Step 7: Build Emotional Fitness Before Building a Balance Sheet

Rastegar spent years in speech therapy, grew up with a lisp, was told he had ADD, and felt constant self-consciousness. What looked like a curse in childhood became the emotional resilience he needed to survive lawsuits, market crashes, and scaling a multibillion-dollar firm. Emotional fitness — the ability to feel the pain of inadequacy and keep going anyway — is more important than financial literacy. Without it, you will quit the first time someone tells you that you are not good enough. Control what you can control: your health, your inputs, your responses.

## Examples

### Example 1: From Johnny Rockets to Law School to Real Estate

Rastegar worked at Johnny Rockets in high school making $2.86 an hour and keeping his tips. That frustration — watching wealthy kids come in and feeling he was on the wrong side of the counter — created the hunger that drove him. He borrowed $3,000 in law school to buy a small lot, built a house for $80,000, and sold it for $115,000. That first deal connected the dots between his old life and his new one. From there, he began to think about scale: "If I built one, maybe I can build 10. Maybe 1,000." The lesson: your first meaningful goal does not need to be your biggest. It just needs to be real enough to get you moving.

### Example 2: Writing the Billion-Dollar Check

At 23, Rastegar wrote himself a personal check for $1 billion and carried it in his wallet for nearly 15 years. He distinguishes between a billion-dollar valuation and a billion dollars in your checking account — "there's levels to how this works." The point of the check was not the money. It was the daily reminder of the destination. Every decision, every deal, every conversation was filtered through the question: does this move me toward the number on that check? A tangible daily reminder of where you are going is one of the most powerful tools you can carry.

### Example 3: Anticipating Market Shifts Through Law

Rastegar's legal training allowed him to see zoning and entitlements as opportunities, not obstacles. When he looked at a 318-acre agricultural site in Kyle, Texas, he saw that the town's needs were evolving from farmland to housing. He advocated to the city council for rezoning, and the site became one of his most impactful developments. The lesson: real estate at its best is not about buying what exists — it is about seeing what a community will need before the market prices it in, and having the legal skill to help make it happen.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Write down specific, numeric goals and revisit them daily
- ✅ Agree with failure and use it as fuel, not as a stop sign
- ✅ Start with a meaningful first goal before chasing a grandiose one
- ✅ Learn contract law and deal structure before making large real estate investments
- ✅ Show up consistently even when the reward is not yet visible
- ✅ Surround yourself with people operating at the level you want to reach
- ✅ Build emotional fitness through hardship — do not avoid pain, grow from it
- ✅ Control your inputs: what you eat, what you read, what you focus on
- ❌ Do not set goals so large they become a form of procrastination
- ❌ Do not let failure define your identity — let it refine your strategy
- ❌ Do not enter a real estate deal without understanding the contract
- ❌ Do not isolate yourself from people who are already winning at the level you aspire to

## Keep In Mind

- There is a difference between success and fulfillment. Success is a formula — you can study it and replicate it. Fulfillment is an art — it requires knowing what matters to you beyond the numbers.
- "Having a billion-dollar valuation is not the same as having a billion dollars in your checking account." Understand the difference between paper wealth and liquidity.
- You do not need to have all the answers at the beginning. You only need to have the next step clear enough to take it.
- Emotional pain in childhood is not a life sentence — it is raw material for resilience if you are willing to work with it.
- The compound effect requires time. The habits that build a billion-dollar portfolio are boring, repetitive, and invisible for years before they become undeniable.
- Real estate is ultimately a people business. The best investors understand human psychology — what a community needs, what a seller fears, what an investor wants — better than they understand spreadsheets.

## Security & Safety Notes

- This is educational content based on a publicly available podcast episode.
- Real estate investments carry significant financial risk. Always perform due diligence before acquiring property.
- Consult a qualified attorney before signing any real estate contract or partnership agreement.
- Do not invest borrowed money in speculative real estate deals without understanding the full downside scenario.
- Zoning and entitlement changes carry political risk. Do not rely on anticipated rezoning as the sole basis for a property acquisition.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Setting a goal so large it becomes intimidating and prevents action.
  **Solution:** Break it into a first goal you can hit in 12 months. Momentum builds from small, proven wins.

- **Problem:** Treating every failure as a signal to quit.
  **Solution:** Reframe failure as data. Each setback is teaching you what does not work. The person who fails the most and keeps going wins.

- **Problem:** Skipping contract education and relying on others to "handle the details."
  **Solution:** Learn the mechanics of every deal you are in. If you do not understand the contract, you do not understand the risk.

- **Problem:** Letting proximity to wealth make you feel inadequate rather than inspired.
  **Solution:** Use the gap between where you are and where you want to be as a compass, not a source of shame. The feeling of inadequacy is a sign that you are close to something that matters.

- **Problem:** Waiting for motivation instead of building routines.
  **Solution:** Motivation and inspiration run out quickly. Build a daily routine — read, exercise, eat well, meditate — that keeps you in a peak state regardless of how you feel on any given day.
