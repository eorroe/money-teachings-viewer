# I Took Down McDonald's & Wendy's... Now I Make Millions ｜ Patrick Terry

## Overview

This money teaching distills Patrick Terry's journey of founding P. Terry's, a fast-casual burger chain in Austin, Texas, that outlasted every major competitor in its original location. The lessons cover why simple, authentic concepts can beat complex corporate machines, how partner alignment matters more than business plans, and why starting a business in your forties can be an advantage rather than a liability.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When launching a food, retail, or service business with limited resources
- When facing established, well-funded competitors in your market
- When choosing a business partner or co-founder
- When you feel it is too late to start the business you have always wanted to start
- When the user asks about restaurant startups, competitive positioning, or starting later in life

## Steps

### Step 1: Start With a Concept So Simple It Does Not Need Explanation

P. Terry's competes with McDonald's, Wendy's, In-N-Out, and Whataburger on the exact same product: burgers, fries, and milkshakes. The advantage is not complexity; it is authenticity and consistency. Build your business around a core offering that requires no education for the customer. If you have to explain why your product is good, it is probably not good enough yet.

### Step 2: Choose Your Partner Based on Character, Not Just Skillset

Terry and his wife Kathy built P. Terry's together, and he is explicit that it would not have survived without her. The partner you choose will be with you through cash-flow crises, lease disputes, equipment failures, and public failures. Look for someone who shares your values and your tolerance for hardship, not just someone who fills a functional gap. Test alignment under stress before you sign anything.

### Step 3: Persist Through the Phase Where No One Takes You Seriously

In the early years, Terry talked about opening a burger stand for years before anyone believed it would happen. Most people dismissed the idea or forgot he was serious. Use that lack of external belief as fuel rather than validation. The people who succeed at building something real are usually the ones who kept showing up after the audience stopped watching.

### Step 4: Outlast the Big Competitors by Being Better, Not Cheaper

When P. Terry's opened, six competing chains were within eyesight of the original location. Every single one of them eventually closed. Terry did not beat them by lowering prices or out-spending them on marketing. He beat them by refusing to cut corners on ingredients, training, or cleanliness. Compete on quality and consistency, because large chains eventually sacrifice both for margin.

### Step 5: Visualize the Outcome Before You Can Afford It

Terry knew exactly where everything in the kitchen would go before he had the lease or the funding. Visualization is not daydreaming; it is a planning tool that exposes logistical problems before you have signed contracts. Spend real time walking through your business operation in your head, because the details you spot early will save you expensive mistakes later.

## Examples

### Example 1: Competing Against Established Chains

A new coffee shop opens in a neighborhood with three national chains within a two-block radius. Instead of copying their loyalty programs, the independent shop focuses on single-origin beans, barista training, and a community bulletin board. Within two years the chains notice declining foot traffic, and the independent shop expands to a second location.

### Example 2: Partner Alignment Under Pressure

Two founders start a landscaping business. Six months in, a major client delays payment and cash flow is tight. The founder who is willing to skip their own paycheck and work extra hours to cover the gap discovers that the co-founder is already looking for a different job. The partnership dissolves before it destroys the business.

### Example 3: Starting at 45 Versus 25

A former accountant spends 20 years in corporate finance before starting a home-staging business at 47. The industry knowledge, client communication skills, and financial discipline from the corporate career become the foundation for a company that scales to 12 locations in eight years.

## Best Practices

- Lead with a product so good it requires no explanation
- Test partner alignment under real pressure before launching
- Outlast competitors through quality and consistency, not price wars
- Visualize operations in detail before committing capital
- Start when the timing feels right to you, not when external validation arrives

## Keep In Mind

- Simplicity beats complexity in customer-facing products
- Character matters more than credentials in a co-founder
- The first location will either prove or disprove the model

## Security & Safety Notes

- All advice is drawn from a publicly available podcast interview
- Do your own due diligence before making any business or investment decisions
- Consult qualified legal and financial advisors when structuring partnerships or leases

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Overcomplicating the product to stand out
  **Solution:** Strip the offering down to what customers actually want and do that perfectly

- **Problem:** Choosing a partner based on skills rather than shared values
  **Solution:** Test how the person handles stress, failure, and sacrifice before committing

- **Problem:** Competing on price against larger competitors
  **Solution:** Compete on quality, consistency, and authenticity

- **Problem:** Waiting for external permission to start
  **Solution:** Keep showing up even when no one else believes in the idea
