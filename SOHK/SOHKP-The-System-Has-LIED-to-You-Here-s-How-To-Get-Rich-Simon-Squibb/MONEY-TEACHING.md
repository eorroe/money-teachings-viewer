# The System Has LIED to You... Here's How To Get Rich ｜ Simon Squibb

## Overview

This money teaching distills the key financial and business principles from 'The System Has LIED to You... Here's How To Get Rich ｜ Simon Squibb' into actionable steps you can apply to build wealth and achieve financial freedom. The episode challenges conventional education and motivation-based advice, arguing that real-world financial success is forged through necessity, action, and relentless follow-through — not classroom learning or waiting for the right moment.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you feel stuck and believe no opportunity or external rescue is coming
- When you are considering starting a business but lack capital or feel unqualified
- When you want to understand why motivation alone is insufficient for lasting success
- When you have failed before and need a framework for turning that failure into compounding advantage
- When building or scaling a team and need a reminder that collaboration outperforms individual effort
- When you are tempted to delay action until conditions feel "perfect"

## Steps

### Step 1: Accept That No One Is Coming to Save You

Traditional education does not teach real-world survival skills. Thirteen years of schooling can leave a person well-versed in academic subjects yet unprepared for financial independence. The first and most important shift is internal: stop waiting for an employer, the government, a mentor, or circumstance to hand you a solution. Survival and wealth-building become your sole responsibility. Accepting this reality is the prerequisite for all meaningful action.

### Step 2: Start Before You Feel Ready

Necessity is a more powerful catalyst than preparation. Many successful entrepreneurs begin with almost nothing — not because they planned perfectly, but because circumstances forced action. A person who is homeless and has only 200 units of currency does not wait for the ideal conditions to start a business; necessity removes the option of waiting. The same principle applies at any income level: start with the resources currently available rather than waiting for more.

### Step 3: Treat Entrepreneurship as a Muscle That Wakes Up Under Pressure

Entrepreneurial ability is not an innate trait reserved for a select few. It is a response that activates when necessity demands it — similar to how the body finds hidden strength in an emergency. Schools teach in a way that dulls this instinct by rewarding compliance and individual performance on exams. Real business, by contrast, requires the same adaptive urgency that surfaces when survival is at stake. Reframe entrepreneurship not as a talent but as a capacity that awakens when the need is real enough.

### Step 4: Convert Failure Into Compounding Learning

Failure is not the opposite of progress — it is a form of progress when processed correctly. A first business may fail due to avoidable errors like seasonal miscalculation. The critical question is not whether the venture succeeded, but what was extracted from it: hiring experience, sales skills, market awareness, and operational knowledge. These learnings compound across every subsequent venture. Every failure should be audited for its transferable lessons before moving forward.

### Step 5: Reject the Myth of Passive Income

Wealth requires ongoing execution. The idea that money alone removes the need to work is a misconception. Even the wealthiest individuals continue to make decisions, manage assets, and execute on opportunities. Financial independence means having the freedom to choose how to direct your effort — not eliminating the need for effort entirely. Maintaining and growing wealth is itself a skill that must be exercised continuously.

### Step 6: Begin With What You Already Possess

Fancy equipment, large capital reserves, and polished credentials are not prerequisites for starting. A media company can be launched with a single smartphone. The limiting factor is rarely the tools available — it is the willingness to begin. Identify the minimum viable assets at hand and build from there. Solve the first problem with the first resource, then reinvest proceeds into expanding capability.

### Step 7: Let Necessity Drive You More Than Motivation

Motivation is unreliable; it fluctuates with mood and circumstances. Necessity is constant. An entrepreneur who starts purely out of desire may quit when results are slow. An entrepreneur who starts out of genuine need has no such option — and that persistence is often the deciding factor. Reframe your "why" from something desirable to something essential. When the work is non-negotiable, follow-through becomes inevitable.

### Step 8: Build a Team — Because 1 + 1 = 11

Success is never a solo pursuit. Academic environments reward individual performance on isolated exams, but business rewards collaboration, delegation, and collective problem-solving. A capable team multiplies output far beyond what any individual can achieve alone. Invest time in finding people whose strengths complement your weaknesses. Learn to delegate outcomes, not just tasks, and build a culture where the team's success is tied to shared goals.

### Step 9: Prioritize Follow-Through Over Idea Generation

Most people generate ideas but very few execute on them when results are delayed. The differentiator between those who build wealth and those who remain stagnant is not the quality of their ideas — it is their willingness to continue when progress is invisible. Set a standard for yourself: commit to a minimum period of consistent execution before evaluating whether to continue or pivot. Quitting early is the most common and most costly mistake.

### Step 10: Take Action Before You Feel Prepared to Plan

Planning has value, but over-planning is a sophisticated form of procrastination. Many people use the need for more capital, more knowledge, or more connections as an excuse to avoid starting. The fastest way to gather real knowledge is through execution, not study. Solve the immediate problem in front of you with the resources you already control. Action creates information that no amount of planning could have predicted — and that information is what makes future decisions better.

## Examples

**Example 1: The Homeless Founder With 200 Pounds**

An individual with no stable housing and only 200 pounds to their name started a gardening business. They did not wait until they had a storefront, a van, or a team. They used what they had — their own labor and the cash in hand — and began offering a service people needed. Necessity removed the paralysis that planning would have created. The business may not have lasted year-round, but it generated income, built confidence, and produced lessons in hiring and sales that funded every subsequent venture.

**Example 2: Building a Media Company With a Smartphone**

A media company was launched not with professional cameras and a production studio, but with a single iPhone. The founder did not let the absence of industry-standard equipment block entry into the market. Instead, they identified that content quality and audience connection matter more than production value at the earliest stage. Revenue from early content funded better equipment later. Starting imperfectly is always superior to never starting at all.

**Example 3: The Seasonal Business Pivot**

A first business failed because the founder did not account for seasonal demand — work dried up in winter and the operation could not sustain itself. Rather than viewing this as a final defeat, the founder extracted the transferable skills: how to hire, how to sell, how to manage cash flow, and how to talk to customers. Those skills were applied to a new venture with better timing and structure. The failure was not wasted; it was tuition paid for a more capable version of the founder.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Accept full responsibility for your financial outcomes — no external rescue is coming
- ✅ Start with your current resources rather than waiting for ideal conditions
- ✅ Treat every failure as data: document what went wrong and what you would change
- ✅ Build a team early and delegate outcomes, not just tasks
- ✅ Measure follow-through in months of consistent execution, not days of motivation
- ✅ Reinvest early profits into capability expansion rather than lifestyle upgrades
- ❌ Do not wait for motivation to act — let necessity drive your decisions
- ❌ Do not over-plan as a substitute for starting
- ❌ Do not believe that wealth eliminates the need for ongoing work and decision-making
- ❌ Do not attempt to build significant wealth alone — collaboration is non-negotiable at scale
- ❌ Do not let a single failure define the limits of what you can build next

## Keep In Mind

- The system you were educated in was not designed to make you wealthy — understand this clearly and stop expecting it to deliver financial outcomes
- Necessity is a more reliable engine than inspiration; design your situation so that action becomes unavoidable
- Every skill you build, even in a failed venture, transfers to your next opportunity
- Wealth is built through compounding small, correct decisions over time — not single dramatic moments
- Your current resources are always sufficient to begin; the barrier is almost always psychological, not material
- Follow-through in the absence of visible results is the rarest and most valuable skill in entrepreneurship

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Waiting until you have enough money, knowledge, or connections to start
  **Solution:** Begin with the resources and knowledge you already possess; execution will generate the rest

- **Problem:** Letting a failed business or project define your identity or limit future attempts
  **Solution:** Conduct an honest audit of every failure, extract the transferable lessons, and apply them to your next venture

- **Problem:** Relying on motivation to sustain long-term effort
  **Solution:** Anchor your persistence in necessity and commitment rather than fluctuating emotional states

- **Problem:** Attempting to build and scale a business entirely alone
  **Solution:** Actively recruit and invest in a team early; delegate outcomes and build systems that others can operate

- **Problem:** Believing that reaching a certain income level eliminates the need for ongoing work
  **Solution:** Maintain the discipline of continuous execution regardless of current financial status; wealth maintenance is an active skill

- **Problem:** Using detailed planning as an excuse to delay starting
  **Solution:** Set a hard deadline to begin with a minimum viable version of your idea, then iterate based on real-world feedback
