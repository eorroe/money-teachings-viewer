# SMB - How To Become A Market Wizard with Jack Schwager

SMB MARKET-WIZARD: How to Become a Market Wizard with Jack Schwager and George Coyle

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills four decades of market wisdom from Jack Schwager and George Coyle, authors of the Market Wizards series, on what truly separates traders. The core message is that successful trading is not about finding an effective strategy but about discovering a style that fits your personality, maintaining composure under pressure, and practicing disciplined risk management. This guide focuses on the proven principles, psychological traits, and practical steps that have produced the results of top traders profiled in the Market Wizards series.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you have recently started trading and want to understand what it actually takes to trade successfully
- When you are a practicing trader struggling to find your edge or style
- When you want to improve your risk management and emotional discipline
- When you are curious about how top traders profiled in the Market Wizards series think and operate
- When you need guidance on position sizing and avoiding losses
- When you want to build resilience and persistence in the face of drawdowns

## Steps

### Step 1: Commit to Deep Self-Awareness and Personality Alignment

Understand that trading requires self-awareness. Before you can trade successfully, you must identify your personality and risk tolerance. The Market Wizards span a range of temperaments: from Kenny Sharkness, who thrives managing positions and strategies simultaneously, to traders like the volunteer firefighter who never had a losing month in 15 years because of tremendous risk avoidance. Neither style is inherently better; the key is finding a methodology that fits who you are. Ask yourself: Do you want to live in front of a screen for 14 to 16 hours a day, as day traders do, or do you prefer checking in each day? Would you rather take consistent gains or accept drawdowns? Your answers will narrow the set of viable strategies.

### Step 2: Read and Learn From Established Principles

Start by reading about the consistent principles that have stood the test of 100 years of traders. The fundamentals include: respect price action of the market, stick with your winners, and cut your losses. Jack Schwager advises that if you read a book and get one good idea, you have gotten something of value. Read the Market Wizards series to see different styles and hear directly from traders profiled in the Market Wizards series. Use books and research to generate ideas, not to find a step-by-step formula. Test concepts that resonate with you and discard those that do not.

### Step 3: Start Small and Protect Your Capital

Never open a full account with all the capital you think you can risk. Take 25,000 instead. George Coyle and Jack Schwager both emphasize starting with 25,000 instead of 500,000. Set a hard stop: if you lose a small amount, stop trading. Come back later. This approach ensures you cannot cause large losses and gives you ongoing opportunities to try again, which is necessary because many market wizards blew up three or four times before succeeding. Most traders recommend keeping individual bet size below 1%.

### Step 4: Embrace Asymmetric Risk/Reward

The vast majority of elite traders set up trades where if they are right, they generate profits, and if they are wrong, they lose only a limited amount. Asymmetric trades appear across all the prior Market Wizards books. More than half of elite traders lose more than half of the time, yet they still generate profits because their wins are larger than their losses. Avoid strategies with open-ended risk, such as shorting small-cap stocks, where the worst-case scenario can produce losses that exceed your initial stop and stops may not protect you due to gaps. If you do trade high-risk strategies, ensure you can exit immediately when conditions deteriorate.

### Step 5: Test Ideas Thoroughly and Iterate

Do not assume that whatever strategy you start with is what you will end up with. A good percentage of traders evolve over time. Test thousands of trades to see if an idea actually works before committing capital. Kenny Sharkness, featured in the new book, built up his repository of trades and strategies over 20 years, not overnight. Experiment with different styles, track results, and be willing to pivot when something does not fit your personality or is not profitable.

### Step 6: Develop Emotional Control and Mental Fortitude

Cultivate the ability to remain calm and controlled even when markets are moving against you and you face pressure. This trait appeared consistently across decades of interviews. Paul Tudor Jones described Eli Tullis losing millions yet being completely cordial and in a good mood, with visitors unable to tell if it was a bad day or a good day. Stanley Druckenmiller maintained composure by having the presence of mind to cover a leveraged long position and go back short on the morning of the October 19, 1987 crash, despite having made the worst trade the day before. You must train yourself to let market outcomes be unrelated to your mood.

### Step 7: Persist Through Adversity and Maintain Faith

Successful traders persist even when all signs suggest they are not suited for trading. Your family might urge you to do something else, your results might be terrible, and you might have blown up your account three or four times. Yet elite traders have faith in themselves despite no evidence supporting that faith at the time. This persistence is particularly prevalent among independent traders and prop traders who are fundamentally fighting against the world. If you cannot persist through three or four failures, trading is not for you.

### Step 8: Continuously Adapt and Monitor Strategy Efficacy

Markets change over time. Strategies that worked in the 1960s and 1970s, such as simple trend following, might no longer be effective because they have become crowded with traders using similar strategies, producing choppy price action that causes whipsaws. Always be on the lookout for new strategies to replace those that stop working. Kenny Sharkness is regularly adding new strategies to his toolkit. AI, competition from quant funds, and changes in market structure are ongoing challenges that require attention. However, long-term thematic trends are very hard for computers to overcome and might still offer opportunities.

## Examples

### Example 1: Kenny Sharkness - Multiplicity and Personality Fit

Kenny Sharkness, featured in Market Wizards: The Next Generation, exemplifies finding a style that suits a unique personality. He manages positions simultaneously and uses strategies at once, combining equities with option structures to create the exact risk profile he wants. At first glance, this looks chaotic, but when analyzed closely, the bulk of his profits come from a few days per year and a few trading styles. The rest of his activity supports those core drivers. As a college student, he was already multitasking across screens, writing a paper on one and playing poker on another. His success came not from mastering one thing, but from building an integrated approach over 20 years that fits his natural ability.

### Example 2: Eli Tullis and Stanley Druckenmiller - Emotional Control Under Pressure

Paul Tudor Jones idolized Eli Tullis, who once lost millions in a bad day yet remained completely cordial and in a good mood during afternoon meetings. Visitors could not tell whether it had been a good day or a bad day. Similarly, Stanley Druckenmiller made what may have been the worst trade by switching from short to long the day before the October 19, 1987 crash. On the Monday morning after Black Monday, he had the presence of mind to immediately cover his entire leveraged long position and go back short the instant the market opened. In both cases, market outcomes were entirely separate from his mood.

### Example 3: The Volunteer Firefighter - Risk Aversion as a Strength

One trader in the book, a volunteer firefighter, went 15 years with virtually no losing months. His return-risk numbers were better than those of other traders. He was not interested in scaling up because he was simply comfortable trading modestly. This shows that a different personality, one with tremendous risk avoidance, can produce consistent gains even if total compounded wealth is modest. The lesson is that modest gains are valid and aligned with specific personalities.

### Example 4: Overcoming Catastrophic Drawdowns

Traders in the series blew up their accounts three or four times before finally succeeding. One trader started by putting 100% of his capital on a single trade and lost it all. He eventually became a Market Wizard. George Coyle says that these traders persist even when the entire world tells them they are not suited for trading, typically without a convincing reason for their faith. This illustrates that surviving long enough to learn is critical, which is why starting with 25,000 and being able to try again is so important.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Find a trading style that suits your personality rather than copying someone else's method
- ✅ Start with 25,000 in capital and set a hard stop-loss on total account drawdown
- ✅ Keep individual bet size below 1% of capital until you have consistency
- ✅ Respect price action, cut losses quickly, and let winners run
- ✅ Seek asymmetric trades where you generate profits when right and your losses are limited when wrong
- ✅ Read widely to gather ideas, but test them thoroughly before committing
- ✅ Maintain composure by letting market results be separate from your mood
- ✅ Persist through setbacks and view failures as part of the learning process
- ✅ Continuously add to your repository of trades as old methods stop working
- ✅ Verify statements, track records, and results rather than relying on reputation alone

## Keep In Mind

- Most people are better off not trading professionally. Trading requires commitment, long hours, and emotional strength that most people lack.
- Trading can be done as a hobby and is preferable to gambling because it offers a chance to generate profits if done well.
- You do not need to know a single universal formula; there are ways to generate profits in markets.
- Before you trade professionally, study successful traders. If you do not want their lifestyle, do not trade using their methods.
- For traders like Kenny Sharkness, the bulk of profits come from a few days per year and a few strategies, not from constant activity.
- Markets change. Strategies that worked in the 1960s and 1970s may no longer be effective due to competition, AI, and structural shifts.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never risk capital beyond what you can afford to lose. Treat trading as risk capital, not as savings.
- Be skeptical of traders who sell services or courses. Charlatans exist in this space. Verify track records through statements, tax returns, and third-party sources before following any trader.
- If you give something away for free, people value it less. If you charge for it, certain people will dismiss you regardless of quality. Judge traders by results, not by whether they sell education.
- Be aware that strategies with open-ended risk, such as shorting small-cap stocks, can lead to losses beyond your initial stop if the market gaps.
- Diversify your ideas and sources. Do not rely on a single mentor, social media account, or book for your trading education.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Starting with a too large position size and blowing up the account before learning anything.
  **Solution:** Start with 25,000. Risk only what you can afford to lose on any single trade or month, and stop trading if you hit your loss threshold.

- **Problem:** Chasing a strategy that does not fit your personality, leading to bad execution and burnout.
  **Solution:** Study several styles and test them. Ask whether you would want the lifestyle of that trader before committing to their approach.

- **Problem:** Assuming a win rate above 50% is necessary for profitability.
  **Solution:** Focus on asymmetric risk/reward. The vast majority of elite traders lose more than half of the time but generate profits because their winners are larger than their losers.

- **Problem:** Letting emotions drive decisions after a loss or during a drawdown.
  **Solution:** Build the habit of treating each trade as a separate event. Use stop-losses automatically and step away from the screen if you cannot maintain clear judgment.

- **Problem:** Believing a strategy that worked previously will continue working forever.
  **Solution:** Evaluate strategy performance. Be ready to adapt or replace strategies as market conditions, competition, and technology evolve.

- **Problem:** Overcomplicating trading with too many strategies before mastering one.
  **Solution:** Build your repository over years, not days. Complexity should serve your edge, not replace it. Kenny Sharkness did not start with 50 strategies; he accumulated them over two decades.
