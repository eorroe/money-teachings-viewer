# SMB - How To Become A Market Wizard with Jack Schwager

SMB MARKET-WIZARD: How to Become a Market Wizard with Jack Schwager and George Coyle

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills four decades of market wisdom from Jack Schwager and George Coyle, authors of the Market Wizards series, on what truly separates elite traders from everyone else. The core message is that successful trading is not about finding a magical strategy but about discovering a style that fits your personality, maintaining emotional control under pressure, and practicing disciplined risk management. This guide focuses on the timeless principles, psychological traits, and practical steps that have produced the world's best market performers.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are new to trading and want to understand what it actually takes to succeed
- When you are an experienced trader struggling to find your edge or style
- When you want to improve your risk management and emotional discipline
- When you are curious about how the greatest traders think and operate
- When you need guidance on position sizing and avoiding catastrophic losses
- When you want to build resilience and persistence in the face of drawdowns

## Steps

### Step 1: Commit to Deep Self-Awareness and Personality Alignment

Understand that trading requires immense self-awareness. Before you can succeed, you must identify your personality type and risk tolerance. The Market Wizards span an enormous range of temperaments: from Kenny Sharkness, who thrives managing dozens of positions and strategies simultaneously, to traders like the volunteer firefighter who never had a losing month in 15 years because of extreme risk aversion. Neither style is inherently better; the key is finding a methodology that fits who you are. Ask yourself: Do you want to live in front of a screen for 14+ hours a day, or do you prefer checking in a few times daily? Would you rather take smaller, consistent gains or swing for home runs with larger drawdowns? Your answers will narrow the universe of viable strategies.

### Step 2: Read and Learn From Established Principles

Start by reading about the consistent principles that have stood the test of 100 years of trading. The fundamentals include: respect price action of the market, stick with your winners, and cut your losses. Jack Schwager advises that if you read a book and get one good idea, you have gotten something of value. Read the Market Wizards series to see a broad range of styles and hear directly from successful traders. Use books and research to generate ideas, not to find a cookbook recipe. Test concepts that resonate with you and discard those that do not.

### Step 3: Start Small and Protect Your Capital

Never open a full account with all the capital you think you can risk. Take a small amount instead. George Coyle and Jack Schwager both emphasize starting with a small number, such as 25,000 instead of 500,000. Set a hard stop: if you lose a predetermined small amount, stop trading. Come back in a few months. This approach ensures you cannot do too much damage and gives you repeated opportunities to try again, which is necessary because many market wizards blew up multiple times before finding success. Most traders recommend keeping individual bet size below 1%.

### Step 4: Embrace Asymmetric Risk/Reward

Most elite traders set up trades where if they are right, they generate substantial profits, and if they are wrong, they do not lose that much. Asymmetric trades are the common denominator across almost all Market Wizards books. The vast majority of elite traders lose more than half the time, yet they still make money because their wins are much larger than their losses. Avoid strategies with open-ended risk, such as shorting small-cap stocks, where the worst-case scenario can be catastrophic and stops may not protect you due to gaps. If you do trade high-risk strategies, ensure you have the instinct and sensitivity to exit immediately when conditions deteriorate.

### Step 5: Test Ideas Thoroughly and Iterate

Do not assume that whatever strategy you start with is what you will end up with. A good percentage of traders evolve significantly over time. Test many thousands of trades to see if an idea actually works before committing significant capital. Kenny Sharkness, featured in the new book, gradually built up his repository of trades and strategies over 20 years, not overnight. Experiment with different styles, track results, and be willing to pivot when something does not fit your personality or is not profitable.

### Step 6: Develop Emotional Control and Mental Fortitude

Cultivate the ability to remain calm and controlled even when markets are moving against you and you face significant pressure. This trait appeared repeatedly across decades of interviews. Paul Tudor Jones described Eli Tullis losing millions yet being completely cordial and in a good mood, with visitors unable to tell if it was a bad day or a good day. Stanley Druckenmiller demonstrated nerves of steel by having the presence of mind to cover a leveraged long position and go back short on the morning of the October 19, 1987 crash, despite having made the worst possible trade the day before. You must train yourself to let market outcomes be unrelated to your mood.

### Step 7: Persist Through Adversity and Maintain Faith

Successful traders persist even when everything tells them they are not cut out for this. Your family may urge you to do something else, your results may be terrible, and you may have blown up your account three or four times. Yet the elite traders have deep faith in their abilities despite zero evidence supporting that faith at the time. This persistence is especially common among independent traders and prop traders who are essentially fighting against the world. If you cannot persist through repeated failure, trading is not for you.

### Step 8: Continuously Adapt and Monitor Strategy Efficacy

Markets change dramatically over time. Strategies that worked in the 1960s and 1970s, such as simple trend following, may no longer be effective because they have become crowded and choppy. Always be on the lookout for new strategies to replace those that stop working. Kenny Sharkness adds new strategies constantly to his toolkit. AI, competition from quant funds, and changes in market structure are ongoing threats that require vigilance. However, some long-term thematic trends resist automation and may still offer opportunities.

## Examples

### Example 1: Kenny Sharkness - Multiplicity and Personality Fit

Kenny Sharkness, featured in Market Wizards: The Next Generation, exemplifies finding a style that suits a unique personality. He manages many positions simultaneously and uses numerous strategies at once, combining equities with various option structures to create the exact risk profile he wants. On the surface, this looks chaotic, but when analyzed closely, the bulk of his profits come from a few key days and a handful of trading styles. The rest of his activity supports those core drivers. As a college student, he was already multitasking across screens, writing a paper on one and playing poker on another. His success came not from mastering one thing, but from building a mosaic over 20 years that fits his natural bandwidth.

### Example 2: Eli Tullis and Stanley Druckenmiller - Emotional Control Under Pressure

Paul Tudor Jones idolized Eli Tullis, who once lost millions in a bad day yet remained completely cordial and in a good mood during afternoon meetings. Visitors could not tell whether it had been a good day or a bad day. Similarly, Stanley Druckenmiller made what may have been the worst trade imaginable by switching from short to long the day before the October 19, 1987 crash. On the Monday morning after Black Monday, he had the presence of mind to immediately cover his entire leveraged long position and go back short the instant the market opened. In both cases, market outcomes were entirely separate from emotional state.

### Example 3: The Volunteer Firefighter - Risk Aversion as a Strength

One trader in the book, a volunteer firefighter, went 15 years with virtually no losing months. His return-risk metrics were off the charts compared to other traders. He was not interested in scaling up because he was simply comfortable trading small. This shows that a different personality, one with extreme risk avoidance, can produce excellent results even if total compounded wealth is not spectacular. The lesson is that steady, small gains are valid and aligned with certain temperaments.

### Example 4: Overcoming Catastrophic Drawdowns

Several traders in the series blew up their accounts multiple times before finally succeeding. One trader started by putting 100% of his capital on a single trade and lost it all. He eventually became a Market Wizard. George Coyle notes that these traders persist even when the entire world tells them they are not cut out for this, usually without a rational explanation for their faith. This illustrates that surviving long enough to learn is critical, which is why starting small and being able to try again is so important.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Find a trading style that suits your personality rather than copying someone else's method
- ✅ Start with a small amount of capital and set a hard stop-loss on total account drawdown
- ✅ Keep individual bet size below 1% of capital until you have proven consistency
- ✅ Respect price action, cut losses quickly, and let winners run
- ✅ Seek asymmetric trades where upside is large and downside is limited
- ✅ Read widely to gather ideas, but test them thoroughly before committing
- ✅ Maintain emotional control by letting market results be separate from your mood
- ✅ Persist through setbacks and view failures as part of the learning process
- ✅ Continuously add to your strategy repertoire as old methods lose efficacy
- ✅ Verify statements, track records, and results rather than relying on reputation alone

## Keep In Mind

- Most people are better off not trading professionally. Trading requires intense commitment, long hours, and emotional fortitude that few possess.
- Trading can be done small as a hobby and is preferable to gambling because it offers a chance to make money if done correctly.
- You do not need to know the one secret recipe; there are hundreds of ways to make money in markets.
- Before you play the game, study the winners. If you do not want their life, do not play their game.
- The bulk of profits for many complex traders often comes from a few days per year and a handful of strategies, not from constant activity.
- Markets change. Strategies that worked decades ago may no longer be effective due to competition, AI, and structural shifts.

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never risk capital you cannot afford to lose. Treat trading as risk capital, not as savings.
- Be skeptical of traders who sell services or courses. Some are charlatans. Verify track records through statements, tax returns, and third-party sources before following anyone.
- If you give something away for free, people often value it less. If you charge for it, some will dismiss you regardless of quality. Judge traders by results, not by whether they sell education.
- Be aware that strategies with open-ended risk, such as shorting small-cap stocks, can lead to losses far beyond your initial stop if the market gaps.
- Diversify your ideas and sources. Do not rely on a single mentor, social media account, or book for your trading education.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Starting with too large a position size and blowing up the account before learning anything.
  **Solution:** Start small. Risk only what you can afford to lose on any single trade or month, and stop trading if you hit your predetermined loss threshold.

- **Problem:** Chasing a strategy that does not fit your personality, leading to poor execution and burnout.
  **Solution:** Study multiple styles and test them. Ask whether you would want the lifestyle of that trader before committing to their approach.

- **Problem:** Assuming a high win rate is necessary for profitability.
  **Solution:** Focus on asymmetric risk/reward. Many elite traders win only 30-40% of the time but make substantial money because their winners are much larger than their losers.

- **Problem:** Letting emotions drive decisions after a loss or during a drawdown.
  **Solution:** Build the habit of treating each trade as an independent event. Use stop-losses automatically and step away from the screen if you cannot maintain objectivity.

- **Problem:** Believing a strategy that worked in the past will continue working forever.
  **Solution:** Regularly evaluate strategy performance. Be ready to adapt or replace strategies as market conditions, competition, and technology evolve.

- **Problem:** Overcomplicating trading with too many strategies before mastering any.
  **Solution:** Build your repertoire gradually over years, not days. Complexity should serve your edge, not replace it. Kenny Sharkness did not start with 50 strategies; he accumulated them over two decades.
