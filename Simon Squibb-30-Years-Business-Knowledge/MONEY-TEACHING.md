# 30 Years of Business Knowledge

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills 30 years of business experience from building 19 companies and investing in 78 startups into actionable guidance for starting, growing, and selling a business. It covers the complete business lifecycle from zero investment launch through scaling globally to eventual exit, emphasizing purpose-driven entrepreneurship over profit-only models. The core philosophy is that success comes from delayed gratification, building value before monetizing, and aligning your work with what you genuinely love doing.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you want to start a business with no money or minimal investment
- When you need a practical alternative to traditional business plans
- When you are trying to find product-market fit without wasting capital
- When you want to understand how to structure equity and avoid founder disputes
- When you need guidance on hiring, firing, and building company culture
- When you are preparing to raise money from investors or sponsors
- When you want to build a personal brand or company brand that lasts
- When you are planning to sell or exit your business

## Steps

### Step 1: Identify What You Love and What You Hate

Write down exactly what you enjoy doing and what you avoid. Your business idea must link to your genuine passion, because you will need to outwork everyone else in that space. The school system teaches that weakness means you must improve at it, but the truth is you should outsource what you hate and obsess over what you love. Business ideas begin when you combine your passion with a partner who fills your skill gaps.

### Step 2: Execute a Simple First Step

Do not build a complex product before validating demand. Your first execution should be the simplest possible version of your idea: a podcast, a blog post, a LinkedIn article, or a social media handle. Validate that people want what you are offering before spending significant money. Airbnb started by renting air mattresses at conferences, not by building a platform. Keep execution cheap, iterate quickly, and improve quality over time.

### Step 3: Experiment with Revenue Models

Do not lock yourself into a single pricing model on day one. Test multiple ways to make money from your activity: hourly billing, outcome-based pricing, subscriptions, licensing, or pre-sales. The business model should emerge from what customers actually pay for, not from a theoretical plan written before launch. Stay nimble like a boxer and adjust your punch combination based on what works.

### Step 4: Install a Purpose Bigger Than Profit

A purpose-driven business does not need to be a world-saving mission. It must simply be a reason customers, employees, and partners want to show up beyond money. When your purpose is clear, you stop managing people and start managing alignment. Hire only people who genuinely care about that purpose, check their social media and history for evidence of that care, and give them equity so they own the outcome.

### Step 5: Create a Mind Map Instead of a Business Plan

A mind map starts in the middle with your hobby or passion, then branches outward into every possible direction the business could take. Unlike a 20-page business plan, a mind map stays fluid and grows with your business. Map out podcast networks, brand partnerships, app platforms, team roles, merchandise, and distribution channels. Update it as new opportunities appear and connect dots across branches. A mind map costs nothing and keeps you nimble.

### Step 6: Delay Gratification and Build a Brand

The secret to winning long-term is refusing to monetize too early. Facebook waited nearly a decade before meaningful advertising. Build massive user value first, then monetize carefully. A brand is what people say about you when you are not in the room; a business is just an entity that files taxes. Build the brand by delivering consistent value, and the business will follow. Sacrifice short-term revenue to protect long-term trust.

### Step 7: Learn to Lose and Embrace Failure

Success is not about avoiding failure; it is about learning to lose and showing up again. Let go of short-term ego, avoid the need to look successful in front of people who do not matter, and allow yourself to be underestimated. A "D student" mindset means taking your time, questioning assumptions, and refusing to let other people define your success. The businesses that failed taught more than the wins, because failures expose blind spots that wins hide.

### Step 8: Sell the Sizzle, Not the Steak

Sales is a system, not a personality trait. The first step is research: understand exactly who you are selling to and whether they actually need what you have. The second step is connection: genuinely like the person and ensure they like you back. If either side does not feel the connection, walk away. The third step is the deal, which will usually structure itself if the first two steps are done correctly. Always think long term: the top one percent of salespeople contact leads up to five times through value-added follow-ups, not just one cold pitch.

### Step 9: Market with a Staircase Philosophy

Marketing is not one channel; it is a living system of multiple entrances into your business. Identify your "staircase" — the unique, memorable thing that makes you stand out — and evolve it over time. Do fewer channels well rather than many channels badly. Build systems around the channels that fit your personality: if you love writing, prioritize email and LinkedIn; if you love video, prioritize YouTube or TikTok. Marketing must be enjoyable or you will quit before it compounds.

### Step 10: Get Targeted PR by Making It Easy for Journalists

PR is not about ego; it is about driving tangible results. Target journalists who cover your specific niche, not mainstream outlets that will ignore your story. Write the article for them: provide a complete press release, high-resolution images, and a headline that makes their editor happy. Journalists are busy; if you hand them a finished story, they are far more likely to run it. Build relationships by engaging with their posts on Twitter before pitching, and never post anything online that would make a journalist refuse to associate with you.

### Step 11: Raise Money Using the Right Method for Your Stage

There are five main paths to capital, and most founders use the wrong one. First, family and friends can work if you are honest about the risk of total loss. Second, treat your future team members as investors: hire the best people and offer equity instead of full salary. Third, ask angels for advice, not money; create FOMO by showing them why they would be lucky to join. Fourth, target VCs only if they are actively deploying capital and have invested in similar businesses; get introduced by a portfolio company. Fifth, crowdfunding, pre-sales, or brand-funded expansion are often better than diluting equity early.

### Step 12: Get Sponsors by Understanding Brand Values

Sponsorship succeeds when you combine trackable value return with an emotional connection. Study the brand deeply before pitching: understand their existing activations, their CEO's personal interests, and their cultural values. A jewelry brand will not sponsor a beer coaster; they sponsor James Bond. Work with media buyers and agencies who already hold the brand's budget, because they can approve deals faster than direct outreach. The best sponsorship often arrives organically when you genuinely use and love a product in your daily business.

### Step 13: Build a Brand Using Reference or Leadership Models

Choose one of two scalable brand strategies. The reference model sponsors external people who already embody your values, borrowing their credibility. The leadership model puts you or a designated leader front and center as the brand ambassador. Both models carry risk: reference models fail if the influencer misbehaves, and leadership models fail if the leader leaves without a transition plan. The most important brand skill is saying no to the wrong clients, partnerships, and opportunities. One bad client can destroy a reputation built over decades.

### Step 14: Hire for Purpose, Pay with Equity, and Fire the 7s and 8s

If your business has a strong purpose and your team believes in it, you will never manage people — you will only manage alignment. Give every employee equity, not just income, so they own the outcome. Use the "seven and eight rule" to identify underperformers: if a employee is discussed more than once or twice per week in team conversations, they are likely a 7 or 8 and will drain energy from 9s and 10s. Help 7s and 8s find roles where they can be 9s and 10s, but do not let them stay and demoralize your top performers.

### Step 15: Go Global to Reduce Risk and Increase Scale

A business confined to one market is vulnerable to local economic shocks. Going global is easier than most founders believe: research where your product could sell, set up franchising or licensing arrangements so partners do the work for you, or use brand partnerships to fund expansion into new markets. A big company is often easier to run than a small one because you can afford specialists, systems, and management. Do not trap yourself in a small market because running it feels easier; it is not.

### Step 16: Get a Coach, Not a Mentor

What most founders actually need is accountability and specific answers, not a mentor who tells war stories. If you need accountability, get a co-founder. If you need sales help, hire a salesperson. If you need expertise, hire an advisor with specific knowledge and offer equity in exchange. The best way to get someone's time is to give them value first: redesign their website, introduce them to a useful contact, or solve a problem they have. Never ask for mentorship from a stranger without researching what matters to them.

### Step 17: Structure Equity Correctly from Day One

Equity ownership does not equal control; control comes from the shareholder agreement. For co-founders, use a 50/50 split with a clear decision-making process or trusted third-party tie-breaker. Avoid 52/48 splits based on who had the idea first; they create psychological resentment without real control benefits. Do not sell too much equity early, because you will need it for future fundraising rounds. Understand the difference between share options and actual equity: share options can be taken away if the company goes private, while real equity pays out in an exit. Consider using a SAFE agreement to defer valuation conversations until the business is worth more.

### Step 18: Sell Your Business by Building What You Love

The best way to maximize exit value is to build a business you never want to sell. The largest exit often happens when you are not trying to exit. Build partnerships with companies that might acquire you, let relationships mature naturally, and avoid pitching investors with an exit story. When you do sell, options include: selling to a non-competitor who sees more value in your business than your industry does, merging with a competitor for an easy but often lower-value exit, using a specialist M&A agent, or letting your management team buy the business over time. Never build a business solely to sell it; build a business you love and let the exit happen accidentally.

## Examples

### Example 1: Starting Airbnb with No Money

Airbnb launched by selling air mattresses at design conferences to attendees who needed accommodation. They did not build a platform first; they validated demand with manual labor and then used the revenue to fund technology. This execution-first approach kept risk low and proved that customers would pay for the concept before millions were spent on development.

### Example 2: Fluid Agency Pricing by Outcome

When Simon launched his agency Fluid, most competitors charged hourly. Fluid charged based on the outcome they delivered: if they helped a client sell more product, they took a percentage of the increase. This aligned incentives, removed risk from the client, and turned the agency into a partner rather than a vendor. The model required confidence in results but created far stronger long-term relationships.

### Example 3: The Staircase Marketing Campaign

Simon bought a London staircase at auction for 26,000 pounds because it symbolized step-by-step progress. The purchase generated millions in free PR from BBC, New York Times, and global newspapers. They then added a doorbell labeled "press for your dream" and partnered with Ring doorbell and Amazon for sponsorship. A single unconventional purchase created earned media, brand alignment, and sponsorship revenue simultaneously.

### Example 4: Hiring the Number Two at LinkedIn

Instead of hiring an unknown, Simon targeted the number two person at LinkedIn, reasoning that they had money to invest, talent to contribute, and motivation to prove themselves. By hiring them with equity instead of full cash compensation, he got a world-class operator, reduced cash burn, and brought in an investor who was also building the product. This approach turns every key hire into a potential capital source.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Start with passion, not a market gap; combine your obsession with a complementary partner
- ✅ Execute the simplest possible first step before building anything expensive
- ✅ Test multiple revenue models before committing to one
- ✅ Write a mind map instead of a business plan; keep it fluid and infinite
- ✅ Delay gratification and build a brand before monetizing
- ✅ Embrace failure as data; let your ego go and be willing to look like a loser early
- ✅ In sales, research the customer first, build genuine connection second, pitch third
- ✅ Follow up with leads politely and persistently for months or years
- ✅ Choose one or two marketing channels and build systems around them
- ✅ Make PR easy for journalists by writing the full story and providing assets
- ✅ Ask investors for advice and value before asking for money
- ✅ Understand brand values before pitching sponsorship
- ✅ Give employees real equity, not just options, to align incentives
- ✅ Use 50/50 co-founder splits with a clear tie-breaker process
- ✅ Structure equity with your exit goal in mind from day one
- ✅ Build a business you love so selling it happens accidentally at maximum value

## Keep In Mind

- Luck is not random; it is hackable through persistence, knowing your destination, and taking calculated risk
- Working hard does not equal success; taking risk does
- Culture eats strategy for breakfast every single time
- A "D student" who takes risks often outperforms an "A student" who fears failure
- The education system does not teach you to be free; it teaches you to work for someone else
- Marketing is meant to be fun; if you are not enjoying it, you will not sustain it
- Personal brand is not scalable for everyone; focus on building company brand where possible
- Big companies are easier to run than small ones because you can afford specialists
- You do not need a mentor; you need a coach, an advisor, or a co-founder
- Selling hours limits your life; build leverage through equity and systems instead

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not oversell investment opportunities to family and friends; clearly state they could lose everything
- Avoid investors who only care about money and have no alignment with your purpose
- Be careful with equity structure: a 52/48 split creates psychological resentment without real control benefits
- Do not let one client or brand become too large a portion of revenue; concentration creates dependency risk
- Review all shareholder agreements and equity paperwork with a qualified lawyer before signing
- Never share sensitive financial projections, cap tables, or investor terms publicly
- Be disciplined on social media; one inappropriate post can destroy brand reputation and block PR opportunities
- Ensure proper legal separation between personal and business finances from day one

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Starting with an original idea instead of your passion.
  **Solution:** Start with what you love doing and combine it with a partner who fills your gaps; execution matters more than novelty.

- **Problem:** Locking into a single revenue model before testing alternatives.
  **Solution:** Experiment with pricing, licensing, subscriptions, and outcome-based models before settling on one.

- **Problem:** Building a business plan instead of a mind map.
  **Solution:** Use a mind map that branches infinitely from your core passion; it keeps you nimble while a 20-page document anchors you to assumptions.

- **Problem:** Hiring people who do not believe in the company purpose.
  **Solution:** Check social media history, ask for references, and give equity only to those who genuinely care about the mission.

- **Problem:** Giving away too much equity too early.
  **Solution:** Structure equity with your end goal in mind; bootstrap if possible, and use SAFE agreements to avoid valuation fights in the early days.

- **Problem:** Trying to manage people instead of managing purpose.
  **Solution:** Install purpose at the center of hiring, give equity to align incentives, and let the mission drive behavior instead of rules.

- **Problem:** Selling to investors by pitching an exit story.
  **Solution:** Build a business you love and never want to sell; investors and buyers will line up for companies that founders refuse to part with.

- **Problem:** Posting impulsively on social media and damaging brand reputation.
  **Solution:** Treat every post as a permanent PR decision; journalists and customers will judge you by what you publish online.
