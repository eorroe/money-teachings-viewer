# Give Me 25 Minutes And I'll Make You Insanely Rich - 10 Habits by Simon Squibb

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills the 10 compounding habits that Simon Squibb used to build and sell 19 companies — including three for more money than he'll ever need. The habits cover how to keep ideas explainable in one sentence to a stranger, avoid being the person every decision flows through, spend at least 4 hours per day talking to customers, build your network before you need funding, seek mentors who have already achieved the specific outcome you want, resist raising investment until you have 6 months of runway or positive cash flow, anchor your business in a specific non-monetary purpose, build a community of waitlist members before launch, and hire for evidence of work ethic and mission alignment over resume credentials. Apply these habits together and you will be able to execute on business ideas that generate real revenue.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are launching a new business or side project and want to avoid the mistakes that cause new businesses without a verified customer or revenue model to fail in their first two years
- When you feel overwhelmed by multitasking, feature creep, or busyness that is not producing results
- When you are unsure whether to raise investment, hire your first employee, or pick a co-founder
- When you want a practical framework for turning an idea into recurring revenue and long-term wealth

## Steps

### Step 1: Keep It Simple

Focus on one specific pain point you can solve with a measurable outcome — for example, "help freelancers invoice 2 hours faster per week." Cut every feature, idea, or explanation that does not serve that single problem. Before trying to earn a million dollars, prove you can earn $1 in profit — once you have that working, scale becomes repeatable. Test your idea by explaining it to an 8-year-old; if they cannot understand it in one sentence, it is still too complicated.

### Step 2: Do One Thing Well

Pick one product or service and make it so reliable that 9 out of 10 customers would recommend it without being asked. Resist shiny object syndrome — say no to every trend, technology, or distraction that does not directly solve your core problem. Build a repeatable system and measure progress by results delivered, not by hours worked or tasks completed.

### Step 3: Don't Be the Bottleneck

Document your systems so others can follow them using tools like Notion or Loom. Delegate tasks where you are not the expert — hire an accountant, use automation software, or bring in a virtual assistant. Trust your team with decisions that involve less than $1,000 in spend or less than 2 hours of your review time so they build real ownership. Ask yourself every week, "What am I holding up?" and hand it off immediately.

### Step 4: Build an Obsession With Sales

Spend at least 4 hours per day talking to customers. Reframe sales as problem-solving — you are not pushing a product, you are helping someone who already has a problem you can solve. Track daily outreach with a concrete target — for example, 10 messages, 5 calls, and 2 meetings per day. Treat rejection as feedback: every "no" teaches you what to fix. Build an online presence with a website that includes your full name or business name as the domain, a professional email address on that domain, and at least one social profile with a consistent bio and profile photo, so a customer knows who you are before they speak to you.

### Step 5: Start With People, Not Products

Find co-founders and partners who share your specific core values — write down your top 3 values and look for people who demonstrate the same ones in past decisions. Skills can be taught, but shared purpose cannot. Build your network before you need it using LinkedIn, local meetups, and online communities. Ask customers what they want for your specific product category before building anything. Dropbox proved demand with a demo video before writing a single line of product code — do the same. Write a 60-second origin story about why you started this business and share it with every new customer, because people buy from people, not brands, and a specific personal reason is a stronger marketing asset than a generic brand message.

### Step 6: Surround Yourself With Mentors Who've Done It Before

You do not need a mentor you compensate financially. Follow the free content of people who have actually achieved the specific outcome you want — watch their videos, read their writing, study their decisions. When you need to ask something specific, ask one clear, well-researched question tied to a real decision you face. A person who publishes free content about their expertise will usually reply to a single thoughtful question because it takes less time than a formal mentoring request. Avoid asking someone to become your ongoing mentor — a request for ongoing mentoring is usually declined because it requires an ongoing time commitment without a clear return for the mentor.

### Step 7: Don't Rush to Raise Investment

Ignore Shark Tank and Dragon's Den narratives — money does not fix an idea that has not generated revenue from at least 10 paying customers, and the wrong investor can destroy your business. Prove demand by selling before you build anything. Tesla took pre-orders years before cars were delivered, and that proof of demand made raising money easy. Bootstrap until you have 6 months of operating runway or positive cash flow. Only take investment when you know with confidence that every $1 invested will return $3 or more — otherwise equity is the most expensive growth currency you will ever spend.

### Step 8: Build Around Purpose

Money fades, purpose lasts. Write down your exact motivation in one sentence without embellishment — if that sentence does not include a specific outcome other than profit, such as "help small retailers save 10 hours per week on inventory," you will quit when it gets hard. Align your business decisions with the specific values you wrote down. Share your origin story so people buy into you, not just your product. When temptation or shortcuts appear — especially ones that harm others — let purpose, not profit, make the final decision.

### Step 9: The Economic Future Is Community

Build a community of people who have already signed up for your waitlist or pre-ordered your product using free tools like Discord, Slack, or WhatsApp groups. Give more than you take — share free resources, insights, and connections that are directly useful to the specific problem your product solves. Create spaces where people connect with each other, not just with your brand. Let the community shape your next product: LEGO's Star Wars sets came directly from fan-made creations, not from a boardroom. Listen to what your community is building and use that signal to decide where to go next.

### Step 10: Hire for Heart, Not Skills

Skills can be taught; consistent work ethic, long-term commitment to the company mission, and honesty are harder to develop. Look for specific evidence of work ethic and reliability in every hire. Ask character-driven interview questions — for example, "If I gave you $10 million, what would you do with it?" — to reveal a person's true motivations. Promote from within when you see loyalty and honesty. Build culture first by writing down the 3 to 5 behaviors that define how your team works together, because culture eats strategy for breakfast. In an AI-driven world, the way your team members communicate, resolve conflicts, and support each other is a competitive advantage that AI tools and large corporate budgets cannot replicate because it is built on trust and shared history.

## Examples

### Example 1: Selling Before Building (Habits 4 and 7)

Before writing any code, the Dropbox team released a 3-minute demo video showing what their product would do. Within 24 hours, the waiting list grew from 5,000 to 75,000 people. That proof of demand was more persuasive than a pitch deck because it showed real customer interest, and it made the process of raising investment straightforward. Apply this habit by selling the outcome first, then building once you have paying customers or a verified waiting list.

### Example 2: Hiring for Heart Over Skills (Habit 10)

Simon Squibb hired Talia even though she had no formal credentials or prior experience in that exact job title — she was demotivated, staring at a wall in a merchandising job, and not confident in her abilities. What she had was personal connection to the problem: her partner was a struggling startup founder and her father had just been laid off after 20 years. She cared deeply. Within months she delivered higher revenue per dollar of salary than peers with more credentials, because personal investment in the problem drives the willingness to learn skills and produce results.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Start every new project by stating the single pain point it solves in one sentence
- ✅ Spend 4 or more hours per day with customers, no more than 1 hour per day on internal operations
- ✅ Track daily outreach numbers and hold yourself accountable to a minimum quota
- ✅ Bootstrap until you can prove that $1 invested returns $3 or more
- ✅ Document every system so your business does not die if you are unavailable
- ✅ Build a community before you launch a product
- ✅ Ask one thoughtful question when reaching out to a potential mentor
- ✅ Promote loyal team members before recruiting externally
- ❌ Do not add features, products, or side projects before your core offering passes the 8-year-old test: a stranger off the street can explain what it does and who it is for in one sentence
- ❌ Do not micromanage — it makes you the bottleneck and kills team ownership
- ❌ Do not raise investment to fix an idea that has not generated revenue from at least 10 paying customers or fund experiments you have not validated
- ❌ Do not hire for resume credentials when evidence of work ethic and alignment with your written mission statement are missing

## Keep In Mind

- Complexity kills momentum; simplicity builds it — the 8-year-old test is the standard you apply to every new idea before adding features: a stranger off the street can explain what it does and who it is for in one sentence
- Luck is a function of risk-taking and volume of action, not hours worked
- Rejection is data, not failure — every "no" improves your pitch, product, or target list
- Equity is your freedom — give it away as slowly and expensively as possible
- People buy from people, not brands — your written origin story and consistent personal presence across channels are assets that AI-generated content and large corporate marketing budgets cannot replicate
- Community will be an economic advantage in an AI-driven world; businesses that treat customers as members of a group with shared interests will retain customers longer because people return to groups where they have relationships, and generate more referrals because members introduce other members, compared to businesses that treat each customer as a one-time transaction
- Job-specific skills can be learned in months; consistent work ethic, reliability, and alignment with your written mission are harder to change — hire for these criteria first

## Security & Safety Notes

- Do not share customer data, pricing strategies, or confidential business metrics with people outside your team and advisors who have signed a confidentiality agreement
- When delegating or hiring, limit access to sensitive systems using role-based permissions — do not give every new team member admin access by default
- Be cautious of shortcuts involving confidential competitor information — using stolen client lists or insider pricing data may violate laws and will erode the trust that sustains long-term businesses
- Domain names and online presence affect customer trust — use reputable registrars and avoid domain names that contain numbers or hyphens in place of letters, or that are visually or phonetically similar to existing well-known brands in your industry

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Building a product in isolation for months before talking to a single customer
  **Solution:** Talk to 10 potential customers before you write code or manufacture inventory. Validate demand verbally or with a landing page before spending meaningful money on build
- **Problem:** Becoming the bottleneck because every decision, edit, or message flows through you
  **Solution:** Document your processes, delegate outcomes (not just tasks), and trust your team with decision-making authority within documented rules such as: no single expense over $1,000 without two approvals, no customer data shared outside the team, and all hiring decisions require two interviewers
- **Problem:** Raising investment too early and giving away equity for capital you have not yet learned to multiply
  **Solution:** Bootstrap until you have proven that every dollar spent generates three dollars in return. Investment is a scaling tool, not a survival mechanism
- **Problem:** Hiring for impressive resumes and technical skills while ignoring evidence of work ethic and alignment with your written mission
  **Solution:** Use character-driven interview questions, verify loyalty and reliability through past behavior examples, and promote from within before hiring externally
- **Problem:** Chasing every trend (AI, 3D reality, the next viral platform) instead of staying focused on your single core objective
  **Solution:** Say no to anything that does not directly serve your single core objective as stated in your one-sentence project description. Trends fade; compound focus on a specific goal builds enduring wealth
- **Problem:** Building in secret and assuming you know what customers want without asking
  **Solution:** Ask customers directly, test hypotheses with real conversations, and let community feedback shape your roadmap — do not rely on guesswork
