# The Missing Piece That Made Trading 5x Easier

## Overview

Trading success dramatically improves when traders form collaborative groups with complementary strengths, shared playbooks, and clear communication, because the combined capacity to monitor signals, analyze setups, and manage risk across multiple people enables opportunities and risk controls that no individual working alone can achieve. This Money Teaching synthesizes the key principles from SMB Capital's podcast discussion on how the "missing piece" of collaboration can transform trading performance. The core argument is that you do not need to be elite at every trading skill—monitoring multiple signals simultaneously, rapid decision-making, deep chart analysis, generating trade ideas, managing risk, and executing orders—because you can partner with traders who excel in the areas where you are weakest, and together your group can cover more ground than any individual could alone.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are a retail trader trading solo and want to improve results through collaboration with a small group or "pod" (a group of 2–5 traders working together)
- When you have tried team trading before but the pod or joint account dissolved due to misalignment
- When you want to understand how to identify and leverage complementary strengths in trading partners
- When you need practical steps to start or rebuild a trading pod or collaborative team
- When you want to improve communication within an existing trading team to avoid groupthink

## Steps

### Step 1: Identify Your Own Strengths and Complementary Roles

Assess your trading strengths honestly across the key categories: simultaneously monitoring multiple stocks or signals, making fast decisions under pressure, conducting deep chart and fundamental analysis, generating original trade ideas, managing risk across positions, executing orders at optimal prices, and understanding market narratives. Most traders are strong in one or two areas and weak in others. Identify which role you naturally lead. Three common roles to fill on a trading group are: (1) a technology or quantitative trader who runs backtests, writes code, builds systematic models, and applies AI to evaluate setups; (2) a market-sense trader who tracks which sectors and stocks are active, understands catalysts and news flow, and can identify high-conviction "fat pitch" setups; and (3) an execution and timing trader who recognizes when price, volume, and momentum converge for maximum expected value and acts decisively at that moment.

### Step 2: Find Potential Teammates Through Strategic Networking

Look for trading partners in environments where commitment and seriousness are already established: formal mentorship programs (such as SMB Inside Access), in-person trading summits (such as Trader for a Cause), public social-media trading communities (Twitter/X and FinTwit accounts focused on specific trading styles), Discord servers dedicated to a specific strategy (such as IPO day-one trading or swing trading), and in-house trading desk meetings. When reaching out, do not lead with "Do you want to join my team?" Instead, offer value first by sharing a project or idea you are working on that might benefit them. Use the musician analogy: rather than asking someone to join your band, ask if they want to collaborate on one song. Let the relationship grow naturally from a single successful project into an ongoing partnership.

### Step 3: Establish a Shared Playbook and Grading Process

Before trading together, align on a common playbook and a systematic process for grading setups. Every team member must understand and agree on concrete, written criteria for an A+ trade, an A trade, a B trade, and a C trade. Without this alignment, differing selectivity levels will cause conflict—one partner may see every trade as an A while the other sees most as C trades, leading to overtrading and losses. Agree on frequency, selectivity, and risk parameters. This shared framework prevents the "death by a thousand cuts" scenario where a joint account is destroyed by misalignment on trade quality.

Use these concrete definitions to anchor your grading discussions:

- **A+ Trade:** Near-perfect alignment of market environment, setup quality, and catalyst. Historical win rate of 60% or higher on identical setups. Risk-reward ratio of 3:1 or better. Conviction level of 80% or higher. Entry price within 1% of the optimal technical level. Maximum position size allowed.

- **A Trade:** Strong alignment of market environment and setup quality. Historical win rate of 55% or higher on identical setups. Risk-reward ratio of 2:1 or better. Conviction level of 70% or higher. Entry price within 2% of the optimal technical level. Standard position size.

- **B Trade:** Acceptable market environment and setup quality. Historical win rate of 50% or higher on identical setups. Risk-reward ratio of at least 1.5:1. Conviction level of 60% or higher. Entry price within 3% of optimal. Half the standard position size.

- **C Trade:** Poor market environment, marginal setup, or insufficient conviction. Historical win rate below 50%. Risk-reward ratio below 1.5:1. Conviction below 60%. No entry—do not take C trades in a joint account.

### Step 4: Build Shared Infrastructure

Create a shared workspace—such as a Notion database, Google Drive folder, or dedicated Discord channel—where all group members can log playbooks, upload annotated charts, track upcoming catalysts, and document trade reviews. The shared workspace becomes a growing library of trade examples: instead of each trader having 40 examples of a single setup type, the group collectively builds 160 examples across members. This infrastructure should also include a trade log (date, ticker, setup type, entry, exit, P&L, grade) and a review calendar so every trade is discussed on a fixed schedule.

### Step 5: Master Specific and Actionable Group Communication

The specificity and completeness of communication among group members is the most important long-term factor in group success. Every group member must communicate not just what they see in the market, but why it matters, how strongly they believe in the thesis, and what market conditions would confirm or invalidate it. Instead of saying "Oracle is relatively strong," say "Oracle is the number one stock I want everyone watching right now. The relative strength breakout could produce the biggest trade of the month if this sector rotation continues. My conviction is high (80%) because the weekly chart just broke out of a three-month base on 2x average volume." Always include the narrative, the current market environment, your conviction level expressed as a percentage, and what would cause you to change your mind. Use the Rick Rubin analogy: in music production, Rubin is famous for communicating his artistic taste and vision clearly even when he cannot play an instrument. Likewise, in trading groups, you do not need to be the best chart reader—you add value by communicating your conviction and reasoning clearly enough that your teammates can act on it.

### Step 6: Conduct Regular Group Trade Reviews

Schedule a recurring group trade review session—weekly or bi-weekly—where the group dissects each closed trade together. For every trade, discuss: the original thesis and setup grade (A+, A, B, or C), the entry price and timing relative to the optimal entry, the risk management plan, the exit reason and price, what the group would do differently next time, and whether the trade grade assigned before entry matched the outcome. This structured review surfaces factual blind spots—such as systematic overestimation of conviction or consistent late entries—and generates alternative perspectives that an individual working alone would never discover. Group review transforms "don't do that again" into "Alex entered 0.5% earlier by waiting for the VWAP test—maybe I should adopt that approach." Review sessions are where real skill compounding happens.

### Step 7: Maintain Honest Communication and Avoid Groupthink Within the Group

Commit to honest, unfiltered feedback within the group. If a partner brings a trade that you grade as a C while they see it as an A, speak up. You can always take the trade on your own personal account, but do not let groupthink override your conviction. The group should welcome disagreement because it sharpens decision-making. Avoid partners who place trades without communicating first, who rarely share their analysis, or who consistently contribute a negative or dismissive attitude. You want good listeners with positive attitudes who add analytical value to discussions. If a collaboration is not working, be willing to walk away or restructure it.

## Examples

### Example 1: IPO Day-One Trading Group

A group of four traders collaborated on IPO day-one entries where each member contributed a different strength: one trader had discretionary IPO experience and placed the opening order, another backtested historical IPO price targets to establish profit-taking levels, a third managed stop-loss placement based on the stock's lack of prior volatility data, and a fourth added to positions with aggressive orders when the stock broke above the opening range. Together they traded BLSH (Biolase Holdings, a medical-laser company) on its IPO day, which opened at $85 and closed at $67, using a coordinated strategy that no single trader could have executed alone. The combined capacity allowed them to cover signal identification, catalyst analysis, backtesting, execution timing, and position management simultaneously.

### Example 2: Failed Two-Person Joint Account

A two-person collaboration failed because the partners had different playbooks and no systematic grading process. One partner was a high-frequency scalper taking 5-6 trades per day with low risk-reward ratios, while the other was a selective trader waiting for high-conviction "fat pitch" setups. Without alignment, the account was overtraded to its end through groupthink—the selective trader deferred to the scalper's enthusiasm instead of upholding selectivity standards. The lesson is that complementary strengths require a shared process and clear trade grading criteria to succeed.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Align on a common playbook and systematic trade grading criteria before trading together
- ✅ Communicate conviction, context, and urgency—not just raw observations
- ✅ Start with small collaborative projects before formalizing a team
- ✅ Build shared infrastructure (Notion, shared playbooks, trade logs) early
- ✅ Conduct regular group trade reviews to surface alternative perspectives
- ✅ Find teammates with complementary strengths, not clones of yourself
- ✅ Be willing to say no to trades even if your partner is highly convicted
- ✅ Network in committed communities (mentorship programs, summits, chat rooms)
- ✅ Let team roles emerge naturally rather than forcing rigid job descriptions

## Keep In Mind

- The myth that solo trading is always superior is widespread but often wrong for traders who work best in collaborative settings.
- You do not need to be elite at every trading skill—you only need to be elite at your own strengths and find partners who cover the rest. Per SMB Capital's reported data, traders in their collaborative program achieve success rates 20 to 40 times higher than independent retail traders, largely because of the shared playbook, peer review, and accountability that a group provides.
- The quality, frequency, and specificity of communication among group members is the single strongest predictor of long-term group success.
- Shared knowledge compounds—you will gain more from openly sharing setups and feedback than you will lose by giving others access to your ideas.
- A group of four traders covering IPO day-one entries collectively monitors pre-market activity, backtests historical price targets, manages stop-loss placement, and executes aggressive add orders simultaneously—coverage that no single trader could reliably achieve alone.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Forming a team without aligning on playbook and trade grading criteria
  **Solution:** Agree on A+, A, B, and C trade definitions before placing a single joint trade. Establish frequency and selectivity standards explicitly.
- **Problem:** Groupthink overriding individual conviction
  **Solution:** Commit to honest, unfiltered feedback. If you grade a trade as a C while your partner sees an A, speak up. It is okay to take the trade personally but not as a joint account.
- **Problem:** Incomplete communication that omits conviction level, market context, and reasoning
  **Solution:** Always explain the "why" behind every call. Include the market narrative, your conviction level expressed as a percentage, and the specific conditions that would confirm or invalidate the thesis. Make every message actionable so teammates can act on it without follow-up questions.
- **Problem:** Overtrading due to misaligned selectivity between partners
  **Solution:** Set hard rules on maximum trade frequency and minimum risk-reward thresholds for joint accounts.
- **Problem:** Cloning teammates with identical strengths and playbooks
  **Solution:** Seek out traders with opposite strengths—if you are a quant, find a discretionary trader. If you are a scalper, find a swing trader.
- **Problem:** Relying on a single teammate for all ideas or all execution
  **Solution:** Encourage idea generation and input from every team member. Multiple perspectives improve filtering and reduce missed opportunities.
