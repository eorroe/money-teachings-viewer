# The Missing Piece: How Collaboration Made Trading Easier for SMB Capital Traders

## Overview

Trading success improves (measured as improved risk-adjusted returns through shared analysis and accountability) when traders form collaborative groups with complementary strengths, shared playbooks, and specific and complete communication, because the combined capacity to monitor signals, analyze setups, and manage risk across multiple people enables opportunities and risk controls that an individual working alone would be unlikely to achieve alone. This Money Teaching synthesizes the key principles from SMB Capital's podcast discussion on how the "missing piece" of collaboration can transform trading performance. The core argument is that you do not need to be strong at every trading skill—monitoring setups, making decisions under pressure, deep chart analysis, understanding market narratives, generating trade ideas, managing risk, and executing orders—because you can partner with traders who excel in the areas where you are less strong, and together your group can handle more tasks and opportunities than any individual could alone. These skills map to the three roles in Step 1 as follows: technology/quantitative trader (deep chart analysis, understanding market narratives, generating trade ideas); market-sense trader (monitoring setups, understanding catalysts and news flow, identifying high-conviction "fat pitch" setups); execution and timing trader (making decisions under pressure, executing orders, managing risk).

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you are a retail trader trading solo and want to improve results through collaboration with a small group or "pod" (a small group of traders working together)
- When you have tried team trading before but the pod or joint account dissolved due to differing goals or selectivity
- When you want to understand how to identify and leverage complementary strengths in trading partners
- When you need practical steps to start or rebuild a trading pod or collaborative team
- When you want to improve communication within an existing trading team to avoid groupthink

## Steps

### Step 1: Identify Your Own Strengths and Complementary Roles

Assess your trading strengths across the specific categories: simultaneously monitoring multiple stocks or signals, making decisions under pressure, conducting deep chart and fundamental analysis, generating trade ideas, managing risk across positions, executing orders at the best available prices, and understanding market narratives. Traders are strong in one or two areas (of the seven skills listed above) and less strong in others. Identify which role you tend to lead. Three roles to fill on a trading group are: (1) a technology/quantitative trader who runs backtests, writes code, builds systematic models, and applies AI to evaluate setups; (2) a market-sense trader who tracks which sectors and stocks are active, understands catalysts and news flow, and can identify high-conviction "fat pitch" setups; and (3) an execution and timing trader who recognizes when price, volume, and momentum converge for the highest potential gain relative to risk and acts decisively at that moment.

### Step 2: Find Potential Teammates Through Strategic Networking

Look for trading partners in environments where commitment to the process is established: formal mentorship programs (such as SMB Inside Access), in-person trading summits (such as Trader for a Cause), public social-media trading communities (Twitter/X and FinTwit accounts focused on specific trading styles), Discord servers dedicated to a specific strategy (such as IPO day-one trading or swing trading), and in-house trading desk meetings. When reaching out, do not lead with "Do you want to join my team?" Instead, offer value first by sharing a project or idea you are working on that might benefit them. Use the musician analogy: rather than asking someone to join your band, ask if they want to collaborate on one song. Let the relationship grow from a single successful project into an ongoing partnership.

### Step 3: Establish a Shared Playbook and Grading Process

Before trading together, align on a shared playbook and a consistent grading process. Every team member must understand and agree on concrete, written criteria for an A+ trade, an A trade, a B trade, and a C trade. Without this alignment, differing selectivity levels can cause conflict—one partner may see more than 50% of trades as A-grade while the other sees more than 50% as C-grade, leading to trading more than the agreed-upon frequency, which reduces returns. Agree on trade frequency, selectivity standards, and risk limits. This shared framework prevents the "gradual losses from repeated small misaligned trades" scenario where a joint account is eroded by repeated trades that do not meet shared quality standards.

Use these concrete definitions to anchor your grading discussions:

- **A+ Trade:** Near-perfect alignment of market environment, setup quality, and catalyst, as defined by the group's shared rubric. Risk-reward ratio of at least 3:1. Conviction level of at least 80%. Entry price within 1% of the key technical level identified in the setup. Maximum position size allowed as defined by the group's risk management agreement.

- **A Trade:** Strong alignment of market environment and setup quality, as defined by the group's shared rubric. Risk-reward ratio of at least 2:1. Conviction level of at least 70%. Entry price within 2% of the key technical level identified in the setup. The standard position size agreed upon by the group.

 - **B Trade:** Acceptable market environment and setup quality, as defined by the group's shared rubric. Risk-reward ratio of at least 1.5:1. Conviction level of at least 60%. Entry price within 3% of the key level identified in the setup. Half of the group's standard position size (which is 1% of total capital per trade); the group's standard position size is 1% of total capital per trade for all trade grades unless otherwise specified.

- **C Trade:** Poor market environment, marginal setup, or insufficient conviction. Historical win rate below 50%. Risk-reward ratio below 1.5:1. Conviction below 60%. No entry—do not take C trades in a joint account.

### Step 4: Build Shared Infrastructure

Create a shared workspace—such as a Notion database, Google Drive folder, or dedicated Discord channel—where all group members can log playbooks, upload annotated charts, track upcoming catalysts, and document trade reviews. The shared workspace becomes a growing library of trade examples: the group collectively builds more examples together than any individual could alone. This infrastructure should also include a trade log (date, ticker, setup type, entry, exit, Profit and Loss (P&L), grade) and a review calendar so every trade is discussed at least weekly.

### Step 5: Master Specific and Actionable Group Communication

The specificity and completeness of communication among group members is one of the most important factors in the long-term success of a pod or joint account according to SMB Capital's observed outcomes. Every group member must communicate not just what they see in the market, but why it matters, how strongly they believe in the thesis, and what market conditions would confirm or invalidate it. Instead of vague statements like "Oracle is relatively strong," say "Oracle is the number one stock I want everyone watching right now" with specific narrative and conviction. In detailed communications, include the narrative, the current market environment, your conviction level expressed as a percentage, and what would cause you to change your mind. In a trading pod, you can add value through conviction and communication even without being the strongest chart reader, similar to how a producer shapes an album without being the performer. Likewise, in trading groups, you do not need to be the best chart reader—you add value by communicating your conviction and reasoning so your teammates can act on it.

### Step 6: Conduct Regular Group Trade Reviews

Schedule a recurring group trade review session—weekly or every two weeks—where the group dissects each closed trade together. For every trade, discuss: the original thesis and setup grade, the entry price and timing, the risk management plan, the exit reason and price, what the group would do differently next time, and whether the trade grade matched the outcome. This structured review surfaces oversights in the trade assessment—such as consistent overestimation of confidence in setups (grading a trade as A when it scores below 80 on the shared rubric (for example, in observed SMB Capital cases where a trader's conviction exceeds the rubric score)) or entering trades later than the planned point, for example, in observed cases—and generates alternative perspectives that an individual working alone might miss. Group review transforms "don't do that again" into "A teammate entered earlier by waiting for a VWAP test confirmation—maybe I should adopt that approach." Review sessions are where measurable skill improvement happens (track improvement by comparing trade grades before and after review sessions).

### Step 7: Maintain Honest Communication and Avoid Groupthink Within the Group

Commit to direct feedback on trade quality and reasoning within the group. If a partner brings a trade that you grade as a C while they see it as an A, speak up. You can take the trade on your own personal account, but do not let groupthink override your conviction. The group should welcome disagreement. Avoid partners who place trades without communicating first, who do not contribute analysis to discussions, or who consistently reject ideas without explanation, engage in personal attacks, or refuse to engage with shared analysis. You want engaged team members who contribute constructively and add insight that improves the group's analysis. If a collaboration is not working, be willing to walk away or restructure it (restructure options include changing the playbook, changing roles, changing membership, or changing the account structure).

## Examples

### Example 1: IPO Day-One Trading Group

A group of four traders collaborated on IPO day-one entries where each member contributed a different strength: one trader had discretionary IPO experience and placed the opening order, another backtested historical IPO price targets to establish profit-taking levels, a third managed stop-loss placement based on the stock's lack of prior volatility data, and a fourth added to positions with aggressive orders when the stock broke above the opening range. Together they traded BLSH (Biolase Holdings, a medical-laser company) on its IPO day, which opened at $85 and closed at $67, using a coordinated strategy that would be difficult for a single trader to execute alone. The combined capacity allowed them to handle signal identification, catalyst analysis, backtesting, execution timing, and position management simultaneously.

### Example 2: Failed Two-Person Joint Account

A two-person collaboration failed because the partners had different playbooks and no systematic grading process. One partner was a high-frequency scalper taking 5-6 trades per day with risk-reward ratios below 1.5:1, while the other was a selective trader waiting for high-conviction "fat pitch" setups. Without alignment, the account was depleted to the point of closure through excessive trading through groupthink—the selective trader deferred to the scalper's enthusiasm instead of upholding selectivity standards. The case shows that complementary strengths benefit from a shared process and clear trade grading criteria to succeed.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Align on a shared playbook and systematic trade grading criteria before trading together
- ✅ Communicate conviction, context, and urgency—not just raw observations
- ✅ Start with single-setup analysis projects (projects involving one trade idea and one shared analysis) before formalizing a team
- ✅ Build shared infrastructure (Notion, shared playbooks, trade logs) early
- ✅ Conduct weekly group trade reviews to surface alternative perspectives
- ✅ Find teammates with complementary strengths, not clones of yourself
- ✅ Be willing to say no to trades even if your partner is highly convicted
- ✅ Network in communities with structured mentorship and regular meetings (such as SMB Inside Access, Trader for a Cause summits, or trading Discord servers with weekly review sessions)
- ✅ Let team roles emerge rather than forcing rigid job descriptions

## Keep In Mind

- The myth that solo trading is superior for all traders is a common belief among retail traders but frequently incorrect for traders who work best in collaborative settings.
- You do not need to be strong at every trading skill—you only need to be strong at your own strengths and find partners who cover the rest. Traders working in groups have been observed to improve risk-adjusted returns through shared playbooks, peer review, and accountability (SMB Capital traders working in groups have demonstrated this improvement).
- The quality, frequency, and specificity of communication among group members is a significant predictor of long-term group success.
- Shared knowledge grows over time—traders often gain more from openly sharing setups and feedback than they lose by giving others access to their ideas.
- A group of four traders covering IPO day-one entries collectively monitors pre-market activity, backtests historical price targets, manages stop-loss placement, and executes aggressive add orders simultaneously—capability that would be difficult for a single trader to reliably achieve alone.

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Forming a team without aligning on playbook and trade grading criteria
  **Solution:** Agree on A+, A, B, and C trade definitions before placing a single joint trade. Establish frequency and selectivity standards explicitly.
- **Problem:** Groupthink overriding individual conviction
  **Solution:** Commit to direct feedback on trade quality and reasoning. If you grade a trade as a C while your partner sees an A, speak up. It is okay to take the trade on your own personal account.
- **Problem:** Incomplete communication that omits conviction level, market context, and reasoning
  **Solution:** Always explain the "why" behind every call. Include the market narrative, your conviction level expressed as a percentage, and the specific conditions that would confirm or invalidate the thesis. Make every message specific enough that teammates can act on it without follow-up questions.
- **Problem:** Overtrading due to misaligned selectivity between partners
  **Solution:** Set documented rules with specific thresholds for maximum trade frequency and minimum risk-reward on joint accounts.
- **Problem:** Cloning teammates with identical strengths and playbooks
  **Solution:** Seek out traders with different strengths, such as a quant and a discretionary trader. If you are a scalper, find a swing trader.
- **Problem:** Relying on a single teammate for all ideas or all execution
  **Solution:** Encourage idea generation and input from every team member. Multiple perspectives improve filtering and reduce missed opportunities.
