1: # Increasing Your Financial IQ (financial intelligence)
2: 
3: ## Overview
4: 
5: This Money Teaching distills the core financial education principles discussed by Donald Trump and Robert Kiyosaki on increasing your Financial IQ. It argues that traditional financial advice like "get out of debt" and "live below your means" can be misleading without proper context, and it explains how financial intelligence determines whether an asset truly creates wealth. The goal is to shift your mindset from trading time for money to understanding how money works so you can make informed investment decisions.
6: 
7: ## When to Follow These Money Teachings
8: 
9: - When you need to evaluate whether an investment is truly an asset or a liability
10: - When working with debt, real estate, or any investment vehicle
11: - When the user asks how to build wealth beyond traditional savings and conventional advice
12: - When you want to understand why smart people still lose money in "safe" investments
13: 
14: ## Steps
15: 
16: ### Step 1: Redefine What an Asset Is
17: 
18: Understand that, in this framework, your primary residence is not considered an asset—it is categorized as a liability. When you cannot make your house payments, it becomes your biggest financial burden. An asset puts money in your pocket whether you work or not; a liability takes money out. Apply this definition consistently to everything you own or are considering buying.
19: 
20: ### Step 2: Use Debt Strategically, Not Blindly
21: 
22: Debt used without financial education can result in losses; good debt (debt used to acquire income-producing assets) can increase wealth. The key difference is financial education. Do not follow the blanket advice to "get out of debt"—instead, evaluate whether the debt you carry finances assets that generate income exceeding the cost of that debt. The more debt you use, the more rigorously you must evaluate deals, interest rates, and cash flow.
23: 
24: ### Step 3: Expand Your Means Instead of Living Below Them
25: 
26: "Live below your means" can restrict options and limit growth potential. Rather than restricting your lifestyle, invest in financial education so you can expand your means without taking on debt used for consumption rather than investment. Study financial principles daily—your knowledge is your greatest asset. Use specific goals and role models to build confidence and track measurable financial metrics that motivate you to improve.
27: 
28: ### Step 4: Achieve Goals First, Then Decide If You Still Want Them
29: 
30: Build the capability to acquire the things you want—luxury homes, investments, experiences—then decide if you still want them. The teaching suggests the pain is not in having; the pain is in wanting something you cannot afford. Achieve that capability, and you can take it or leave it from a position of strength.
31: 
32: ### Step 5: Develop Your Financial IQ to Evaluate Investments
33: 
34: Financial intelligence determines whether an investment is suitable for your financial goals. You can lose money or make money in gold, real estate, stocks, and oil. Without strong financial literacy, you cannot evaluate whether an investment aligns with your goals, or whether advice is appropriate for your situation. Study cash flow statements, study core financial terms and concepts, and practice evaluating deals before committing capital.
35: 
36: ## Examples
37: 
38: ### Example 1: Evaluating a Home Purchase
39: 
40: Before buying a house, calculate the monthly cash flow. If the property does not generate positive cash flow after mortgage, taxes, insurance, and maintenance, treat it as a liability. Consider using bank debt (good debt) to acquire a rental property that pays you monthly, rather than draining your savings for a personal residence that costs you money.
41: 
42: ### Example 2: Responding to Market Downturns
43: 
44: When conservative stocks drop 50% in value, do not assume the asset class itself is bad. A person with strong financial literacy sees the opportunity; a person with limited financial literacy sees only loss. Use downturns to practice evaluating whether the underlying business still has positive cash flow, and whether the price now reflects a margin of safety (the difference between an asset's purchase price and its intrinsic value).
45: 
46: ## Best Practices
47: 
48: - ✅ Study financial statements (income statement, balance sheet, cash flow) regularly
49: - ✅ Study financial principles daily through books, mentors, and real deal analysis
50: - ✅ Use good debt to acquire cash-flowing assets like income-producing real estate
51: - ✅ Treat financial literacy as a skill—learn to speak it fluently through practice and experience
52: - ❌ Do not accept "live below your means" as a permanent strategy without building financial intelligence first
53: - ❌ Do not assume your house is automatically a good investment just because home prices have risen over the past 20 years
54: - ❌ Do not take investment advice from people who lack direct experience in the asset class they are recommending
55: 
56: ## Keep In Mind
57: 
58: - Financial IQ is measured by your ability to solve financial problems, not by how much money you currently have
59: - According to this teaching, debt applied with financial education can build wealth, while debt applied without that education can increase financial burdens
60: - Learning to build wealth requires the same commitment as learning any complex skill—it takes time, practice, and dedication
61: - Your knowledge is a powerful financial asset—choose teachers wisely and continue learning throughout your life
62: - Achieve that capability first, then decide if you still want those things. A constraint-focused approach may limit potential; purposeful ambition may expand it.
63: 
64: ## Security & Safety Notes
65: 
66: - Never invest based on hot tips from unqualified sources, especially those within the employee quadrant (the E quadrant in the cash flow quadrant framework (a model categorizing income sources as Employee, Self-Employed, Business Owner, or Investor)) who lack real investing experience
67: - Verify the track record of anyone giving financial advice—look for someone who has successfully done what they are teaching
68: - Be aware that some financial advisors operate on a "sell" model rather than a "teach" model; prioritize education over product recommendations
69: - Understand the tax implications and legal structure of any investment before committing substantial capital
70: 
71: ## Common Pitfalls
72: 
73: - **Problem:** Buying a house and calling it an asset without understanding cash flow or opportunity cost
74:   **Solution:** Calculate true cash flow after all expenses. If the property does not pay you monthly, classify it correctly as a liability and factor that into your wealth-building plan.
75: - **Problem:** Following the "live below your means" mantra without expanding financial knowledge, leading to a scarcity mindset
76:   **Solution:** Replace restriction with education. Invest time and money into learning how to generate more income through assets rather than simply cutting expenses.
77: - **Problem:** Losing money in conservative stocks and blaming the asset class instead of the lack of financial intelligence
78:   **Solution:** Recognize that any investment can lose money. Focus on building Financial IQ so you can distinguish between a temporary price drop and a permanently impaired asset.
79: - **Problem:** Taking investment advice from people who have never successfully invested in the asset class they are recommending
80:   **Solution:** Seek mentors and teachers who have a documented track record—such as completed investment returns, consistent deal flow, or audited results—in the specific investment type you are studying. Prioritize experience over credentials.
