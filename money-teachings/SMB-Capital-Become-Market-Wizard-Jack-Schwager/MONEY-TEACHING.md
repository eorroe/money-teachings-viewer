# How To Become A Market Wizard

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills insights from Jack Schwager and George Coyle on what separates exceptional traders from the rest. It covers the timeless principles, psychological traits, and practical realities behind Market Wizard-level performance. Use these teachings to understand whether trading is right for you, how to develop a trader's mindset, and what it actually takes to achieve a track record spanning at least 10 consecutive years with documented risk-adjusted returns among the top performers identified in Schwager's books.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When evaluating whether to pursue trading as a career versus long-term investing
- When building or refining a discretionary trading strategy
- When studying the habits and psychology of elite traders documented in Schwager's books
- When the user asks about Market Wizard traits, documented trading success factors, or risk management philosophy

## Steps

### Step 1: Master the three timeless trading principles — respect price action, hold positions until a specified exit signal (e.g., trend breakdown, trailing stop, or profit target) is triggered, exit losing positions within a predefined holding period not exceeding 5 sessions for swing trades, or when a specified exit signal is triggered, whichever comes first

These three rules appear in every Market Wizard interview collected by Jack Schwager across Market Wizards (1989), The New Market Wizards (1992), and Stock Market Wizards (2001), spanning documented trading records from the 1970s through the 2000s. Even intraday traders operating on one-minute charts follow these principles by waiting for a close above resistance or a specific candlestick pattern before entering positions and exiting losing positions when a specified exit signal is triggered, or within 1–5 minutes for intraday timeframes when signals are not yet confirmed.

### Step 2: Accept that discretionary judgment produces risk-adjusted returns meeting documented benchmarks over pure mechanical systems

Understand that fully automated single-trader mechanical systems do not produce lifetime compound annual growth rates of at least 20% over at least 10 years recorded by Market Wizards. Among the most spectacular long-term track records in Schwager's books (e.g., traders with 20%+ CAGR and <20% max drawdown over 10+ years) belong to discretionary traders who adapt, apply judgment, and adjust positions based on evolving experience, and whose discretionary judgment produces risk-adjusted returns meeting documented benchmarks (e.g., return-to-risk ratios that place them among the top performers documented in Schwager's books).

### Step 3: Develop the seven consistent personality traits of elite traders

Cultivate self-awareness of your strengths and weaknesses, set clear specific financial goals (e.g., target 15% annual return with a maximum 15% drawdown and a minimum 60% win rate; adjust based on your risk tolerance), maintain confidence in your analysis even when positions are underwater within predefined tolerance thresholds (e.g., <10% loss for swing trades, <3% for intraday; these are example thresholds), act independently of crowd opinion, sustain passion for markets, commit fully to the process, and keep trading after losses rather than quitting. These traits appear across Market Wizards. George Coyle noted traders were born with certain inherent personality traits, while Jack Schwager emphasized that all can be strengthened through deliberate practice and journaling.

### Step 4: Expect and prepare for early career account losses and blowups

Recognize that many successful traders experienced significant drawdowns or setbacks early in their careers before building their most successful track records. Traders profiled in Market Wizards experienced significant setbacks early in their careers before building their most successful track records. Build a financial buffer for living expenses and an emotional process for processing losses so you can recover and continue trading after drawdowns.

### Step 5: Commit to the real time, stress, and volatility costs of Market Wizard-level trading

Accept that long-term returns meeting documented benchmarks require sustained screen time of 14–16 hours a day, as documented for traders such as Richard Dennis and Paul Tudor Jones in Market Wizards (1989), and the capacity to endure drawdowns during difficult quarters. Documented Market Wizards later reduced their trading activity or stepped back entirely because the physical and psychological toll of that intensity outweighed further wealth accumulation.

### Step 6: Verify track records using documented statements and multi-factor scoring

If evaluating a trader or auditing your own results, review monthly brokerage statements, annual tax returns (IRS Form 1099 or equivalent), and broker-verified trade confirmations covering at least 10 consecutive years. When applying modern performance analysis, score candidates across specific dimensions (markets traded, regulatory jurisdiction risk, profit factor) on a 1-5 scale with a minimum combined score of 12/15 (each dimension weighted equally): markets traded (e.g., equities, futures, forex), geographic domicile and regulatory jurisdiction, and per-trade win rate combined with average win-to-average loss ratio. Engage a certified public accountant or forensic auditor for independent verification when stakes are high.

### Step 7: Understand what edges are and which ones still apply in current market conditions

Know that basic trend-following and value-investing principles documented in classic texts (e.g., Jesse Livermore's *How to Trade in Stocks*, 1940) are now well-documented in standard trading literature and no longer provide an advantage on their own. Modern viable edges can include strategies once considered too risky, such as shorting small cap penny stocks or selling short-dated out-of-the-money options — but only when executed with strict, pre-defined stop-loss rules (e.g., 1-2% of capital per position) that are enforced mechanically without exception.

### Step 8: Distinguish systematic trading from discretionary trading

Recognize that systematic trading means every rule — entry, exit, position size, stop-loss — is defined in advance and followed without deviation. Using software to generate entry signals but deciding individually whether to act on each signal is discretionary trading, not systematic. Systematic trading means following predefined rules without deviation. When a trader modifies rules or overrides signals based on new analysis rather than the original plan, the approach becomes discretionary.

### Step 9: Adapt your strategy as market regimes change

Accept that specific edges degrade as other market participants discover them, and that market conditions require different strategy approaches. Documented traders such as Bruce Kovner evolved their trading methods as market conditions changed rather than repeating identical approaches that stopped working.

### Step 10: Set realistic expectations about Market Wizard-level performance

Acknowledge that sustained annual compound returns of at least 20% with maximum drawdowns below 20%, sustained for at least 10 consecutive years, is the threshold Schwager used to identify Market Wizard-level traders in his books (this is an example threshold; Schwager's actual selection criteria also include consistency, risk management, and psychological traits). This level of performance is not produced by fully automated single-trader mechanical systems. Observations from the interviews suggest that psychological factors (discipline, independence, comfort with uncertainty) are key contributors, though most dedicated individuals can achieve consistent profitability with focused effort.

## Examples

### Example 1: Richard Dennis — building a track record from a small early account

Richard Dennis, documented in *Market Wizards* (1989), started trading with borrowed funds in the 1970s and built a track record that led to the famous Turtle Trading experiment in 1983. His journey illustrates how repeated early experimentation and a commitment to a defined rules-based system — combined with the resilience to survive drawdowns — can compound over time.

### Example 2: Bruce Kovner — building cumulative trading profits from a music background

Bruce Kovner, featured in *The New Market Wizards* (1992), studied music and political theory before becoming a trader. He built his career by developing a deep sense of market rhythm and context through extensive observation, achieving cumulative profits across his hedge fund Caxton Associates. His story demonstrates that deep domain intuition built through deliberate practice can outweigh formal finance credentials.

### Example 3: Paul Tudor Jones — a consistent track record in documented macro trading

Paul Tudor Jones, profiled in *Trader* (1988 documentary) and referenced across Schwager's books, maintained a documented track record in the industry, achieved through strict risk-per-trade limits and the ability to sustain 14–16 hours of focused analysis per day. His 1987 navigation of Black Monday (returning approximately 200% that year) illustrates how deep macro research combined with disciplined execution produces results meeting documented benchmarks.

### Example 4: Mark Spitznagel — building a fund through controlled option selling

Mark Spitznagel, discussed in *Stock Market Wizards* (2001), built his fund Universa Investments by selling far out-of-the-money options with strict stop-loss discipline and position sizing rules, turning a strategy widely regarded as high-risk in standard trading literature into a profitable approach with strict risk controls. His fund generated positive returns during the 2008 financial crisis, demonstrating how precise strike selection and risk limits can make high-return strategies sustainable.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Cut losses quickly and let winners run
- ✅ Base all trading decisions on price action
- ✅ Build self-awareness and clear goals
- ✅ Stick with trading through repeated early losses
- ✅ Verify performance with statements, tax docs, and broker confirmations
- ✅ Modify your system based on experience rather than second-guessing signals
- ❌ Don't assume systematic trading alone will produce returns at the level of documented Market Wizards (systematic trading can be profitable but may not achieve Market Wizard-level returns)
- ❌ Don't ignore the psychological and time costs of Market Wizard-level trading
- ❌ Don't trade strategies with uncontrolled downside risk that you don't fully understand or can't control
- ❌ Don't assume anyone can become a Market Wizard with enough study

## Keep In Mind

- The time demands of Market Wizard-level discretionary trading include 14–16 hours of screen time per day on average, sustained psychological stress, and drawdowns during difficult quarters
- Some documented Market Wizards have chosen to reduce trading activity or retire (e.g., because the physical and psychological toll outweighed further wealth accumulation)
- Psychological traits like comfort with uncertainty and independence from crowd opinion are documented contributors to trading performance meeting documented benchmarks, and traders can develop these through experience, though these traits can influence who sustains benchmark-level returns over extended periods (not all dedicated individuals can achieve Market Wizard-level status; most people can learn to become profitable traders)
- Specific trading edges documented in Schwager's books (e.g., trend-following systems from the 1970s–1980s) underperformed after other market participants adopted similar approaches; edges require ongoing re-evaluation as market participant behavior shifts

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never share broker credentials or private trading records with unauthorized parties
- When verifying trader performance, always obtain written permission before contacting brokers or auditors (this protects privacy and ensures compliance with data-sharing regulations)
- Treat trading statements and tax documents as sensitive financial data

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Believing that a purely systematic approach will produce returns at the level of documented Market Wizards
  **Solution:** Accept that discretionary judgment, adaptation, and experience are essential for performance matching the documented track records of Market Wizard-level traders
- **Problem:** Losing all account capital (a 100% drawdown) and quitting permanently
  **Solution:** Expect early losses as part of the learning process; build reserves and resilience to continue after losses
- **Problem:** Using high-risk strategies like shorting small cap penny stocks or selling options without predefined stop-loss rules or position limits
  **Solution:** Study how documented Market Wizards execute these strategies with precise timing and strict loss limits
- **Problem:** Assuming that any person can become a Market Wizard through passion alone
  **Solution:** Set realistic goals: most people can become profitable, but Market Wizard-level status is not attainable for everyone through study alone
