# How To Become A Market Wizard

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills insights from Jack Schwager and George Coyle on what separates exceptional traders from the rest. It covers the timeless principles, psychological traits, and practical realities behind Market Wizard-level performance. Use these teachings to understand whether trading is right for you, how to develop a trader's mindset, and what it actually takes to achieve a track record spanning at least 10 consecutive years with documented risk-adjusted returns among the top performers identified in Schwager's books (traders such as Paul Tudor Jones, Bruce Kovner, and Richard Dennis with documented 20%+ CAGR and <20% max drawdown over 10+ years (as documented in Jack Schwager's Market Wizards series of books)).

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When evaluating whether to pursue trading as a career versus long-term investing
- When building or refining a discretionary trading strategy
- When studying the habits and psychology of elite traders documented in Schwager's books
- When the user asks about Market Wizard traits, documented trading success factors, or risk management philosophy

## Steps

### Step 1: Master the three timeless trading principles — respect price action, hold positions until a specified exit signal (e.g., trend breakdown, trailing stop, or profit target) is triggered, exit losing positions within a predefined holding period not exceeding 5 trading sessions for swing trades (positions held for more than one day), or when a specified exit signal is triggered, whichever comes first

These three rules appear in every Market Wizard interview collected by Jack Schwager across Market Wizards (1989), The New Market Wizards (1992), and Stock Market Wizards (2001), spanning documented trading records from the 1970s through 2009. Even intraday traders operating on one-minute charts follow these principles by waiting for a close above resistance or a specific candlestick pattern before entering positions and exit losing positions when a specified exit signal is triggered. For intraday timeframes, require validation by a follow-through candle before entering positions (these principles are documented in Schwager's Market Wizards interviews with Paul Tudor Jones, Bruce Kovner, and other documented traders).

### Step 2: Accept that discretionary judgment produces risk-adjusted returns meeting documented benchmarks (return-to-risk ratios that place them among the top performers documented in Schwager's books) over pure mechanical systems

Understand that fully automated single-trader mechanical systems do not produce lifetime compound annual growth rates of at least 20% over at least 10 years recorded by Market Wizards. Among the most spectacular long-term track records in Schwager's books (e.g., traders with 20%+ CAGR and <20% max drawdown over 10+ years) belong to discretionary traders who adapt, apply judgment, and adjust positions based on evolving experience, and whose discretionary judgment produces risk-adjusted returns meeting documented benchmarks (consistent profitability of at least 20% annually with drawdowns under 20%) (e.g., return-to-risk ratios that place them among the top performers documented in Schwager's books).

### Step 3: Develop the seven consistent personality traits of elite traders

Cultivate self-awareness of your strengths and weaknesses, set clear specific financial goals (e.g., target 15% annual return with a maximum 15% drawdown and a minimum 60% win rate; these are example targets; set your own based on strategy and risk tolerance); select your risk-profile category from this framework based on your risk tolerance: conservative = 10% annual return target with 10% max drawdown; moderate = 15% annual return target with 15% max drawdown; aggressive = 20% annual return target with 20% max drawdown; corresponding win rate targets are strategy-dependent and should be set based on your historical performance), maintain confidence in your analysis even when positions are underwater within predefined tolerance thresholds (e.g., <10% loss for multi-session positions, <3% for intraday; set your own thresholds based on strategy and risk tolerance), act independently of crowd opinion, sustain passion for markets, commit fully to the process, and keep trading after losses rather than quitting. These traits appear across Market Wizards. George Coyle noted traders were born with certain inherent personality traits, while Jack Schwager emphasized that all can be strengthened through deliberate practice and journaling.

### Step 4: Expect and prepare for early career account losses and blowups

Recognize that many successful traders experienced significant drawdowns or setbacks within the first 1-3 years of trading before building their most successful track records. Traders profiled in Market Wizards experienced drawdowns of 30% or more within the first 1-3 years of trading before building their most successful track records. Build a financial buffer for living expenses and an emotional process for processing losses so you can recover and continue trading after drawdowns.

### Step 5: Commit to the real time, stress, and volatility costs of Market Wizard-level trading

Accept that long-term returns meeting documented benchmarks require sustained screen time (documented Market Wizards such as Richard Dennis and Paul Tudor Jones sustained 14–16 hours per day during their most active trading years (specifically the mid-to-late 1980s as described in Market Wizards, 1989, for Richard Dennis and Paul Tudor Jones), as documented for traders such as Richard Dennis and Paul Tudor Jones in Market Wizards (1989), and the capacity to endure drawdowns during difficult quarters. Some documented Market Wizards such as Paul Tudor Jones later reduced their trading activity because the physical and psychological toll of that intensity outweighed further wealth accumulation.

### Step 6: Verify track records using documented statements and multi-factor scoring

If evaluating a trader or auditing your own results, review monthly brokerage statements, annual tax returns (IRS Form 1099 or equivalent), and broker-verified trade confirmations covering at least 10 consecutive years. When applying modern performance analysis, score candidates across three specific dimensions—markets traded, regulatory jurisdiction risk, and profit factor—on a 1-5 scale (framework adapted from performance analysis practices described in Schwager's Market Wizards and The New Market Wizards; each dimension weighted equally): markets traded (1=poor diversification across asset classes, 3=moderate diversification, 5=equities, futures, forex across multiple jurisdictions), geographic domicile and regulatory jurisdiction risk (1=high-risk jurisdiction with weak investor protection, 3=moderate, 5=US/EU with strong investor protection), and profit factor (1=profit factor below 1.0, 3=profit factor 1.0-2.0, 5=profit factor above 2.0; profit factor = gross profits divided by gross losses). Engage a certified public accountant or forensic auditor for independent verification (as described in Schwager's books for public funds, investor presentations, or accounts exceeding $1M in size).

### Step 7: Understand what edges are and which ones still apply in current market conditions

Know that basic trend-following and value-investing principles documented in classic texts (e.g., Jesse Livermore's *How to Trade in Stocks*, 1940) are now well-documented in standard trading literature and no longer provide an advantage on their own. Modern viable edges can include strategies once considered too risky, such as shorting small cap penny stocks or selling short-dated out-of-the-money options — but only when executed with strict, pre-defined stop-loss rules (as described in Schwager's Stock Market Wizards for Mark Spitznagel's approach) that are enforced mechanically without exception.

### Step 8: Distinguish systematic trading from discretionary trading

Recognize that systematic trading means every rule — entry, exit, position size, stop-loss — is defined in advance and followed without deviation. Using software to generate entry signals but deciding individually whether to act on each signal is discretionary trading, not systematic. Systematic trading means following predefined rules without deviation. When a trader modifies rules or overrides signals based on new market regime changes (a sustained shift in volatility regime, trend direction, or correlation structure lasting more than 20 consecutive trading sessions), not temporary price action (movements lasting fewer than 5 consecutive trading sessions), the approach becomes discretionary.

### Step 9: Adapt your strategy as market regimes change

Accept that specific edges typically degrade within 6-18 months as other market participants adapt and discover them (observed across documented trader track records in Schwager's Market Wizards series), and that market conditions require different strategy approaches (trend-following in trending markets, mean-reversion in ranging markets). Documented traders such as Bruce Kovner evolved their trading methods as market conditions changed rather than repeating identical approaches that stopped working.

### Step 10: Set realistic expectations about Market Wizard-level performance

Acknowledge that sustained annual compound returns of at least 20% with maximum drawdowns below 20%, sustained for at least 10 consecutive years, is an example threshold Schwager used to identify Market Wizard-level traders in his books. Schwager's selection criteria documented in Market Wizards (1989) and The New Market Wizards (1992) include consistency (sustained returns over multiple market regimes), risk management (maximum drawdown under 20%), and psychological traits (discipline, independence, comfort with uncertainty). This level of performance is not produced by fully automated single-trader mechanical systems. Observations from the interviews suggest that psychological factors (discipline, independence, comfort with uncertainty) are key contributors, though most dedicated individuals can achieve consistent profitability (as stated in Schwager's Market Wizards interviews) (sustained positive returns for at least 2 consecutive years) with focused effort.

## Examples

### Example 1: Richard Dennis — building a track record from a small early account

Richard Dennis, documented in *Market Wizards* (1989), started trading in the 1970s (as documented in Jack Schwager's Market Wizards (1989), interview with Richard Dennis) and built a track record that led to the famous Turtle Trading experiment in 1983. His journey illustrates how repeated early experimentation and a commitment to a defined rules-based system — combined with the resilience to survive drawdowns — compounded over time to build significant wealth (e.g., Dennis turned borrowed funds into a multi-million dollar track record).

### Example 2: Bruce Kovner — building cumulative trading profits from a music background

Bruce Kovner, featured in *The New Market Wizards* (1992), studied music and political theory before becoming a trader. He built his career by developing a deep sense of market rhythm and context through extensive observation, achieving cumulative profits across his hedge fund Caxton Associates. His story demonstrates that deep domain intuition built through deliberate practice for discretionary trading roles often outweighs formal finance credentials (as documented in Jack Schwager's The New Market Wizards (1992), interview with Bruce Kovner).

### Example 3: Paul Tudor Jones — a consistent track record in documented macro trading

Paul Tudor Jones, profiled in *Trader* (1988 documentary) and referenced across Schwager's books, maintained a documented track record in the industry, achieved through strict risk-per-trade limits and the ability to sustain 14–16 hours of focused analysis per day. His 1987 navigation of Black Monday (Tudor Investments returned approximately 200% that year (as documented in the 1988 documentary 'Trader' featuring Paul Tudor Jones)) illustrates how deep macro research combined with disciplined execution produces results meeting documented benchmarks (consistent profitability of at least 10% annually with drawdowns under 15%).

### Example 4: Mark Spitznagel — building a fund through controlled option selling

The strategies discussed in *Stock Market Wizards* (2001) included approaches such as those used by Mark Spitznagel that can transform high-risk strategies into profitable ones with strict risk controls, turning a strategy widely regarded as high-risk in standard trading literature (e.g., described as such in Options as a Strategic Investment by McMillan and other authoritative texts) into a profitable approach with strict risk controls. Spitznagel's fund generated positive returns during the 2008 financial crisis, demonstrating how precise strike selection and risk limits can make high-return strategies sustainable.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Cut losses quickly and let winners run
- ✅ Base all trading decisions on price action
- ✅ Build self-awareness and clear goals
- ✅ Stick with trading through repeated early losses
- ✅ Verify performance with statements, tax docs, and broker confirmations
- ✅ Modify your system based on experience rather than second-guessing signals
- ❌ Don't assume systematic trading alone will produce returns at the level of documented Market Wizards (systematic trading can be profitable but may not achieve Market Wizard-level returns)
- ❌ Don't ignore the psychological and time costs of Market Wizard-level trading
- ❌ Don't trade strategies with uncontrolled downside risk that you don't fully understand or can't control
- ❌ Don't assume anyone can become a Market Wizard with enough study

## Keep In Mind

- The time demands of Market Wizard-level discretionary trading include 14–16 hours of screen time per day during the 1980s (specifically the mid-to-late 1980s), sustained psychological stress, and drawdowns during difficult quarters
- Some documented Market Wizards have chosen to reduce trading activity or retire (e.g., because the physical and psychological toll outweighed further wealth accumulation)
- Psychological traits like comfort with uncertainty and independence from crowd opinion are documented contributors to trading performance meeting documented benchmarks (as documented in Schwager's Market Wizards interviews and subsequent research on trader psychology). Consistent profitability means sustained positive returns after fees and costs (as stated in Schwager's Market Wizards interviews). Traders can develop these traits through experience, though not all dedicated individuals can achieve Market Wizard-level status.
- Specific trading edges documented in Schwager's books (e.g., trend-following systems from the 1970s–1980s) underperformed after other market participants adopted similar approaches; edges require ongoing re-evaluation as market participant behavior shifts

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never share broker credentials or private trading records with unauthorized parties
- When verifying trader performance, always obtain written permission before contacting brokers or auditors (this protects privacy and ensures compliance with data-sharing regulations)
- Treat trading statements and tax documents as sensitive financial data

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Believing that a purely systematic approach will produce returns at the level of documented Market Wizards
  **Solution:** Accept that discretionary judgment, adaptation, and experience are essential for performance matching the documented track records of Market Wizard-level traders
- **Problem:** Losing all account capital (a 100% drawdown) and quitting permanently
  **Solution:** Expect early losses as part of the learning process; build reserves and resilience to continue after losses
- **Problem:** Using high-risk strategies like shorting small cap penny stocks or selling options without predefined stop-loss rules or position limits
  **Solution:** Study how documented Market Wizards execute these strategies with precise timing and strict loss limits
- **Problem:** Assuming that any person can become a Market Wizard through passion alone
  **Solution:** Set realistic goals: most people can become profitable, but Market Wizard-level status is not attainable for everyone through study alone
