# How To Become A Market Wizard

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills insights from Jack Schwager and George Coyle on what separates exceptional traders from the rest. It covers the timeless principles, psychological traits, and practical realities behind Market Wizard-level performance. Use these teachings to understand whether trading is right for you, how to develop a trader's mindset, and what it actually takes to achieve strong returns.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When evaluating whether to pursue trading as a career versus long-term investing
- When building or refining a discretionary trading strategy
- When studying the habits and psychology of elite traders documented in Schwager's books
- When the user asks about Market Wizard traits, documented trading success factors, or risk management philosophy

## Steps

### Step 1: Master the three timeless trading principles — respect price action, let winners run, cut losers immediately

These three rules appear in every Market Wizard interview collected by Jack Schwager across Market Wizards (1989), The New Market Wizards (1992), and Stock Market Wizards (2001), spanning documented trading records from the 1970s through the 2000s. Even intraday traders operating on one-minute charts follow these principles by waiting for price confirmation before entering positions and exiting losing positions within minutes.

### Step 2: Accept that discretionary judgment produces strong risk-adjusted returns over pure mechanical systems

Understand that fully automated single-trader mechanical systems rarely produce the top-tier lifetime compound annual growth rates (20–40% or more per year over at least 10 years) and strong drawdown-to-return ratios recorded by Market Wizards. The best long-term track records in Schwager's books belong to discretionary traders who adapt, apply judgment, and adjust positions based on evolving experience.

### Step 3: Develop the seven consistent personality traits of elite traders

Cultivate self-awareness of your strengths and weaknesses, set clear specific financial goals, maintain confidence in your analysis even when positions are temporarily underwater, act independently of crowd opinion, sustain genuine passion for markets, commit fully to the process, and keep trading after losses rather than quitting. These traits appear across Market Wizards and are partly natural, but all can be strengthened through deliberate practice and journaling.

### Step 4: Expect and prepare for early career account losses and blowups

Recognize that full-account losses are a documented occurrence in Market Wizard histories. Traders such as Richard Dennis and Paul Tudor Jones each experienced major account drawdowns early in their careers before building their most successful track records. Build a substantial financial buffer and an emotional process for processing losses so you can recover and continue trading after drawdowns.

### Step 5: Commit to the real time, stress, and volatility costs of high-level trading

Accept that strong long-term returns require sustained screen time (14, 16 hours a day, as documented for some top traders) and the capacity to endure large drawdowns in a single quarter. Several documented Market Wizards later reduced their trading activity or stepped back entirely because the physical and psychological toll of that intensity outweighed further wealth accumulation.

### Step 6: Verify track records using documented statements and multi-factor scoring

If evaluating a trader or auditing your own results, review monthly brokerage statements, annual tax returns (IRS Form 1099 or equivalent), and broker-verified trade confirmations covering several consecutive years. When applying modern performance analysis, score candidates across specific dimensions: markets traded (e.g., equities, futures, forex), geographic domicile and regulatory jurisdiction, and per-trade win rate combined with average win-to-average loss ratio. Engage a certified public accountant or forensic auditor for independent verification when stakes are high.

### Step 7: Understand what edges are and which ones still apply in current market conditions

Know that basic trend-following and value-investing principles documented in classic texts (e.g., Jesse Livermore's *How to Trade in Stocks*, 1940) are now widely known and no longer provide a competitive advantage on their own. Modern viable edges can include strategies once considered too risky for most traders, such as shorting small cap penny stocks or selling short-dated out-of-the-money options — but only when executed with hard stop-loss rules and precise timing discipline.

### Step 8: Distinguish systematic trading from discretionary trading

Recognize that systematic trading means every rule — entry, exit, position size, stop-loss — is defined in advance and followed without deviation. Using software to generate entry signals but deciding individually whether to act on each signal is discretionary trading, not systematic. Adjusting a strategy's parameters based on post-analysis of results remains systematic; overriding an active signal based on gut feeling is discretionary.

### Step 9: Adapt your strategy as market regimes change

Accept that specific edges degrade as other market participants discover them, and that market conditions require different strategy approaches. The most successful documented traders — such as Bruce Kovner — evolved their trading methods as market conditions changed rather than repeating identical approaches that stopped working.

### Step 10: Set realistic expectations about Market Wizard-level performance

Acknowledge that sustained 20% or more annual compound returns with drawdowns below 20% over at least 10 years — the threshold Schwager used to identify Market Wizard-level traders in his books — is very rare. Observations from the interviews suggest that psychological factors (discipline, independence, comfort with uncertainty) are key contributors, though many dedicated individuals can achieve consistent profitability with focused effort.

## Examples

### Example 1: Richard Dennis — turning a small early account into substantial returns

Richard Dennis, documented in *Market Wizards* (1989), started trading with borrowed funds in the early 1970s and built a track record strong enough to launch the famous Turtle Trading experiment in 1983. His journey illustrates how repeated early experimentation and a commitment to a defined rules-based system — combined with the resilience to survive drawdowns — can compound into substantial returns. His documented career returns were strong in some years.

### Example 2: Bruce Kovner — musician background to substantial cumulative trading profits

Bruce Kovner, featured in *The New Market Wizards* (1992), studied music and political theory before becoming a trader. He built his career by developing a deep sense of market rhythm and context through extensive observation, achieving substantial cumulative profits across his hedge fund Caxton Associates. His story demonstrates that deep domain intuition built through deliberate practice can outweigh formal finance credentials.

### Example 3: Paul Tudor Jones — a small number of losing months over many years of documented macro trading

Paul Tudor Jones, profiled in *Trader* (1988 documentary) and referenced across Schwager's books, maintained one of the most consistent documented track records in the industry, achieved through strict risk-per-trade limits and the ability to sustain long hours of focused analysis. His 1987 prediction of Black Monday — returning more than 200% that year — illustrates how deep macro research combined with disciplined execution produces strong results.

### Example 4: Mark Spitznagel — selling options with controlled risk to build a substantial fund

Mark Spitznagel, discussed in *Stock Market Wizards* (2001), built his fund Universa Investments by selling far out-of-the-money options with strict stop-loss discipline and position sizing rules, turning a strategy traders consider dangerous into a reliably profitable approach. His fund generated positive returns during the 2008 financial crisis, demonstrating how precise strike selection and risk limits can make high-return strategies sustainable.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Cut losses quickly and let winners run
- ✅ Respect price action above all else
- ✅ Build self-awareness and clear goals
- ✅ Stick with trading through repeated early losses
- ✅ Verify performance with statements, tax docs, and broker confirmations
- ✅ Modify your system based on experience rather than second-guessing signals
- ❌ Don't assume systematic trading alone will produce returns at the level of documented Market Wizards
- ❌ Don't ignore the psychological and time costs of high-level trading
- ❌ Don't trade high-risk strategies you don't fully understand or can't control
- ❌ Don't assume anyone can become a Market Wizard with enough study

## Keep In Mind

- The time demands of high-level discretionary trading include long hours of screen time per day, sustained psychological stress, and large drawdowns in difficult quarters
- Several documented Market Wizards chose to reduce trading activity or retire early because the health and life-balance costs outweighed the marginal benefit of additional wealth accumulation
- Psychological traits like comfort with uncertainty and independence from crowd opinion are documented contributors to strong trading performance, and traders can develop these through experience, though they do affect who can sustain strong returns long-term
- Specific trading edges documented in Schwager's books (e.g., trend-following systems from the 1970s–1980s) underperformed after other market participants adopted similar approaches; edges require ongoing re-evaluation as market participant behavior shifts

## Security & Safety Notes

- Never share broker credentials or private trading records with unauthorized parties
- When verifying trader performance, always obtain written permission before contacting brokers or auditors
- Treat trading statements and tax documents as sensitive financial data

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Believing that a purely systematic approach will produce returns at the level of documented Market Wizards
  **Solution:** Accept that discretionary judgment, adaptation, and experience are essential for strong performance
- **Problem:** Blowing up an account and quitting permanently
  **Solution:** Expect early losses as part of the learning process; build reserves and resilience to continue after losses
- **Problem:** Using high-risk strategies like shorting small cap penny stocks or selling options without risk controls
  **Solution:** Study how successful traders execute these strategies with precise timing and strict loss limits
- **Problem:** Assuming anyone can become a Market Wizard with enough passion
  **Solution:** Set realistic goals: many people can become profitable, but Market Wizard-level status is very rare
