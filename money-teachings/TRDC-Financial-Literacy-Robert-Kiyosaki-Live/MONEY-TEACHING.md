# Financial Literacy Video with Robert Kiyosaki - Live

## Overview

This Money Teaching distills the core financial literacy principles from Robert Kiyosaki's live discussion, co-presented with Donald Trump. It explains why financial education is missing from traditional schooling and provides actionable steps to shift from an employee mindset to an investor mindset. The teaching focuses on the E-S-B-I quadrant framework, the true definitions of assets and liabilities, and practical strategies for building passive income.

## When to Follow These Money Teachings

- When you want to understand the fundamental difference between assets and liabilities
- When you are deciding between pursuing a traditional job versus building a business or investment portfolio
- When the user asks how to achieve financial independence or passive income
- When evaluating a potential investment, especially real estate
- When struggling with debt and looking for a strategic payoff approach
- When questioning conventional financial advice like "save money" or "get a safe job"

## Steps

### Step 1: Master the Six Words of Financial Literacy

Understand and internalize the five fundamental words of financial literacy: **Income, Expense, Asset, Liability, and Cash Flow**. These are the building blocks of all financial intelligence. Financial literacy is not about your FICO score or credit rating — it is about your ability to read and understand a financial statement (income statement, balance sheet, statement of cash flow). If you cannot manage cash flow, you cannot build wealth, regardless of your income level.

### Step 2: Know the E-S-B-I Quadrant

Understand the four types of people in business and identify where you currently stand:
- **E (Employee)** — Works for a paycheck, benefits, and a 401k. The school system primarily prepares you for this quadrant.
- **S (Small Business / Specialist)** — Self-employed, cannot stop working without income stopping.
- **B (Big Business Owner)** — Owns a system that generates income without their direct labor.
- **I (Investor)** — Makes money from investments, passively, whether working or not.

Your goal is to move from the left side (E and S) to the right side (B and I). The richest people in the world are B's and I's, not E's.

### Step 3: Apply the Asset and Liability Test Correctly

Apply Kiyosaki's simple definition: **An asset puts money in your pocket. A liability takes money out of your pocket.** Do not rely on accounting labels. For example, a personal residence that you live in takes money out through mortgage payments, taxes, insurance, and upkeep — it is a liability. A rental property that generates monthly income after expenses is an asset. A car that you use for personal use is a liability. A car that you rent out is an asset. Ask yourself for every purchase: "Is this going to put money in my pocket or take money out?"

### Step 4: Stop Working for Money — Make Money Work for You

The fundamental mistake most people make is working for money. The rich do not work for money — they work to acquire assets that generate cash flow. When you work for a paycheck, income stops when you stop working. When you own assets, income continues whether you work or not. Shift your goal from "get a high-paying job" to "acquire assets that generate passive income." True financial freedom is defined by Forbes as a million dollars a year in passive income, whether you work or not.

### Step 5: Use Debt Strategically, Not Recklessly

Debt is not inherently bad — it is a tool. The middle class uses bad debt (credit cards, car loans, consumer debt) that takes money out of their pocket. The rich use good debt (investment real estate, business loans) that is paid off by the income from an asset, leaving them with infinite returns. If you borrow to buy an asset that pays for itself and generates profit, you are using debt wisely. Always understand the cash flow impact before borrowing.

### Step 6: Pay Off Debt With a Strategic, Momentum-Based Approach

When paying down multiple debts, do not always start with the highest interest rate. Instead, pay off the smallest debt first to create psychological momentum. Kiyosaki and Trump both used this approach: list all debts, attack the one you can eliminate fastest, and cross it off. This "small wins" method builds confidence and keeps you motivated to continue attacking the remaining debts.

### Step 7: Invest in Your Financial Education First

Before investing in anything, invest in your own financial education. The school system will not teach you about money — it was designed to produce employees, not investors. Learn about tax law, insurance, accounting, debt, and the different asset classes (business, real estate, paper assets, commodities). The more you know, the fewer mistakes you will make with your money. Kiyosaki learned finance on the streets, not at Wharton, and that practical education was what built his wealth.

### Step 8: Choose Your Associates Carefully

People can be assets or liabilities. A bad financial advisor, a crook, or a partner who steals from you is a human liability that can drain your wealth. Surround yourself with people who are smarter than you in areas where you are weak. The people you spend time with directly influence your financial outcomes. Be selective about who you take advice from and who you partner with.

### Step 9: Build Assets That Provide for Future Obligations

Plan ahead for future financial obligations such as caring for aging parents or family medical costs. Kiyosaki notes that bond markets are failing to provide adequate income for these needs, meaning individuals must build their own asset base to cover them. Ask: "How much monthly cash flow will I need to cover these future liabilities?" and build assets that produce that income.

## Examples

### Example 1: Evaluating a Personal Residence

You are considering buying a $400,000 home with a $320,000 mortgage. Before buying, apply the asset/liability test. Monthly costs include mortgage ($2,000), property taxes ($500), insurance ($200), and maintenance ($300). Total outflow: $3,000/month. This home is a liability because it takes $3,000 out of your pocket every month. To turn it into an asset, you could rent out rooms or the entire property to generate $3,500/month in rental income. Now the property puts $500/month in your pocket. It has shifted from liability to asset.

### Example 2: Escaping the Paycheck Trap

You earn $80,000/year as an employee. After taxes, you take home about $60,000 ($5,000/month). You have $2,000/month in expenses, leaving $3,000 for savings. Instead of saving this in a 401k or savings account (where returns are low and taxes apply), you use the $3,000/month as a down payment on a small rental property. The property costs $150,000, with a $30,000 down payment. After a tenant moves in, rent is $1,800/month and the mortgage (including taxes and insurance) is $1,200/month. The property puts $600/month in your pocket. You have created an asset. As you repeat this process, your monthly passive income grows. After five properties, you have $3,000/month in passive income — equal to your job income. You have the option to quit your job.

### Example 3: Paying Off Multiple Debts

You have three credit cards: Card A ($500 at 20% APR), Card B ($3,000 at 15% APR), Card C ($8,000 at 25% APR). Financially, you should pay Card C first (highest rate). But using the momentum approach: start with Card A ($500) because you can eliminate it in one or two payments. The psychological win of crossing it off keeps you motivated to attack Card B, then Card C. The result is the same — all cards get paid — but the momentum approach is more likely to be completed.

## Best Practices

- ✅ Master the income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement before investing
- ✅ Test every purchase with: "Does this put money in my pocket or take it out?"
- ✅ Acquire assets before buying liabilities (house, car, luxury items)
- ✅ Use debt only when an asset pays for the debt and still generates profit
- ✅ Invest in financial education before investing in anything else
- ✅ Focus on building passive income as your primary wealth metric
- ✅ Choose the right quadrant — aim for B (Big Business) and I (Investor)
- ✅ Surround yourself with people who are financially smarter than you
- ❌ Do not trust the government or your employer to take care of your financial future
- ❌ Do not let your house, car, or spouse be your primary retirement plan
- ❌ Do not rely on a 401k, IRA, or pension as a secure retirement (these are liabilities)
- ❌ Do not let your emotions (fear, greed) drive investment decisions
- ❌ Do not trust a financial advisor who has never achieved what you want to achieve

## Keep In Mind

- A FICO score measures your ability to borrow money, not your ability to build wealth — Kiyosaki borrowed hundreds of millions of dollars without a high FICO score
- The school system was designed to produce employees (E quadrant), not investors (I quadrant)
- Financial IQ is measured by how big a financial problem you can solve, not by your academic degree
- "Live below your means" is bad advice — instead, find ways to expand your means through assets
- Political parties (Democrat and Republican) both serve special interests, not the middle class
- Your house is not an asset unless it puts money in your pocket — it is a liability
- The richest people own businesses first, then invest in real estate, then paper assets

## Security & Safety Notes

- Always use a qualified real estate attorney when completing property transactions, even when using discount services or online kits
- Never rely solely on verbal agreements or informal arrangements for significant financial matters
- Verify the credentials and track record of any financial advisor before following their advice
- Protect your personal financial data — never share account numbers, Social Security numbers, or banking details with untrusted parties
- Diversify across asset classes and jurisdictions; do not keep all assets in one location or currency
- Use legal structures (LLCs, corporations) to protect personal assets from business liabilities
- Be aware that government programs and promises (Social Security, pensions, FDIC insurance) may not be sufficient or sustainable for your retirement needs

## Common Pitfalls

- **Problem:** Calling your personal residence an asset when it takes money out every month
  **Solution:** Apply the cash flow test. If it doesn't put money in your pocket every month, it is a liability.

- **Problem:** Getting a high-paying job and saving money as a wealth strategy
  **Solution:** Save to invest in assets, not to accumulate cash. Cash loses value to inflation. Assets generate cash flow.

- **Problem:** Letting your 401k or IRA be your primary retirement plan
  **Solution:** These are tax-deferred vehicles that often produce insufficient income. Build a separate portfolio of income-producing assets (real estate, businesses, commodities).

- **Problem:** Paying off high-interest debt slowly because it "feels safer"
  **Solution:** Use the momentum method: eliminate the smallest debt first, then tackle the next. The psychological win keeps you motivated.

- **Problem:** Waiting until you have "enough money" before learning about investing
  **Solution:** Invest in financial education first. The knowledge compounds faster than money and prevents costly mistakes.

- **Problem:** Listening to financial advice from people who are not where you want to be
  **Solution:** Only take advice from people who have achieved the results you are seeking. A broke financial advisor cannot make you rich.

- **Problem:** Assuming the government or your employer will provide for your retirement
  **Solution:** The government has no incentive to make you rich. Build your own asset base that generates income independent of any government program.

- **Problem:** Using consumer debt (credit cards, car loans) to finance a lifestyle
  **Solution:** Consumer debt is the fastest way to stay poor. Use only good debt — debt that is paid for by an asset's income.
